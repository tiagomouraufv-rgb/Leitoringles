## Part 21 — The Grand Armada

The *Pequod* sailed east across the Indian Ocean towards Java and the islands of Asia. To reach the Pacific, we had to go through the Straits of Sunda, a narrow passage between the islands of Sumatra and Java. Many ships go through it, and it is a famous place for pirates.

As we came near the Straits, we saw an amazing sight. Ahead of us, spread across the sea in a huge half-circle, there were hundreds — no, thousands — of sperm whales, all swimming together. Their spouts rose into the air like the chimneys of a great city. It was like an army, a Grand Armada.

Sperm whales sometimes travel in huge groups like this, when they are afraid. Too many ships hunt them now, and they gather together for protection.

Ahab ordered every sail to be raised, and we chased the great herd. But just then, Tashtego shouted from the mast-head that there were other ships behind us — small, fast Malay pirate boats. They were coming after us! So now we were chasing the whales, and the pirates were chasing us. "Ahab," I thought, "is hunting the whale, and death is hunting Ahab."

But the *Pequod* was faster than the pirates, and soon they were left behind. Then the whales seemed to become confused. They stopped running and began to swim round and round in circles. The boats were lowered.

**In the Centre**

Queequeg threw his harpoon into a whale, and the whale pulled our boat at great speed right into the middle of the herd. Whales were all around us, crashing through the water. We expected to be killed at any moment. Then the harpoon came out of the whale, and we found ourselves in a strange place.

In the centre of the herd, the water was completely calm, like a quiet lake. Hundreds of whales were swimming slowly around us, peaceful and unafraid. They were the females and the young ones, the mothers and their babies, kept safe in the middle of the herd by the others swimming in circles around them.

The young whales came close to our boat and looked at us with curious eyes, like puppies. Queequeg touched their heads, and Starbuck scratched their backs with his lance, and they did not move away. Some of them seemed to sniff at us, like dogs.

And under the water, in that clear sea, we saw something even more wonderful. Deep down, the mother whales were feeding their babies with milk. We could see the babies, small and new, still tied to their mothers by a long cord, the way human babies are tied to their mothers when they are born. One of the babies looked up at us as it drank, as if we were only a piece of floating seaweed.

It was the most peaceful thing I have ever seen. And I thought: in the same way, although the storms of life are all around me, deep in the centre of my soul there is a calm place where I can rest, and where I am always happy.

**The Panic**

But our peace did not last. In the outer circles of the herd, some of the other boats had attacked whales. One whale had been wounded with a cutting-spade, which was still tied to the end of a rope. The whale swam wildly through the herd, dragging the spade behind him. The sharp spade cut and hurt the other whales as it passed them, and they became mad with fear.

Suddenly, the calm circle broke. The whales began to rush in all directions. The great herd moved towards us, and we were in the middle of a storm of whales. We rowed for our lives, pushing between the huge bodies. They almost crushed our boat. At last we escaped, and the whales swam away, faster than ever, until they disappeared over the horizon.

After all that excitement, we had caught only one whale. Many whales had been wounded, but they had escaped. Whaling is like that. There is always more danger than profit.
