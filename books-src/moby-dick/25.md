## Part 25 — Ahab's Leg

When Ahab got back to the *Pequod*, his ivory leg was badly damaged. It looked strong enough, but he did not trust it. He had a secret reason for this.

Before the *Pequod* left Nantucket, Ahab had been found one night lying on the ground, unconscious. His ivory leg had broken, and the sharp end had hurt him badly in the body, near the groin. It was a terrible wound, and it took a long time to heal. That was why he had stayed hidden in his house before the voyage, and why he did not come on deck during the first days at sea. He did not want anyone to know. The wound seemed to him like another attack by the white whale — as if all his pains were connected, one leading to the next, like a family. Every sadness has a father and a grandfather. Even the gods, he thought, must have their sorrows.

Now, after the meeting with the *Samuel Enderby*, he called the ship's carpenter and ordered him to make a new leg at once.

**The Carpenter**

The carpenter of the *Pequod* was an old man who could do everything. He could repair a boat, make a chair, pull a tooth, cut hair, and build a coffin. He worked with his hands, without thinking. He was not stupid, but he never asked questions. He did every job in the same calm way, whether he was making a toy for a sailor or a leg for the captain. He was like a pocket knife with many blades: useful for everything, but without a soul. He talked to himself as he worked, but only because talking helped his hands to work.

He set up his bench on the deck, next to the try-works. The blacksmith, an old man called Perth, started his fire to make the iron parts. The carpenter found a good piece of whalebone, the jaw of a sperm whale, and began to cut and file it into the shape of a leg. White dust flew around him.

**Ahab and the Carpenter**

Late at night, Ahab came to see how the work was going. He stood beside the carpenter and watched him for some time.

"Well, carpenter," he said. "How goes the leg?"

"Well, sir. I'm just measuring it."

"Good. You make legs, and you also make coffins, don't you? Tell me, carpenter, if you could make a whole man, how would you make him?"

The carpenter did not understand, but Ahab answered his own question.

"I would order a complete man," he said, "made to my design. First, fifty feet high. Then, a chest made like a tunnel, with arms three feet thick. No heart at all. A forehead of brass, and about a quarter of an acre of fine brains. And let me see — shall I order eyes to see outwards? No, but put a skylight on top of his head, to light up the inside. There, take the order, and away."

The carpenter thought that the captain was making a joke, and laughed politely. But Ahab was not joking.

"Carpenter," he said after a while, "I'll pay you for this leg. But tell me something. Even though my real leg is gone, I can still feel it. Here, where your new leg will be, I can feel my old leg, as if it were still there. Isn't that strange? And if I can feel a leg that is not there, carpenter, how do you know that there isn't some invisible thing standing right here, where you stand? Some spirit, some ghost, that is here although you cannot see it?"

The carpenter looked around nervously.

"Oh, Life!" said Ahab. "Here I am, proud as a Greek god, and yet I owe my leg to this foolish carpenter. I hate being dependent on anyone. I wish I could be as free as air. But I am in debt to the whole world. How long will it be before I am paid back?" He turned and went away.

The carpenter shook his head and went on working. "He's a strange one, the old man," he said to himself. "Very strange. I've heard that Stubb calls him queer. Queer, queer — that's the only word for him. Well, I'll make his leg, and then I'll make his coffin, if he wants one. A man must be ready for anything."
