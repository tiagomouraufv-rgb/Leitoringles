## Part 30 — The Needle, the Line and the Rachel

**The Needle**

The next morning, the sea was calm again, and the sun was shining. Ahab looked at the sun, and then at the compass, and his face became pale. The sun was behind the ship — but according to the compass, we were sailing towards it! Something was wrong.

"Starbuck!" he cried. "The lightning has turned our compasses round! They point the wrong way. We are sailing west, not east!"

It was true. The lightning in the storm had reversed the magnetism of the compass needles. Now north was south, and south was north.

The sailors were terrified. Without a compass, how could we find our way? But Ahab was not afraid. He took a steel lance, broke off its point, and asked for a hammer and a needle used for sewing sails. Then, in front of all the crew, he made a new compass. He hit the steel with the hammer many times, holding it in a special way, until it became magnetic. Then he put it into the old compass and showed it to the men. The new needle pointed in the right direction.

"Look for yourselves," he cried proudly, "if Ahab is not lord of the magnet! The sun is east, and the compass swears it!"

The sailors looked at him with fear and wonder. To them, Ahab was not a man, but a magician. Their fear of him became even greater.

**The Log and Line**

To measure the speed of a ship, sailors use a **log and line**: a piece of wood tied to a long rope with knots in it. They throw it into the sea and count how many knots go out in a certain time. The *Pequod* had not used her log and line for a long time, and the rope had become old and weak.

Ahab ordered it to be thrown into the sea. The old Manxman held the line. "Sir, I am afraid it is too old and rotten," he said.

"Throw it," said Ahab.

The line ran out, and then suddenly it broke. The log was lost in the sea.

"I crush the quadrant, the thunder turns the needles, and now the mad sea breaks the log-line," said Ahab. "But Ahab can fix everything. Make a new line."

Pip came running towards them, talking strangely. "Pip? Who calls Pip? Pip jumped from the whaleboat. Pip's missing. Let's see if you have fished him up here, fisherman."

"Who are you, boy?" said Ahab.

"Bell-boy, sir. Ship's crier. Ding, dong, ding! Pip! Pip! Pip! One hundred pounds of clay reward for Pip. Five feet high — looks cowardly — quickest known by that!"

Ahab looked at the boy. Something in his face changed. He took Pip's hand.

"Come with me," he said gently. "Ahab's cabin shall be Pip's home from now on, while Ahab lives. You touch my deepest heart, boy. You are tied to me by cords woven of my heart-strings. Come, let's go down."

"What's this? Here's velvet shark-skin," said Pip, looking at Ahab's hand. "Ah, now, if poor Pip had only felt so kind a thing as this, perhaps he had never been lost!"

**The Life-Buoy**

A few days later, at dawn, a sailor climbed to the mast-head to take his watch. He had only been there a few minutes when he gave a cry and fell into the sea. The men ran to the side and threw the **life-buoy** to him — a small, light barrel that floats, for a man in the water to hold. But the barrel had been in the sun too long, and it was dry and cracked. Water came into it, and it sank, and the man sank with it. He was the first man of the *Pequod* to die on the voyage, and it was a bad sign.

We needed a new life-buoy. Queequeg suggested that his coffin could be used. It was empty now, and it would float well. Starbuck did not like the idea, but there was nothing else. So the carpenter closed the coffin with wood and tar, so that no water could get in, and hung it at the back of the ship, ready to be thrown to anyone who fell into the sea.

Ahab came and watched the carpenter work. "Can it be?" he said. "Here is a coffin, the symbol of death, turned into a life-buoy, the symbol of life. Can it be that, in some spiritual sense, a coffin is, after all, only a life-preserver? I'll think about that. But no. So far gone am I in the dark side of earth, that its other side, the light side, seems only uncertain twilight to me."

**The Rachel**

The next day, we saw a large ship coming towards us. She was the *Rachel*, from Nantucket. Before she came close, her captain shouted: "Have you seen a whaleboat adrift?"

But Ahab shouted, "Have you seen the White Whale?"

"Yes, yesterday. Have you seen a whaleboat adrift?"

Ahab was so excited that he could hardly answer. The *Rachel*'s captain, a man called Gardiner, came on board. Ahab knew him; he was from Nantucket too.

"Where was he? Not killed! Not killed!" cried Ahab. "How was it?"

Captain Gardiner told his story. The day before, late in the afternoon, three of the *Rachel*'s boats had been chasing a group of whales. Then Moby Dick had appeared, and the fourth boat had gone after him. The boat had been pulled away by the white whale, very fast, until it disappeared. They had searched all night, but they had found nothing.

Then Gardiner came close to Ahab and spoke in a broken voice.

"My boy, my own boy was in that boat. He is only twelve years old. For God's sake, Captain Ahab, I beg you, let me charter your ship for forty-eight hours. I will pay you anything. Help me search for him. Sail with me, side by side, so that we can see more of the sea. You must do it. You will do it! You have a boy too, Captain Ahab — a child, at home in Nantucket. For your own boy's sake, help me!"

The men of the *Pequod* listened in silence. Stubb said quietly, "He must do it. He can't refuse."

But Ahab stood still, as cold as iron.

"Captain Gardiner," he said, "I will not do it. Even now I am losing time. Goodbye, goodbye. God bless you, man, and may I forgive myself, but I must go. Mr Starbuck, look at the clock. In three minutes all strangers must leave the ship. Then set the sails and keep her to her course."

Gardiner looked at him for a moment, unable to believe it. Then, without a word, he went back to his boat.

Soon the two ships were sailing away from each other. For a long time, we could see the *Rachel* moving backwards and forwards across the sea, searching for her lost children. She was like Rachel in the Bible, crying for her children, who were gone.
