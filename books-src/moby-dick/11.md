## Part 11 — Moby Dick

I, Ishmael, was one of that crew. My shouts went up with the rest, and I swore with them. Because of the fear in my soul, I shouted louder than the others. A wild, mysterious feeling was in me, and Ahab's terrible hatred seemed to be mine too. I listened eagerly to everything about that murderous monster, against whom I and all the others had sworn revenge.

For some time, sailors had told stories about a white whale that swam alone in the seas where sperm whales are hunted. Not all whalemen had heard of him. Only a few had seen him. And very few of those had fought him and lived to tell the story.

The stories about him were strange and frightening. Some sailors said that Moby Dick was everywhere at once — that he had been seen in different oceans at the same time. Others said that he could never die. Harpoons had been thrown into him many times, and he always swam away. Some said that, after a fight, they had seen him swimming quietly again, without a wound, as if nothing had happened.

But the most frightening thing about him was not his size or his colour. It was his intelligence. Many times, he had seemed to run away from the boats in terror. Then, suddenly, he turned and attacked them, breaking the boats into small pieces and sending the men back to their ship, or to the bottom of the sea. Some whalemen believed that he did this on purpose, with cruel, evil intelligence.

Moby Dick had a strange snow-white forehead with deep wrinkles, and a high white hump on his back. The rest of his body was spotted and streaked with white, like marble. His lower jaw was crooked, like a sickle. Old harpoons were stuck in his sides.

**How Ahab Lost His Leg**

On his last voyage, Ahab's boats had attacked Moby Dick. Three boats were broken around him, and the men and oars were thrown into the water. Then Ahab took a long knife, stood up in his broken boat, and tried to reach the whale's heart with it, like an angry man trying to kill an elephant with a small knife. At that moment, the whale suddenly swept its crooked jaw under the boat and cut off Ahab's leg, as a farmer cuts a blade of grass.

After that, Ahab had felt a wild desire for revenge. The pain in his body and the pain in his soul became one. On the long voyage home, he lay in his bed for many months, and he went mad. Sometimes he was so wild that his men had to tie him up in his hammock. His crazy shouting filled the ship.

But when the ship sailed into calmer seas, his madness seemed to go away. He came up on deck and gave his orders calmly. His officers thanked God that he was better. In reality, his madness had not gone. It had only gone deeper inside him, where nobody could see it. His great intelligence was still there, but now it served his mad purpose. He did not lose his reason; he used his reason to plan his madness.

In his mind, the white whale had become the symbol of all the evil in the world — all the things that torture men, that hurt them and confuse them, and that drive them mad. All the pain of all the ages seemed to be there, in Moby Dick. He wanted to fight all of that, and kill it, by killing the whale.

When the *Pequod* was ready to sail again, Ahab hid his mad plan from Peleg and Bildad, and from everybody else in Nantucket. If they had known, they would never have given him the ship. But he did not hide it from his crew. And we, the crew, were with him.

Why did we follow him? I cannot fully explain it. Perhaps it was his strong will. Perhaps it was the gold. Perhaps some evil power made us all mad together. I only know that the white whale swam in our minds as he swam in the sea. For Ahab, he was the enemy. For us, he was a terrible, unknown god.

**The Whiteness of the Whale**

What frightened me most about Moby Dick was something that is hard to explain: his whiteness.

White is usually a beautiful colour. It is the colour of purity and goodness. Brides wear white. Kings have often worn white robes. In many religions, white is the colour of God and the angels. So why is the whiteness of the whale so frightening?

Because whiteness also has another side. Think of the great white bear of the North Pole, or the white shark of the warm seas. Their whiteness makes them seem even more terrible. Think of the white face of a dead man, or of a ghost dressed in a white sheet. Think of the endless white snow of the mountains, or of a white sea in a fog. There is a strange, silent emptiness in whiteness.

Perhaps it is because white is not really a colour at all. It is the absence of colour — and at the same time, it is all colours mixed together. When we look at a white landscape, we feel the empty, cold universe, without meaning and without God. All the beautiful colours of nature are only like paint on the outside of things. Underneath, there is nothing but white emptiness.

And that is why the white whale was so terrible to me. In his whiteness, I saw the emptiness of the world. Is it any wonder, then, that the crew felt a deep fear when they thought of him?
