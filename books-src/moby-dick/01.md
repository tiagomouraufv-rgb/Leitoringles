## Part 1 — Going to Sea

Call me Ishmael. Some years ago — it doesn't matter exactly how many — I had little or no money in my pocket, and nothing in particular interested me on land. So I thought I would sail about a little and see the watery part of the world.

This is what I do when I feel sad and grey inside. When I find myself walking slowly past funeral shops, or following every funeral I meet in the street, or when I want to go out and knock people's hats off, then I know it is time to go to sea again. It is my way of getting rid of bad feelings. Many people feel the same way, even if they don't know it. Almost every man, at some time in his life, has the same feelings towards the ocean as I have.

Look at New York on a Sunday afternoon. Thousands of men stand along the edges of the city, looking out at the water. They work all week in offices and shops, behind desks and counters, and now they come to the sea, as close as they can get without falling in. Why? Nobody knows. But water and deep thinking go together. If you put a man who is dreaming in any place in the country, his feet will carry him to water, if there is any water in that area. Perhaps we see in the water a picture of ourselves, which we cannot catch.

When I go to sea, I never go as a passenger. Passengers must pay, and they often get seasick and argue all the time. I never go as a captain or a cook, either. I go as a simple sailor. Yes, they give me orders, and they make me jump from one end of the ship to the other. At first that hurts my pride. But who is not a slave in some way? Everybody has to obey someone. And there is one big difference between a passenger and a sailor: the sailor gets paid. Besides, the fresh wind is good for the health.

But why, after sailing many times on merchant ships, did I decide this time to go on a whaling voyage? I don't really know. Perhaps it was part of a big plan made by the Fates long ago. Perhaps it was the great whale himself. I had always wanted to see this huge and mysterious monster. I wanted to see the wild seas where he rolls his island-sized body, and the far-away places where he lives. I love to sail on forbidden seas and land on wild coasts. So the great gates of the wonder-world swung open, and in my imagination I saw endless lines of whales swimming into my soul, two by two, and in the middle of them one grand hooded ghost, like a snow hill in the air.

**The Carpet-Bag**

I put a shirt or two into my old carpet-bag, put it under my arm, and started for Cape Horn and the Pacific. I left the old city of Manhattan and arrived in New Bedford on a Saturday night in December. I was disappointed to learn that the little boat to Nantucket had already left, and that there would be no way to get there until Monday.

Most young men who want to go whaling stop at New Bedford and sail from there. But I had decided to sail only from Nantucket, because it is the oldest and most famous whaling town in America. There was something about the place that pleased me. So I had to find somewhere to sleep for two nights in New Bedford.

It was a very dark and cold night. I had only a little money. I walked through the empty streets, looking for a cheap inn. The first two places I found looked too expensive and too happy. I could hear people laughing and singing inside. "No," I said to myself, "these are too good for me." I walked on towards the water, where the buildings looked poorer.

At one point I stumbled over a box of ashes in a doorway, and I almost fell into a building where there was a loud meeting going on. Inside, a black preacher was speaking to a black congregation about the blackness of darkness, and the weeping and wailing there. I quickly left.

At last I saw a weak light near the docks. Over the door there was an old sign with a picture of a tall, straight jet of spray, and these words: *The Spouter Inn: Peter Coffin*.

"Coffin?" I thought. "Spouter? That's not very cheerful. But Coffin is a common name in Nantucket, I believe, and the place looks cheap enough." The house was old and leaning, as if it were sick. The wind howled around it. It looked like the right sort of place for me.

**The Spouter Inn**

When I went in, I found myself in a wide, low entrance hall with old wooden walls, like those of an ancient ship. On one wall hung a very large, very dark oil painting. It was so covered with smoke and dirt that at first I could not understand what it showed. I looked at it for a long time from different sides. In the middle there was a long, black shape, floating in a grey storm. After a while I decided that it was a ship in a terrible storm, half sunk, with only its three masts showing, and a great whale jumping over it, about to land on the ship and break it in pieces.

On the opposite wall there were many strange weapons: clubs and spears from the South Sea islands, with teeth and hair on them. There were also old, rusty whaling lances and harpoons, bent and broken. Some of them had killed many whales, the landlord told me later.

At the end of the hall there was a bar, made in the shape of a whale's huge jaw. Behind it a small, old man, the landlord, was selling drinks to sailors at very high prices.

I asked him for a room. He looked at me and said that the house was full — not a single bed was free. "But wait," he added, scratching his head. "You don't mind sharing a bed with a harpooner, do you? I suppose you're going whaling, so you'd better get used to that sort of thing."

I told him that I did not like the idea of sleeping with another man. If I had to share a bed, it depended on who the harpooner was. If there really was no other place, and the harpooner was not too bad, then perhaps I would accept.

"I thought so," said the landlord. "Now sit down. Supper will be ready soon."

At supper, I ate with a group of sailors. They were quiet and almost shy, although they had just returned from a long voyage. I watched them, but there was no harpooner among them. When I asked the landlord where my bedfellow was, he only smiled in a strange way.

"Oh, he's out," he said. "He's usually back early, but tonight he's late. He's out selling his head."

"Selling his head?" I said. "What do you mean? Are you telling me that this harpooner is walking around the town on a Saturday night, trying to sell his head?"

"That's exactly what he's doing," said the landlord. "I told him he couldn't sell it here, because the market's full of them."

"Full of what?"

"Heads, of course. Aren't there too many heads in the world?"

I became angry. "Landlord," I said, "stop telling me stories. I'm not a fool."

"Calm down," he said. "This harpooner has just come from the South Seas. He bought a lot of dried and preserved heads there, from the islanders. They're very interesting things, and people like to buy them as curiosities. He has sold all of them except one, and he's trying to sell that one tonight. But tomorrow is Sunday, and it wouldn't be right to sell human heads in the street when people are going to church."

This was a strange story, but it explained everything. The landlord took a candle and led me upstairs to a cold, small room with a huge bed. "There," he said. "That's the bed. It's big enough for two. Good night." And he left me.

I looked around the room. There was a strange bag on the floor, and a mat made of grass with a hole in the middle, like the ones worn by South American people. I put it on and looked at myself in the little mirror. It was heavy and smelled wet, so I quickly took it off. Then I got into bed and lay awake for a long time, thinking about the mysterious harpooner who sold heads.

At last, just as I was falling asleep, I heard heavy footsteps in the passage, and a light came under the door.
