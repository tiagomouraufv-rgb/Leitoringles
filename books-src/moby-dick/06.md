## Part 6 — The Officers and the Harpooners

At the wheel, as we sailed away from land, I saw a tall man called Bulkington. I had first noticed him in New Bedford, at the Spouter Inn. He had just come home from a voyage of four years — and now, after only a few days on land, he was going to sea again. The land seemed to burn his feet.

I think of him as a ship in a storm that must stay far from the coast. For a ship in danger, the land is the worst enemy. The safe harbour is where the rocks are. The only safety is in the open sea. In the same way, some men cannot live in comfort and safety. They need the endless, dangerous freedom of the ocean, where the deepest truths are found. Bulkington was one of them. He does not appear again in this story, so I write about him here, like a short gravestone for a brave man.

**In Defence of Whaling**

Many people on land think that whaling is a dirty, low business, and that whalemen are no better than butchers. I want to say a few words against this idea.

Yes, our work is bloody. But soldiers kill too, and the world honours them. Whaling is dangerous: every whaleman risks his life many times on every voyage. And the world owes us a lot. For many years, almost every lamp and candle in the world burned whale oil. Our ships explored the unknown seas long before the navies of the world did. Whalemen found new islands and new coasts. They opened the Pacific Ocean to trade.

Whaling is also an old and noble business. In some countries, the kings used to receive the heads of whales as gifts. And if anything good is ever done by me — if I ever deserve any honour at all — then I will owe it to whaling, because a whaling ship was my college.

**The Three Mates**

The first mate of the *Pequod* was Starbuck. He was from Nantucket, and his family were Quakers. He was a tall, thin man, about thirty years old, with dry skin like old bread that has been kept a long time. He looked healthy and strong, made for long voyages and hard weather. He was a serious man, careful and religious. He did not like to take unnecessary risks.

"I will have no man in my boat," said Starbuck, "who is not afraid of a whale." He meant that the most useful courage is the kind that comes from understanding the danger, not from foolishness. A man who is not afraid of a whale is a fool, and fools get killed. Starbuck was not in the ocean to kill whales for fun. He was there to make a living for his wife and child back in Nantucket. His father and his brother had both been killed by whales. He was brave, but his courage was the courage of a calm, sensible man. Later we will see that it was not strong enough against a madman.

The second mate was Stubb. He was from Cape Cod. He was always happy and relaxed, whatever happened. When a whale was near and the boats were lowered, he went into danger as calmly as a workman goes to work. He hummed songs while he fought the whale. He made jokes when death was close. And he always had a short black pipe in his mouth. He took it out only to sleep, and even then he put it next to his bed, so he could light it the moment he woke up. Perhaps the tobacco smoke kept away all the sad thoughts that most men have.

The third mate was Flask, from Tisbury, on Martha's Vineyard. He was a short, round, red-faced young man. He was very aggressive towards whales. He seemed to think that the great whales were his personal enemies, and that it was his duty to destroy them all. He felt no wonder or fear at their huge size. To him, a whale was just a kind of big mouse, or at most a water-rat. Because he was so short and strong, and because he could not be knocked down, the sailors called him "King-Post", after a short, thick piece of wood used on ships.

These three mates were the heads of three of the whaleboats. When whales were seen, each mate commanded one boat, like a captain commands a ship. And each mate had a harpooner, who was like his squire, as in the days of knights.

**The Harpooners**

Queequeg was chosen by Starbuck as his harpooner.

Stubb's harpooner was Tashtego, a Native American from Gay Head, on Martha's Vineyard. His people had been great hunters in the forests of New England. Tashtego had long, black, shining hair, high cheekbones and black, round eyes. Now, instead of hunting deer in the forest, he hunted whales in the sea.

Flask's harpooner was Daggoo, an enormous black man from Africa. He was six feet five inches tall — a giant. He wore two large gold rings in his ears. When he was young, he had jumped onto a whaling ship that stopped on the coast of his country, and he had been a whaleman ever since. Because Flask was so short and Daggoo was so tall, it was very funny to see them together. When Flask wanted to see far over the waves, he climbed onto Daggoo's shoulders, and Daggoo stood still and proud, like a statue, while the little man looked out from the top.

**The Crew**

The other men on the *Pequod* came from every country in the world. As on most American whaling ships, the officers were Americans, but most of the simple sailors were not. There were men from the Azores, from Ireland, from England, from Denmark, from the islands of the Pacific, from China, from India. They were all "isolatoes" — lonely men, each on his own island, without a country. But now, on the *Pequod*, they were all together, like a crowd of men from every nation who had come to the end of the world to stand before a judge.

And among them was little Pip, a young black boy from Alabama, who beat a small drum and danced for the crew. Poor Pip! You will hear more about him later. He was the most unimportant person on the ship, and his story is one of the saddest.
