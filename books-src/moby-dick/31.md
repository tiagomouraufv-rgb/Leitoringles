## Part 31 — The Symphony

**Pip and Ahab**

Ahab was going up on deck. Pip took his hand and wanted to follow him.

"No, boy, you must not follow me now," said Ahab. "The hour is coming when Ahab would not frighten you away, and yet would not have you near him. There is something in you, poor boy, that seems to cure my illness. And I do not want to be cured now. Like cures like, and for this hunt, my sickness is my most desired health. Stay here below, where they shall serve you as if you were the captain. Sit here in my own chair."

"No, no, no!" cried Pip. "You have no whole body, sir. Use poor me for your missing leg. Only tread on me, sir. I ask no more, so I remain a part of you."

"Oh, boy!" said Ahab. "You make me weak. You are the only one who can soften me. Weep so, and I will murder you! Stay here. Goodbye."

When he had gone, Pip sat in the captain's chair. "Here he goes," he said. "Pip will wait here, in the captain's chair. If anyone asks for Pip, tell them that he is here."

**The Hat**

Now that Moby Dick was near, Ahab wanted to be the first to see him. He did not trust anyone else. He had a basket made out of rope, and he had himself pulled up in it to the top of the main mast. And he gave the end of the rope, the one that held him up, to Starbuck. Of all the men on board, he chose to put his life in the hands of the one man who had opposed him.

He sat in his basket at the top of the mast for many hours, looking at the sea in all directions. Then a black sea-hawk came down from the sky and flew round and round his head, screaming. Suddenly it took Ahab's hat in its claws and flew away with it, far out over the sea, until it was only a small black spot. Then it dropped the hat into the water.

An old story says that when an eagle took the hat off a man's head and then put it back, it meant that he would become king. But the sea-hawk did not bring back Ahab's hat.

**The Delight**

The next ship we met was named, ironically, the *Delight*. She was a sad sight. One of her whaleboats lay on her deck, completely smashed — only its broken white ribs were left, like the bones of a horse.

"Have you seen the White Whale?" Ahab shouted.

"Look!" answered the captain, pointing to the broken boat.

"Have you killed him?"

"The harpoon is not yet made that will ever do that," said the other captain sadly.

"Not made?" Ahab took his own harpoon, the one hardened in blood, and held it up. "Look here, Nantucketer! Here in this hand I hold his death! Hardened in blood, and tempered by lightning are these barbs. And I swear to temper them three times in that hot place behind the fin, where the white whale most feels his cursed life!"

"Then God keep you, old man. See that?" The captain of the *Delight* pointed to a body wrapped in a hammock, lying on the deck. "I bury only one of five strong men, who were alive only yesterday. But they were dead before night. Only that one I bury. The rest were buried before they died. You sail upon their tomb."

Then the crew of the *Delight* threw the body into the sea. As it fell into the water, some of the drops splashed onto the *Pequod*'s side like a cold baptism.

As we sailed away, the men of the *Delight* saw the coffin hanging at the back of the *Pequod* — the coffin that was our life-buoy.

"Ha! Look there, men!" a voice shouted from the *Delight*. "In vain, oh strangers, you run from our sad funeral. You only turn your back to us to show us your coffin!"

**The Symphony**

It was a clear, blue day. The sky and the sea were so blue that you could hardly see where one ended and the other began. The air was soft and gentle, like a woman's touch. The sea moved gently, like the strong chest of a sleeping man.

Ahab stood on the deck, looking over the side, watching his shadow in the water. The beautiful day seemed to touch even his hard heart. From under his hat, a single tear fell into the sea. The whole Pacific Ocean did not contain anything as precious as that one little drop.

Starbuck saw him and came quietly to stand beside him.

"Starbuck," said Ahab. "Oh, Starbuck! It is a soft wind, and a soft sky. On a day just like this one, I killed my first whale. I was a boy-harpooner of eighteen! That was forty years ago. Forty years of whaling! Forty years of danger and storms! In those forty years, I have not spent three years on land. When I think of the life I have lived — so lonely, so far from everyone, a captain that nobody can speak to — it is like the life of a slave. And then I married a young girl, when I was over fifty. The next day I sailed for Cape Horn. She has only one mark of my head on her pillow. A wife? No — a widow, with her husband still alive!

"Yes, I have made that poor girl a widow, Starbuck. And all the madness, the anger, the boiling blood, as I hunted the whale — more like a demon than a man! What a fool old Ahab has been! Why all this struggle? Is Ahab richer or better now? Look at me. Am I not very, very old, Starbuck? I feel weak and bent, as if I were Adam, carrying all the centuries since Paradise on my back.

"God! God! Break my heart! Come close to me, Starbuck. Let me look into a human eye. It is better than looking at the sea or the sky, better than looking at God. In your eye I see the green land and the warm home. I see my wife and my child. No, no — stay on board when I go after Moby Dick. That danger will not be yours. Not with that far-away home in your eye!"

"Oh, my captain! My captain!" said Starbuck. "Noble soul! Great old heart, after all! Why should anyone chase that hated fish? Come away with me! Let us leave these deadly waters! Let us go home! Starbuck has a wife and child too, just as you have, sir. Let me change our course now! How happily we would sail home to see old Nantucket again! I think they have blue days like this in Nantucket too."

"They have, they have. I have seen them," said Ahab softly. "On summer mornings. At this time of day — yes, the boy is waking up from his afternoon sleep now. He sits up in bed, and his mother tells him about me, his cannibal old father. She tells him that I am far away on the sea, but that I will come back one day and dance with him again."

"It is my Mary, my Mary!" said Starbuck. "She promised that every morning she would carry my boy up the hill, to see the first sign of his father's ship! Yes, yes! No more! It is decided! We sail for Nantucket! Come, my captain, let us plan our course and go! Look, look! The boy's face at the window! The boy's hand on the hill!"

But Ahab turned his eyes away. He shook, like a dead tree that drops its last apple to the ground.

"What is it?" he said. "What strange, hidden, unearthly power is it that commands me? Against all my natural love and longing, I keep pushing myself forward, ready to do what in my own heart I would not dare. Is Ahab really Ahab? Is it I, or God, or who, that lifts this arm? If the sun does not move by itself, but is sent across the sky like a servant, how can my small heart beat, or my small brain think, by itself? It is God who does it, not I. We are turned round and round in this world, like that windlass there, and Fate is the handle."

Starbuck had turned away, pale as death, and walked away in despair.

Ahab crossed the deck to look over the other side. But there, reflected in the water, he saw two eyes staring up at him. Fedallah was leaning on the same rail, looking down into the sea.
