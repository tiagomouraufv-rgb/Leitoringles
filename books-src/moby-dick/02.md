## Part 2 — Queequeg

The door opened, and a man came in with a candle in one hand and a dried human head in the other. He did not look towards the bed. He put the candle on the floor in a corner and began to untie the strings of the big bag.

I wanted to see his face, but for some time he kept it turned away. When at last he turned round, I almost cried out. What a face! It was dark purple and yellow, and it was covered with black squares, like a chess board. "He must have been in a fight," I thought, "and these are terrible bruises." But then the candle showed me that they were not bruises at all. They were **tattoos**.

He took off his hat, and I saw that his head was completely bald, except for a small knot of hair on the top of his forehead. His bald purple head looked like a mouldy skull. If he had not been standing between me and the door, I would have run out faster than I ever ate a dinner.

Then he took off his coat and shirt, and I saw that his whole body was covered with the same squares. His back, his chest, his arms, even his legs — they were all tattooed. He was obviously some terrible savage from the South Seas, who had somehow got onto a whaling ship and arrived in this Christian country.

I was frightened, more frightened of him than I have ever been of the devil. And I could not decide what to do. Should I speak to him, or pretend to sleep?

Then he did a very strange thing. He took from his bag a little black wooden figure, a small idol with a hump on its back, exactly the colour of a three-day-old baby. He put it in the fireplace, which was empty, and lit some small pieces of wood in front of it. Then he put some biscuit on the fire and moved his hands quickly near the flames. He seemed to be offering the biscuit to his little god, and singing a kind of prayer song. All the time his face moved strangely, as if he was in pain.

When he had finished, he put the idol back in his bag, as carelessly as if it were a dead bird. Then he took his **tomahawk** — a kind of axe — and put it in his mouth. It was a pipe and an axe at the same time! He lit the pipe, blew out a big cloud of smoke, and then, with the axe still in his mouth, he jumped into bed beside me.

I screamed. He gave a cry of surprise and began to feel me with his hands.

"Who-ee you?" he shouted. "You no speak-ee, damn-me, I kill-ee." And he waved the burning tomahawk over my head in the dark.

"Landlord! Help! Coffin! Angels! Save me!" I shouted.

"Speak-ee! Tell-ee me who-ee you, or damn-me, I kill-ee!" he growled again, and the hot ashes from his pipe fell all around me.

Luckily, the landlord came in with a light, and I jumped out of bed and ran to him.

"Don't be afraid," said the landlord, smiling. "Queequeg here wouldn't hurt a hair of your head."

"Stop smiling!" I shouted. "Why didn't you tell me that this harpooner was a cannibal?"

"I thought you knew. Didn't I tell you he was selling heads in the town? But go to bed again. Queequeg, you know me, and I know you. This man sleeps with you. You understand?"

"Me understand plenty," said Queequeg, and he sat up in bed, smoking his pipe.

"You get in," he added, pointing to me with his tomahawk, and moving to one side of the bed. He did it in a very polite, kind way. I stood looking at him for a moment. For all his tattooing, he was a clean, nice-looking cannibal. "Why have I been making such a noise?" I thought. "The man is a human being just as I am. He has as much reason to be afraid of me as I have to be afraid of him. Better to sleep with a sober cannibal than a drunken Christian."

"Landlord," I said, "tell him to put away his tomahawk, or pipe, or whatever it is. Tell him to stop smoking. I don't like a man smoking in bed with me. It's dangerous."

Queequeg understood at once, and he put out his pipe. Then he politely signed to me to get into bed. He moved right to the side, as if to say: "I won't touch even a leg of yours." I got into bed, and I never slept better in my life.

**Morning**

When I woke up the next morning, Queequeg's arm was lying over me in the most loving and affectionate manner. You would have thought I was his wife. The arm was tattooed all over with an endless pattern, and the blanket we were lying under was made of many small squares of different colours. In the morning light I could hardly tell which was his arm and which was the blanket.

I tried to move his arm away, but even in his sleep he held me tight, as though only death would part us. I called to him, but he only snored. At last, after a long time, I managed to wake him. He sat up, rubbed his eyes, and looked at me as if he did not remember how I came to be there. Then he seemed to remember, and he jumped out of bed. He made signs to tell me that he would dress first and then leave me alone in the room to dress. It was very polite. These savages have a natural sense of good manners.

He dressed in a strange way. First he put on his tall hat. Then he crawled under the bed with his boots, and I heard him struggling and kicking there. At last he came out with the boots on. Then he washed his chest and face, and he shaved — with his harpoon! He took the long wooden pole off the iron head, sharpened the iron on his boot, and shaved his cheeks with it in front of the mirror.

**Breakfast and a Walk**

At breakfast, the room was full of whalemen. I had expected them to tell stories, but they were very quiet, almost shy, like sheep. Queequeg sat at the head of the table, as cool as ice. He did not use a knife or a fork. He reached across the table with his harpoon and pulled the steaks towards him with it. Nobody seemed surprised.

After breakfast I went for a walk around New Bedford. The streets were full of strange people: sailors from all over the world, cannibals, farmers who had left their fields to go whaling. The town was rich. The houses were big and beautiful, and they had all been built with money from whale oil. The wives and daughters were dressed in fine clothes. Everything there, they say, came out of the sea.

**The Chapel**

It was Sunday, so I went to the Whaleman's Chapel. Outside, the weather was terrible — snow and sleet. Inside, there were only a few people, mostly sailors and sailors' wives and widows. They sat apart from each other, silent, each with their own sorrow.

On the walls there were marble tablets with black borders. Each one remembered a man who had died at sea. One said: *Sacred to the memory of John Talbot, who, at the age of eighteen, was lost overboard, near the Isle of Desolation, off Patagonia.* Another remembered the crew of a whaleboat who had been towed out of sight by a whale and never seen again. A third remembered a captain who had been killed by a sperm whale on the coast of Japan.

I sat and read them, and I thought: "Yes, there is death in this business of whaling. A man can disappear in a moment, without a word. But so what? Perhaps I have the wrong idea about life and death. My body is only the outside of me. Anyone can take my body, but nobody can touch my soul." I was surprised to see Queequeg there among the people.

**The Sermon**

Then the preacher came in. His name was Father Mapple. He was a very old man, but strong, and he had been a sailor and a harpooner in his youth. The pulpit was very high, and it had no steps. Instead there was a rope ladder, like the ones on the side of a ship. Father Mapple climbed it hand over hand, like a sailor going up the mast. When he reached the top, he pulled the ladder up after him. Now he was alone in his pulpit, like a captain on his ship, far from the world.

He gave a sermon about Jonah and the whale. He told the story very simply, as one sailor to another. God told Jonah to go to the city of Nineveh and preach against the sins of the people. But Jonah did not want to obey. He tried to run away from God. He went to the port of Joppa and paid for a passage on a ship going to Tarshish, at the other end of the world.

But you cannot run away from God. A great storm came, and the ship was in danger of sinking. The sailors were afraid. They threw lots to find out who was guilty, and the lot fell on Jonah. Jonah told them: "Throw me into the sea, and the storm will stop." They did, and the storm stopped at once. Then a great whale swallowed Jonah, and he was inside it for three days and three nights. There, in the darkness, Jonah prayed to God. He did not ask to be saved; he accepted his punishment. And God made the whale throw him up onto dry land. Then Jonah went to Nineveh and preached the truth, as God had told him.

"Shipmates," said Father Mapple at the end, "this is the lesson. To obey God, we must disobey ourselves. Woe to him who tries to please men rather than to tell them the truth! But joy to him who stands up for God against the proud gods of this earth, and who remains true to God, even if the world is against him."

**A Friend**

When I returned to the inn, Queequeg was sitting alone by the fire. He was holding his little idol, and he was carefully counting the pages of a big book. He counted fifty pages, then stopped and gave a long whistle of surprise. Then he counted another fifty, and whistled again. It seemed he could only count to fifty, and each time he was amazed at the number of pages.

I sat and watched him. Although he was a savage with a terrible face, there was something noble in him. He had a calm, simple dignity. You cannot hide a soul, I thought. Through all his tattoos, I could see an honest heart. In his large, deep eyes there were signs of a spirit that would dare a thousand devils.

I moved my chair closer to him and tried to talk. At first he did not answer, but after a while we were speaking quite easily. Then we smoked his tomahawk pipe together, passing it from one to the other. When we had finished, he pressed his forehead against mine, put his arm around me, and said that from now on we were married. In his country, that meant that we were best friends, and that he would die for me if necessary.

After supper, he gave me the head that he had not sold, and he divided his money — about thirty dollars — into two equal parts. He pushed one part towards me and said, "Yours." I tried to refuse, but he put it in my pocket.

Then he wanted to say his evening prayers to his idol, Yojo. He invited me to join him. I am a good Christian, born and raised in the Presbyterian Church. How could I worship his little piece of wood? But then I thought: "What is worship? To do the will of God. And what is the will of God? To do to my fellow man what I would have my fellow man do to me. Queequeg is my fellow man. I would like him to join me in my worship. So I must join him in his." So I helped him light the little fire, offered biscuit to Yojo, and bowed down to the idol two or three times. Then we went to bed, at peace with our own consciences and with all the world.

**Queequeg's Story**

In bed, we talked for a long time. Queequeg told me about his life. He was born on an island called Kokovoko, far away in the west and south. It is not on any map; true places never are. His father was a king, and his uncle a high priest. But Queequeg was not satisfied with his island. He wanted to see the Christian world and learn its ways, so that he could make his own people happier and better.

When a whaling ship visited his island, he asked to join it. The captain refused. So Queequeg took his canoe and paddled out to a narrow passage the ship had to pass through. When the ship came, he jumped onto it, grabbed a chain and held on. The captain threatened to throw him into the sea, but Queequeg did not let go. He said they could cut off his hands, but he would not leave the ship. At last the captain, admiring his courage, let him stay.

But in the Christian world, Queequeg soon learned that Christians could be even worse than his own people. He saw sailors in the ports who were drunk and wicked. "It's a wicked world in all places," he thought. "I'll die a pagan." Now he was ashamed to go home, because he felt that the Christian ways had made him unfit to become king of his people. "But I will go home one day," he told me, "when I feel baptized again."

I told him that I planned to go to Nantucket and find a ship for a whaling voyage. He said that he would come with me. We would sail on the same ship, eat at the same table, and share the same dangers. I was very happy, because Queequeg was an experienced harpooner, and I knew almost nothing about whaling.
