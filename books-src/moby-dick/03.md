## Part 3 — To Nantucket

On Monday morning, we put our bags in a wheelbarrow and pushed it down to the harbour. People in the street stared at us — not so much at Queequeg, because they were used to seeing cannibals there, but at the two of us together, walking like old friends.

Queequeg told me a funny story about the first time he saw a wheelbarrow. In New Bedford, someone lent him one to carry his bag. He did not know how to use it. So he put the bag in the wheelbarrow, tied it tight, then lifted the whole wheelbarrow onto his shoulders and carried it up the street. Everyone laughed at him.

"But the white men," Queequeg said, "they do stupid things too." He told me about a white sea captain who came to a great feast on his island. In front of everyone, the captain washed his hands in the sacred bowl of coconut milk, thinking it was a bowl of water for washing. "What you think now?" said Queequeg. "Didn't our people laugh?"

**The Little Ship**

We got on a small sailing ship called the *Moss*, which carried passengers to Nantucket. As soon as we were out of the harbour, the ship began to dance on the waves, and I felt wonderfully happy. The wind was fresh, and the sea was blue.

But some of the young men on board were laughing at Queequeg. One of them, a country boy, stood behind him and made faces, imitating him. Queequeg turned round and saw him. He put down his harpoon, took the boy in his strong arms, and threw him high into the air. The boy turned over once and landed on his feet, very surprised but not hurt.

The boy ran to the captain. "Captain! Captain! Here's the devil!"

The captain came to Queequeg, very angry. "What do you mean by that? Don't you know you might have killed that boy?"

"What him say?" Queequeg asked me.

"He says you might have killed that man there," I said.

"Kill-ee?" said Queequeg, and his face showed a strange, scornful look. "Ah! Him small-ee fish. Queequeg no kill-ee so small-ee fish. Queequeg kill-ee big whale!"

"Listen," said the captain. "I'll kill-ee you, you cannibal, if you play any more of your tricks on board here. So mind yourself."

But just then, something terrible happened. The strong wind had broken the rope that held the great boom — the heavy wooden pole at the bottom of the mainsail. The boom was swinging from side to side across the back of the ship, knocking everything over. Suddenly it hit the country boy who had mocked Queequeg, and threw him into the sea.

Everyone was frightened. Nobody knew what to do. But Queequeg acted at once. He fell on his knees, crawled under the swinging boom, caught the end of a rope, and tied it fast to the ship. When the boom stopped swinging, he took off his shirt and jumped into the cold sea.

For three minutes or more, he swam like a dog, looking for the boy, throwing his long arms forward. The boy had disappeared below the water. At last Queequeg dived down, and after a moment he came up again, with one arm still swimming and the other holding a lifeless body. They pulled them on board. The poor boy came back to life, and all the passengers said that Queequeg was a hero. The captain apologised to him.

But Queequeg did not seem to think he had done anything special. He only asked for some fresh water to wash off the salt, then put on dry clothes, lit his pipe, and leaned against the side of the ship, looking calmly at the people around him. He seemed to be thinking: "It's a mutual world, all over. We cannibals must help these Christians."

**Nantucket**

Nantucket is a small island far out in the Atlantic. Look at it on a map. It is nothing but sand — no trees, no grass, just a hill of sand. People joke that the people of Nantucket have to bring wood from the mainland to make fires, and that they plant mushrooms between the stones because nothing else will grow.

But this little island is the centre of the whaling world. The people of Nantucket began by catching crabs and fish on the beach. Then they went out in small boats to catch whales near the coast. Then they built bigger ships, and went further and further, until they had sailed over all the oceans of the world. Now the sea belongs to them, as the land belongs to the emperors. The Nantucketer lives on the sea, as the prairie dog lives on the prairie. He hides among the waves; he climbs them as hunters climb the Alps. For years he sees no land. When he comes home at last, the land smells strange to him, as the moon would smell to a man from the earth. He sleeps with the noise of the waves in his ears, and dreams of whales.

**The Try Pots**

It was quite late in the evening when we arrived at Nantucket. Before we left New Bedford, Peter Coffin had told me about an inn there, run by his cousin Hosea Hussey. "There's no better place in the world for chowder," he said. So we looked for it.

After some time, we found it. At the door, there were two big black pots hanging from the branches of an old mast, like the ones used on whaling ships to boil the fat into oil. That is why the inn was called the Try Pots. The pots looked a bit like gallows, which did not seem a very good sign. But we went in.

Mrs Hussey, a woman with a loud voice and a red face, met us at the door. Her husband was away. "Clam or cod?" she asked us immediately.

"What's that about cods, ma'am?" I asked politely.

"Clam or cod?" she repeated.

"A clam for supper? A cold clam? Is that what you mean, Mrs Hussey?" I said. "Isn't that a rather cold and wet welcome in the winter?"

But she was in a hurry, and she only shouted "Clam for two!" towards the kitchen and disappeared.

Queequeg and I sat down to wait. Soon a delicious smell came from the kitchen, and two steaming bowls of **chowder** arrived. Oh, my friends! It was made of small, juicy clams, mixed with crushed biscuit and salted pork, with plenty of butter, salt and pepper. We were very hungry after our journey, and we ate it quickly. Then I had an idea. I went to the kitchen door and said, "Cod," and waited. A few minutes later we had two bowls of cod chowder, just as good as the first.

Everything at the Try Pots was chowder. Chowder for breakfast, chowder for dinner, chowder for supper, until you began to look for fish bones in your clothes. The milk tasted of fish, and even the cows, they said, were fed on fish heads.

When we went up to our room, Mrs Hussey stopped us and took Queequeg's harpoon away. "No harpoons in the rooms," she said. "Ever since a young man was found dead in Number Three, with a harpoon in his side. Now go to bed, both of you."

The next morning, Queequeg told me something important. He had talked to Yojo, his little god, about which whaling ship we should join. Yojo had said that I should choose the ship myself — alone. Yojo had already decided which ship it would be, Queequeg said, and if I let myself be guided, I would find it by accident.

So, while Queequeg stayed in our room for a day of fasting and prayer, I went down to the harbour alone to look for our ship.
