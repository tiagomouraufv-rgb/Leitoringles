## Part 9 — The Gold Coin

One morning, soon after breakfast, Ahab came up on deck as usual. He walked up and down for a long time. His ivory leg made deep marks in the wood, and his face looked very serious. You could see that he had a fixed idea in his head, and that it was turning round and round inside him, like a wheel.

"Look at him," whispered Stubb to Flask. "The chick that's in him is pecking at the shell. It'll soon be out."

The hours passed. Then, near the end of the day, Ahab suddenly stopped beside the main mast and gave an order. "Mr Starbuck! Send everybody aft!"

Starbuck was surprised, because this order is only given on very important occasions. But he obeyed. Soon the whole crew was standing together at the back of the ship. Even the men at the mast-heads came down. They all looked at Ahab, curious and a little afraid. He looked at them for a moment. Then he shouted:

"What do you do when you see a whale, men?"

"Sing out for him!" they answered with one voice.

"Good!" cried Ahab, in a wild, happy voice. "And what do you do next, men?"

"Lower the boats, and go after him!"

"And what tune is it you pull to, men?"

"A dead whale or a broken boat!"

With every answer, Ahab's face grew more strange and full of joy. The sailors looked at each other, surprised that they were so excited by such simple questions.

Then Ahab held up a large gold coin in the sunlight. It was a Spanish doubloon, worth sixteen dollars.

"Look at this, men! Do you see this Spanish gold? It is worth sixteen dollars. Mr Starbuck, give me a hammer."

He took the hammer, and with the coin in his other hand, he walked to the main mast. "Whoever of you," he shouted, "raises me a white-headed whale with a wrinkled forehead and a crooked jaw — whoever raises me that white-headed whale, with three holes in his right tail — whoever of you raises me that same white whale, he shall have this gold coin, my boys!"

And he nailed the coin to the mast.

"Hooray! Hooray!" cried the sailors, waving their hats.

"It's a white whale, I say," continued Ahab. "A white whale. Keep your eyes open for white water. If you see even a bubble, sing out!"

All this time, Tashtego, Daggoo and Queequeg had been listening with great interest. When Ahab spoke about the wrinkled forehead and the crooked jaw, they started, as if each of them had a memory of it.

"Captain Ahab," said Tashtego, "that white whale must be the same one that some call Moby Dick."

"Moby Dick?" shouted Ahab. "Do you know the white whale, then, Tash?"

"Does he fan his tail a little strangely before he goes down, sir?" asked Tashtego.

"And has he a curious spout too," said Daggoo, "very bushy, even for a sperm whale, and very quick, Captain Ahab?"

"And he have one, two, three — oh! good many iron in him hide, too, Captain," cried Queequeg, "all twiske-tee be-twisk, like him — him —" He moved his hand round and round, trying to find the word for "corkscrew".

"Corkscrew!" cried Ahab. "Yes, Queequeg, the harpoons lie all twisted and bent in him. Yes, Daggoo, his spout is a big one, like a whole field of wheat, and white as a pile of wool. Yes, Tashtego, and he fans his tail like a broken sail in a storm. Death and devils! Men, it is Moby Dick you have seen — Moby Dick — Moby Dick!"

"Captain Ahab," said Starbuck, who, with Stubb and Flask, had been looking at Ahab with growing surprise, "Captain Ahab, I have heard of Moby Dick. But wasn't it Moby Dick that took off your leg?"

"Who told you that?" cried Ahab. Then he became quiet for a moment. "Yes, Starbuck. Yes, my hearts, all of you. It was Moby Dick that took my leg. It was Moby Dick that brought me to this dead stump I stand on now." He raised his arms and shouted with a terrible, loud cry, like a wounded animal. "Yes, yes! And I'll chase him round Good Hope, and round the Horn, and round the Norway whirlpool, and round the fires of hell before I give him up. And this is what you have shipped for, men! To chase that white whale on both sides of the world, until he spouts black blood and rolls dead in the water. What do you say, men? Will you join hands on it now? I think you look brave."

"Yes, yes!" shouted the harpooners and the sailors, running closer to the excited old man. "A sharp eye for the white whale! A sharp lance for Moby Dick!"

"God bless you," Ahab said, half crying and half shouting. "Steward! Bring the drink. But what's this long face about, Mr Starbuck? Won't you chase the white whale? Aren't you brave enough for Moby Dick?"

"I am brave enough for his crooked jaw, and for the jaws of Death too, Captain Ahab, if it comes in the way of our business," said Starbuck. "But I came here to hunt whales, not my commander's revenge. How many barrels will your revenge give you, even if you get it, Captain Ahab? It will not bring much money in our Nantucket market."

"Nantucket market! Ha!" cried Ahab. "If money is your measure, man, then let me tell you that my revenge will bring a great profit — here!" And he hit his own chest.

"Revenge on a dumb animal!" cried Starbuck. "An animal that attacked you only because of its blind instinct! Madness! To be angry with a dumb thing, Captain Ahab, seems like blasphemy — an insult against God."

"Listen to me, Starbuck," said Ahab, more quietly. "All the things that we can see are only masks made of cardboard. But in every event — in every living act — there is something unknown but reasonable behind the mask. If a man wants to strike, he must strike through the mask! How can a prisoner reach the outside except by breaking through the wall? To me, the white whale is that wall. Sometimes I think there is nothing beyond it. But that is enough. He provokes me. He hurts me. I see in him great strength, and great evil behind it. That evil thing is what I hate most of all. Whether the white whale is the agent or the master, I will hit that hate at him. Don't talk to me of blasphemy, man. I'd strike the sun if it insulted me."

Starbuck said nothing. He turned pale and looked down.

"The crew, man, the crew!" said Ahab. "Aren't they all with me against the white whale? Look at them! Stand up among them, Starbuck. Look — they are all with me. Only you are left."

Starbuck said quietly to himself, "God keep me! Keep us all!" But he did not speak against Ahab again.

The steward brought a big jug full of strong drink. Ahab told the three mates to stand in front of him with their lances, and the three harpooners with their harpoons. He made the mates cross their lances in front of him, and he put his hand on the point where they met. As he touched them, he looked into the eyes of each of the mates, as if he wanted to put his own fiery will into them. Stubb and Flask looked away. Starbuck looked down.

Then he told the harpooners to take the iron heads off their harpoons and turn them upside down, so that the hollow metal sockets could be used as cups. He filled them with the fiery drink.

"Drink, you harpooners!" he cried. "Drink and swear, you men who will stand in the front of the deadly whaleboat — death to Moby Dick! God hunt us all, if we do not hunt Moby Dick to his death!"

The harpooners raised the cups and drank. "Death to Moby Dick!" they shouted, and all the crew shouted with them. Starbuck turned pale and trembled. Then Ahab went back into his cabin, and the men slowly went back to their work.
