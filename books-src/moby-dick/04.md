## Part 4 — The Pequod

There were three ships in the harbour that were getting ready for three-year voyages: the *Devil-Dam*, the *Tit-bit* and the *Pequod*. I looked at the *Devil-Dam* and the *Tit-bit*, but they did not please me. Then I went on board the *Pequod*, and I knew at once that this was the ship for us.

The *Pequod* was named after a Native American tribe from Massachusetts, a tribe that no longer exists. She was a small, old ship, with an old-fashioned shape. Her sides had been darkened by the weather of all four oceans. Her old masts had been cut in Japan, after the original ones had been lost in a storm. Her decks were worn like an old church floor where many pilgrims have walked.

But the strangest thing about her was her decoration. All around her sides, instead of wood, there were long, sharp teeth of sperm whales, used like pegs to hold the ropes. Her **tiller** — the handle used to steer the ship — was not made of wood but of the long, thin lower jaw of a whale. She was a cannibal of a ship, decorated with the bones of her enemies.

I looked around for someone in charge. Near the main mast there was a strange little tent, made of long pieces of whalebone. Inside sat an old man, brown and strong, dressed in the old-fashioned Quaker style. He was looking at me with sharp, small eyes, wrinkled from years of looking into storms at sea.

"Is this the captain of the *Pequod*?" I asked.

"Suppose it is. What dost thou want with him?" he said. He spoke in the old Quaker way, using "thee" and "thou" instead of "you".

"I was thinking of shipping," I said.

"Oh, were you? I can see you are not from Nantucket. Have you ever been in a boat that a whale broke?"

"No, sir, never."

"So you know nothing at all about whaling — eh?"

"Nothing, sir. But I have been in the merchant service several times."

"Forget the merchant service! Don't talk to me about it. Why do you want to go whaling?"

I told him that I wanted to see what whaling was, and to see the world.

"Want to see what whaling is, eh? Have you seen Captain Ahab?"

"Who is Captain Ahab, sir?"

"Aye, aye, I thought so. Captain Ahab is the captain of this ship."

"I am mistaken then. I thought I was speaking to the captain himself."

"You are speaking to Captain Peleg, young man. Captain Bildad and I are the owners. We get the ship ready for her voyages. But if you want to know what whaling is, I can tell you. Look at Captain Ahab, young man, and you will see that he has only one leg."

"What do you mean, sir? Was the other one lost by a whale?"

"Lost to a whale! Young man, come closer. It was eaten, chewed up and crushed by the most monstrous whale that ever broke a boat! Ah, ah!"

I was a little frightened by his passion, but I said that I still wanted to go. So he took me to see Captain Bildad, the other owner.

**Peleg and Bildad**

Captain Bildad was sitting in the cabin, reading a big Bible. He was a thin, dry old man, a strict Quaker, who had made a lot of money from whaling. He had been a famous captain, and they said that he had been a very hard one, who never let his men rest. He never swore and never laughed. Peleg, on the other hand, swore all the time and did not care what anyone thought of him.

The two old captains began to discuss how much I should be paid. On a whaling ship, men do not get a salary. Each man gets a **lay** — a share of the profit from the oil, when the ship comes home. The captain gets a big share, the officers get smaller ones, and the simple sailors get very small ones. I expected something like the 275th lay — that means one part in 275 of all the money.

"Well, Captain Bildad," said Peleg, "what lay shall we give this young man?"

"Thou knowest best," said Bildad in a quiet voice, without looking up from his Bible. "The seven hundred and seventy-seventh wouldn't be too much, would it? The Bible says we must not keep treasures on earth..."

"Seven hundred and seventy-seventh!" I thought. That would be almost nothing!

"Damn it, Bildad!" cried Peleg. "You don't want to cheat this young man! He must have more than that. Give him the three hundredth lay."

They argued for a long time. Peleg shouted, and Bildad read his Bible and said quiet things about saving money. In the end, Peleg won, and they put me down for the three hundredth lay. I signed the papers. Then I told them that I had a friend, a harpooner, who would like to sail too. "Bring him along tomorrow," said Peleg.

Before leaving, I asked to see Captain Ahab.

"Why do you want to see him?" said Peleg. "It's all right. You are on the ship's list now."

"Yes, but I should like to see him."

"I don't think you can at the moment. I don't know exactly what's the matter with him. He stays inside his house. He is a kind of sick, and yet he doesn't look sick. Captain Ahab is a grand, ungodly, god-like man. He doesn't speak much, but when he speaks, you should listen. He has studied in colleges, and he has lived among cannibals. He has seen stranger wonders than the waves, and he has fought stranger enemies than whales."

Then he told me that Ahab had been named after a wicked king in the Bible, whose blood was licked by dogs. "But he did not choose his own name," said Peleg. "Captain Ahab is a good man — not a pious, good man, like Bildad, but a swearing good man, something like me, only there's a good deal more of him. Since he lost his leg on the last voyage, he has been a little moody — wild sometimes. But it will all pass. And remember, he has a wife — a sweet, young girl — and a little child. So he can't be completely bad."

I left the ship with strange feelings. I felt pity for Captain Ahab, and also a kind of fear. But I soon forgot him, because I had other things to think about.

**Queequeg's Ramadan**

Queequeg had told me that he would spend the whole day in our room, fasting and praying. It was his "Ramadan", as he called it. I did not want to disturb him, so I stayed away until the evening. But when I returned to the inn, the door of our room was locked, and he did not answer when I knocked.

"Queequeg!" I called. "Queequeg! Why don't you speak?"

Nothing. I began to be afraid. Perhaps he was ill. Perhaps he had killed himself. I ran down to Mrs Hussey, and together we came back. She had brought a hammer and an axe, and she was shouting about the young man in Number Three. I did not wait for her. I threw myself against the door with all my strength, and it burst open.

There sat Queequeg in the middle of the room, cool and calm, squatting on the floor with his legs crossed. On the top of his head sat his little idol, Yojo. He did not look at us. He did not move. He looked like a statue.

"Queequeg," I said, "what's the matter with you?"

He did not answer. Mrs Hussey went away, satisfied that he was alive. I sat down and waited for him to finish. He sat there all night, without moving or speaking, until the sun rose the next morning. Then he stood up, stiff and slow, and smiled at me as if nothing had happened.

I told him then, as gently as I could, what I thought about his Ramadan. I said that fasting for days and sitting on cold floors all night was bad for the health. It was bad for the soul, too. It gave people stomach problems and gloomy thoughts. All these religious ideas about hell, I said, were caused by bad digestion.

Queequeg listened politely, but he did not seem convinced. He only looked at me with a kind of pity, as if he thought I needed someone to save me from my own foolishness. Then we went down to breakfast, and Queequeg ate so much that Mrs Hussey worried that she would not have enough food for her other guests.
