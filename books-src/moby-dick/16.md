## Part 16 — Stubb Kills a Whale

**The Line**

Before I describe our first whale, I must tell you about the **whale-line** — the long rope tied to the harpoon. It is about two hundred fathoms long — more than a thousand feet. It lies in a wooden tub in the middle of the whaleboat, coiled round and round very carefully, so that it can run out fast without getting tangled. From the tub it goes all round the boat, over the men's heads and hands, between their legs, to the front, where it is tied to the harpoon.

When the harpoon hits the whale and the whale dives, the line runs out with terrible speed. It flies through the boat like a snake. If it catches a man's arm or leg, it can pull him into the sea, or cut him in half. So, when the line is running, the men sit very still, and they look as calm as if they were in a church. But they are sitting in the middle of a hundred dangers. All men live surrounded by whale-lines, in a way. All are born with ropes around their necks. But only when they are caught in the sudden turn of death do they understand the silent dangers of life.

**The First Whale**

After the giant squid, the weather became calm and sunny. One day I was at the mast-head again, half asleep, dreaming, when suddenly I saw it: a huge sperm whale, lying quietly on the surface of the sea, not far away, spouting gently like a man smoking a pipe on a warm afternoon.

"There she blows!" I shouted.

In a moment, the boats were in the water. The whale heard us and went down, but soon he came up again, closer. Stubb's boat was the nearest.

"Start her, start her, my men!" said Stubb, with his pipe in his mouth. "Don't hurry yourselves. Take plenty of time — but start her. Start her like thunder-claps, that's all. Start her, Tashtego! Start her, my boy!"

His men rowed fast, and Tashtego stood up with his harpoon.

"Stand up, Tashtego! Give it to him!"

The harpoon flew. The whale felt it and rushed forward, pulling the boat behind him through the water. The line went hissing out of the tub, so fast that smoke rose from the wood where it rubbed. The boat flew over the waves. Water came in over the sides. The men held tight.

When the whale began to tire, Stubb's men pulled on the line, bringing the boat close to his side. Then Stubb took his long, sharp **lance** and pushed it again and again into the whale's body, turning it, looking for his life. At last the whale's spout became red. He was spouting blood. His life was coming out of him.

He rolled and struggled in the red water. Then a terrible shiver went through his great body, and he turned over on his side, dead.

"He's dead, Mr Stubb," said Daggoo.

"Yes," said Stubb, taking his pipe out of his mouth and shaking out the ashes. For a moment he stood looking at the huge dead body. Then he lit his pipe again.

**The Harpooner's Work**

I must say something about the harpooner's job. In a whaleboat, the harpooner rows like everyone else until the very last moment. He has to row as hard as he can, until he is completely out of breath. Then, suddenly, he has to stand up and throw his heavy harpoon with all his strength and perfect aim. It is not surprising that so many harpoons miss. A tired man cannot throw well. If the harpooner could rest before throwing, many more whales would be caught.

**Stubb's Supper**

We tied the dead whale to the side of the ship with heavy chains. It was night now, and the work of cutting it up would begin in the morning.

Stubb was very happy. He wanted to eat a steak from the whale's tail. He told Daggoo to cut one for him, and he called the old black cook, Fleece, to cook it.

Fleece was a very old man, with bad knees. He came slowly, complaining. Stubb ate his steak by the light of a lantern, while thousands of sharks swam around the dead whale in the dark water below, tearing pieces of meat from its body with their teeth. You could hear them biting and fighting.

"Cook," said Stubb, "this steak is too tough. And those sharks are making too much noise. Go and preach to them. Tell them to be quiet and to eat politely."

So the poor old cook leaned over the side of the ship with his lantern and spoke to the sharks. "Fellow-creatures," he said, "I'm told to tell you to stop that damned noise. You hear? Stop that damned smacking of your lips! Your nature is to be sharks, I know. But you must control your nature. A shark that controls himself is an angel. And remember — no matter how much you eat, you must share with your neighbours."

"Well done, old Fleece!" laughed Stubb. "That's Christianity! Go on!"

"No use going on," said Fleece. "The damned sharks will never listen. They don't hear anything. They're just stuffing their stomachs, and when their stomachs are full, they won't hear anything at all. They'll go to sleep." He raised his lantern and gave them his blessing: "Cursed fellow-creatures! Make all the noise you want. Fill your stomachs until they burst — and then die!"

Then Stubb complained about the steak again and told Fleece how to cook whale meat properly in the future. The old man went back to his bed, grumbling. "Wish, by gor, whale eat him, instead of him eat whale," he said. "He's more of a shark than Massa Shark himself."

**Whale Meat**

Is whale meat good to eat? Some people in the past thought so. In old France and England, kings and rich people ate whale meat at their feasts. But today, few people want to eat it. It is too rich and too oily. Sailors sometimes eat the brains of small whales, which they say taste like calf's brains. But most people are disgusted by the idea of eating such a huge creature, perhaps because it seems too much like eating a man.

And yet, do we not eat cows and pigs? Do we not kill animals every day for our food and our clothes? The man who eats beef with a knife and fork, then complains about whalemen who eat a whale, should look at himself first.
