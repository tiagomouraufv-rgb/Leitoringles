## Part 8 — Whales, Officers and the Mast-Head

**What Is a Whale?**

Before we go further, I want to say something about whales themselves. Scientists have tried for many years to put whales into groups, as they do with other animals, but nobody has done it well. The sea is so big, and the whales are so hard to see, that much is still unknown.

Some scientists say that a whale is not a fish, because it breathes air, has warm blood and feeds its babies with milk. I do not agree with them. I follow the old sailors, who say that a whale is a spouting fish with a horizontal tail. That is good enough for me.

I will divide whales into three groups, like books: large, medium and small.

The large whales are the giants. The greatest of them is the **sperm whale**. It is the largest animal in the world, the most dangerous to hunt, and the most valuable. Its head contains a precious oil called **spermaceti**, which makes the best candles. Then there is the **right whale**, which was hunted first by the old whalemen. It gives the long, flexible "whalebone" that ladies use in their clothes. There is the fin-back, a very fast and wild whale, which is rarely caught. There is the humpback, which is playful and often jumps out of the water. And there are others.

The medium whales include the grampus and the killer whale, which is very fierce and attacks even large whales.

Among the small whales are the porpoises, the happy fish that swim in groups in front of ships, jumping in and out of the waves. When sailors see them, they shout, "Hooray!" because they believe it means good luck.

There are also some whales that are known only from old stories. I leave them for others to study. This system of mine is not complete, and I do not think it ever will be. God keep me from ever completing anything. This whole book is only a draft — no, only the draft of a draft.

**The Captain Alone**

In the old days, on Dutch whaling ships, the chief harpooner was almost as important as the captain. Now that is no longer true. But on an American whaling ship, the harpooners still have special rights. They sleep and eat with the officers, at the back of the ship, not with the simple sailors at the front.

The captain is above everyone. On most whaling ships, he is friendly with his officers, and they talk and joke together. But Captain Ahab was not like that. He did not use his power to frighten people, but he kept himself completely apart. He was like the last grey bear in a forest, living alone in his cave for the whole winter, feeding on his own pain.

**Dinner in the Cabin**

At dinner time, the cook's boy called, "Dinner, Mr Starbuck," and then Ahab went down to his cabin. One by one, in order of rank, the officers followed him.

At the table, Ahab sat at the head, like a silent lion among his cubs. The mates waited for him to give them food, with the respect of small children. Nobody spoke. If Ahab did not speak, nobody else dared. Starbuck got his meat first, then Stubb, then Flask. Poor Flask was the last to come and the first to leave. He was always hungry. He had to wait until everyone else had their food, and he had to leave before the others finished. "Since I became an officer," he said, "I have never had a full stomach. I was much happier as a simple sailor, when I could eat as much as I wanted."

When the officers had finished, the harpooners came down to eat what was left. They were very different. They made a lot of noise, laughing and talking. They ate enormous amounts of meat. Queequeg and Tashtego cut the meat with their knives, and Daggoo took big pieces in his huge hands. The cook's boy was afraid of them, and he ran in and out as quickly as he could.

**On the Mast-Head**

On a whaling ship, there are always men at the top of the masts — the **mast-heads** — looking for whales. They stay there all day, for two hours at a time, one man after another. It is a lonely job. You stand on two small pieces of wood, a hundred feet above the deck, and the ship swings gently from side to side like a tree in the wind.

My turn came, like everyone else's. But I was a very bad lookout. In that high, quiet place, with the blue sea all around me, I forgot all about whales. I began to dream. I thought about life and death, about the soul and the universe. The waves below me became a picture of my own deep thoughts. I lost myself completely in my dreams.

This is a danger, and I warn all young men who like to think too much: be careful on the mast-head! If you forget yourself completely, you may move your foot or your hand a little, and fall. Your scream will sound for one moment in the air, and then you will drop into the sea and never come up again. So, young dreamers, keep one eye on your feet.

Whaling captains often complain about young men like me. "These dreamers come on board to think about the universe," they say, "and they never see a single whale."
