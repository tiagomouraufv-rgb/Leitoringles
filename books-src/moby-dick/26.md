## Part 26 — Starbuck, Ahab and Queequeg's Coffin

**The Leak**

One morning, the sailors noticed that oil was coming up with the water from the bottom of the ship. Some of the casks in the hold must be leaking. Starbuck went down to Ahab's cabin to tell him.

Ahab was studying his charts. He did not look up.

"Captain," said Starbuck, "the oil in the hold is leaking. We must bring up the casks and repair them. It will take a day or two."

"Bring them up? Now, when we are getting close to Japan? Stop for a week to repair a few old barrels?"

"Either we do that, sir, or we lose more oil in one day than we can make in a year. What we came twenty thousand miles to get is worth saving, sir."

"So it is, so it is — if we get it."

"I was speaking of the oil in the hold, sir."

"And I was not speaking of it. Go! Let it leak! I'm all leaks myself. I'm leaking in a leaky ship. But I don't stop to repair myself. I go on. Go, Starbuck. Back to the deck!"

"Captain Ahab," said Starbuck, his face red. "What will the owners say?"

"Let the owners stand on Nantucket beach and shout louder than the storms. What do I care? The owners, Starbuck! You are always talking about those mean owners, as if they were my conscience. But listen — the only real owner of anything is its commander. And my conscience is in this ship's keel. Up to the deck!"

"Captain Ahab," said Starbuck, coming further into the cabin, "a better man than I might forgive you. But I ask you, please —"

Ahab took a loaded musket from the wall and pointed it at Starbuck.

"There is one God who is Lord over the earth," he cried, "and one Captain who is lord over the *Pequod*. On deck!"

For a moment, Starbuck's eyes burned. Then he spoke very calmly. "You have insulted me, sir. But I will not ask you to beware of Starbuck. You would only laugh. But let Ahab beware of Ahab. Beware of yourself, old man."

He went out. Ahab stood still, with the musket in his hand. Then he said to himself, "He's brave, but he obeys. What did he say? Ahab, beware of Ahab? There's something in that." After a while, he went up on deck and said to Starbuck, "You are too good a man, Starbuck." And he gave the order to repair the casks.

**Queequeg's Fever**

To find the leaking casks, the men had to go deep down into the dark, wet bottom of the hold, and move the heavy barrels one by one. Queequeg worked there for many days. It was cold and damp, and the air was bad. And then my poor friend caught a terrible fever.

He lay in his hammock for days, getting thinner and weaker. His eyes became bigger and rounder, and they seemed to look into a far-away distance. Everybody thought that he would die. He thought so too.

Then he called me and told me something strange. On his island, he said, when a man died, his body was put in a canoe and sent out to sea, to float away to the stars. Queequeg did not want to be thrown into the sea in his hammock, like a dead sailor, to be eaten by sharks. He wanted a canoe — a coffin shaped like his island canoes.

So the carpenter made him a coffin out of dark wood. When it was ready, Queequeg asked to see it. He lay down in it to try it, with his harpoon beside him and his little idol Yojo on his chest. He asked for some biscuit and a bottle of fresh water to be put in it with him, for his journey. Then he said, "It is good," and they lifted him out and put him back in his hammock.

Little Pip came and looked at him. He took Queequeg's hand. "Poor rover! Will you never finish your weary roving? Where are you going now? If you go to the Antilles, where the waves wash the beaches of white lilies, will you do a small thing for me? Find a boy called Pip, who has been missing for a long time. I think he is in those far Antilles. If you find him, comfort him, because he must be very sad. Look! He left his tambourine behind. I found it. Rig-a-dig, dig, dig! Now, Queequeg, die, and I'll beat your dead march."

**Queequeg Decides to Live**

But then, to everyone's surprise, Queequeg began to get better. In a few days, he was well again. When we asked him how, he said that he had suddenly remembered something important that he had to do on land, and so he had decided not to die. "If a man decides to live," he said, "a small sickness cannot kill him. Only a whale or a storm can kill a man who has decided to live."

He used the coffin as a sea chest, to keep his clothes in. And in his free time, he carved designs on the lid — copies of the tattoos on his body. A wise man on his island had made those tattoos long ago. They told the whole story of heaven and earth, and a way to find the truth. But Queequeg could not read them. So his body was a living book of mysteries that even he could not understand — and one day, those mysteries would die with him.

Ahab looked at Queequeg one morning, as he sat carving the lid, and said, "Oh, devilish torture! Here is a book that its owner cannot read."
