## Part 5 — The Prophet and the Departure

When Queequeg and I went down to the *Pequod* together, Captain Peleg shouted from the deck that cannibals were not allowed on board unless they showed their papers.

"What do you mean?" I said. "This is Queequeg, a member of the First Congregational Church."

"Hmm," said Bildad, looking at Queequeg's tattoos. "Young man, how long has he been a member? He looks like he has not even been baptized."

I told them that Queequeg belonged to the great church of the whole world — the church to which all of us belong, Christians and pagans, because we all worship the same God in our own ways. Peleg laughed. "Young man, you'd better ship as a missionary instead of a sailor! I never heard a better sermon. But let's see if this cannibal can throw a harpoon."

Queequeg did not say a word. He picked up his harpoon and jumped onto the side of the ship. He pointed at a small drop of tar on the water, far out, and said, "You see him small drop tar on water dere? You see him? Well, spose him one whale eye, well, den!" And he threw the harpoon. It flew across the deck and hit the drop of tar exactly, making it disappear.

"Quick, Bildad!" cried Peleg. "Get the papers! We must have him. We'll give him the ninetieth lay, and that's more than any harpooner from Nantucket ever got!"

Queequeg could not write his name. So he signed the paper with a copy of a strange round shape that was tattooed on his arm. Before we left, Bildad gave him a little religious booklet called *The Latter Day Coming; or No Time to Lose*, and tried to talk to him about his soul. Peleg pulled him away. "Leave him alone, Bildad. Pious harpooners never make good sailors. They lose all their courage."

**Elijah**

As Queequeg and I were walking away from the ship, a man stopped us. He was dressed in dirty old clothes, and his face was marked by smallpox. He pointed at the *Pequod* with his thick finger.

"Shipmates," he said, "have you shipped in that ship?"

"Yes," I said. "We have just signed the papers."

"Anything down there about your souls?"

"About what?"

"Oh, perhaps you haven't got any," he said quickly. "No matter, though. I know many men who have no souls. Good luck to them. They are better off without them. A soul's a sort of fifth wheel to a wagon."

"What are you talking about?" I said.

"Have you seen Old Thunder?"

"Who is Old Thunder?"

"Captain Ahab."

"No, we haven't seen him yet. They say he is sick, but he is getting better."

"Getting better!" The stranger laughed strangely. "Look here. When Captain Ahab is all right, then this left arm of mine will be all right. Not before."

"What do you know about him?"

"What did they tell you about him? Did they tell you about the time he was in a fight with a Spaniard in front of an altar in Santa? Did they tell you about the time he lay like dead for three days and nights off Cape Horn? Did they tell you how he lost his leg? Ah, I see they didn't tell you everything. Well, it is all fixed now. Some sailors must go with him, I suppose. Goodbye, shipmates. May God bless you."

"Wait," I said. "If you have something important to tell us, tell us. If not, stop trying to frighten us."

"Oh, you'll find out," he said. "Goodbye." He turned away. Then he looked back and said, "Oh, I forgot. My name is Elijah." And he went away.

I told Queequeg that the man was a crazy fool, just trying to scare us. But for some reason, his strange words stayed in my mind. Elijah was the name of a prophet in the Bible — the prophet who spoke against the wicked King Ahab.

**Getting Ready**

For several days, there was a lot of activity on board the *Pequod*. Men carried on old sails, new ropes, barrels of water, bread, meat, and all the things that a ship needs for a voyage of three years. A whaling ship must carry spare boats, spare masts and spare harpoons, because she is far from any port for many months. She must carry everything a whole village would need.

Captain Bildad's sister, a thin old lady called Aunt Charity, was everywhere at once. She brought jars of pickles, quills for writing, a new Bible for the first mate, and a nightcap for the second mate. She ran up and down the ship all day, worrying about everyone.

But during all this time, nobody saw Captain Ahab. He stayed in his house in the town. When I asked about him, they said that he was getting better, and that he would come on board before the ship sailed.

**At Dawn**

On the morning we were to sail, Queequeg and I went down to the harbour very early, before the sun was up. There was a grey mist. As we came near the ship, I thought I saw some dark figures running through the mist towards the *Pequod*. They went on board and disappeared.

"Look, Queequeg," I said. "Are those sailors?" But when we got on board, there was nobody on deck. Everything was quiet.

At that moment, someone touched my shoulder. It was Elijah.

"Going aboard?" he asked.

"Yes. Leave us alone."

"Did you see anything like men going towards that ship a while ago?"

"Yes, I thought I saw four or five men. But it was too dark to be sure."

"Very dark, very dark," said Elijah. "Well, I wonder if you'll find them tonight. Goodbye. Goodbye. I won't stop you any longer." And he went away into the mist, laughing quietly.

We went on board. Everything was silent. In the forecastle — the room at the front of the ship where the sailors sleep — there was only an old sailor asleep on a pile of ropes. Where had the dark men gone? I never found out, at least not then.

**Merry Christmas**

At last, at noon on a cold, grey Christmas Day, the *Pequod* was ready to sail. The crew were on board. The two old owners, Peleg and Bildad, were giving orders. They would guide the ship out of the harbour and then return to land in a small boat.

There was still no sign of Captain Ahab on deck. They said he was in his cabin. I thought it was strange that a captain would not come up to watch his ship leave port. But perhaps, I thought, it was normal on whaling ships. The owners often take the ship out to sea themselves, and the captain only comes up when they have gone.

Captain Peleg shouted orders and swore at the men. Bildad sang a sad old hymn about the green fields of heaven, beyond the flood. The men sang too, as they pulled up the heavy anchor. At last the *Pequod* began to move. The wind was cold and strong, and the waves were rising. Snow and ice covered the ropes.

When we were outside the harbour, the two old captains got ready to leave. Bildad walked slowly up and down the deck. It was very hard for him to leave the ship, because he had put a lot of his money into her, and because he had sailed for many years himself. He looked at the sails and the ropes, and he took the hands of the officers.

"Mr Starbuck, be careful with the cask of butter. You used too much on the last voyage. And don't let the men waste the candles. And don't whale on Sundays, Mr Stubb, if you can help it. But don't miss a good chance, either. That would be rejecting Heaven's good gifts."

"Come, come, Captain Bildad," said Peleg. "Stop talking. Away!"

The two old men climbed down into their boat. They waved to us, and we gave three loud cheers. Then the *Pequod* turned her face to the open ocean, and we sailed out into the lonely Atlantic, like fate.
