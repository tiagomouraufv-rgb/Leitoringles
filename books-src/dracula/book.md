# Dracula

## About this edition

This is the complete story of *Dracula* by Bram Stoker (1897), retold in English at level B1. Every chapter and every important event of the original is here, but the sentences are shorter and the words are simpler.

Like the original, the story is told through diaries, letters and newspaper reports. Each part begins with the name of the person who is writing. Tap any word to see its meaning. Tap a marked phrase to see a grammar note.

**The main characters**

- **Jonathan Harker** — a young English lawyer
- **Mina Murray** — Jonathan's fiancée, a teacher; later Mina Harker
- **Lucy Westenra** — Mina's best friend
- **Dr John Seward** — a young doctor who runs a hospital for the mentally ill
- **Arthur Holmwood** — Lucy's fiancé; later Lord Godalming
- **Quincey Morris** — a rich American, friend of Arthur and Seward
- **Professor Abraham Van Helsing** — a Dutch doctor and scientist, Seward's old teacher
- **Renfield** — a patient in Dr Seward's hospital
- **Count Dracula** — an old nobleman from Transylvania

## Chapter 1 — The Journey to Transylvania

**Jonathan Harker's Journal**

*3 May. Bistritz.* I left Munich at 8.35 in the evening on 1st May and arrived in Vienna early the next morning. The train was an hour late. Budapest looked like a wonderful city, but I only saw a little of it from the train and the streets near the station. I felt that I was leaving the West and entering the East.

In the evening we arrived at Klausenburg, and I stayed the night at the Hotel Royale. For dinner I had chicken cooked with red pepper. It was very good, but it made me thirsty. I asked the waiter for the recipe, because I want to make it for Mina when I get home.

Before I left London, I visited the British Museum and read some books about Transylvania. I wanted to know something about the country, because I am going to meet a nobleman from there. His name is Count Dracula. My employer, Mr Hawkins, has sent me to him. The Count is buying a house in England, and I must explain all the legal papers to him.

The district where the Count lives is in the far east of the country, near the borders of three states: Transylvania, Moldavia and Bukovina. It is in the middle of the Carpathian mountains, one of the wildest and least known parts of Europe. I could not find the exact place of Castle Dracula on any map. But I learned that Bistritz, a small town, is not far from it.

I read that many people in Transylvania believe in old stories and **superstitions**. They say that the Carpathian mountains are the centre of every strange belief in the world. I must ask the Count about them.

I did not sleep well at the hotel. A dog cried under my window all night. Perhaps it was the red pepper, too, because I drank all the water in my room and was still thirsty. Towards morning I slept, and somebody knocked on my door to wake me.

The train was supposed to leave at eight, but it left at nearly ten. It seems that the further east you go, the less punctual the trains are. All day we travelled through a beautiful country. We passed little towns and castles on top of steep hills. We saw rivers and streams that were very wide, full of water from the melting snow. At every station there were groups of people in colourful clothes. The women looked pretty, but they were not elegant. Some men were tall and strong, with big black moustaches, and they looked like robbers from an old play.

It was dark when we arrived at Bistritz, an interesting old town. The Count had told me in his letter to stay at the Golden Krone Hotel. When I arrived, an old woman in peasant clothes came to the door.

"Are you the English gentleman?" she asked.

"Yes," I said. "Jonathan Harker."

She smiled and spoke to an old man in a white shirt. He went away and came back with a letter:

> *My friend, — Welcome to the Carpathians. I am waiting for you. Sleep well tonight. At three tomorrow the coach will leave for Bukovina; there is a place in it for you. At the Borgo Pass my carriage will be waiting for you, and it will bring you to me. I hope that your journey from London has been happy, and that you will enjoy your stay in my beautiful land. — Your friend, DRACULA.*

*4 May.* The owner of the hotel had also received a letter from the Count, telling him to keep the best seat in the coach for me. But when I asked him and his wife questions about the Count, they suddenly became nervous. They said that they did not understand my German, although until then they had understood it perfectly. They did not want to say anything about the Count or his castle. When I asked them directly, they made the sign of the cross and said nothing more.

Just before I left, the old woman came to my room. She was very nervous.

"Must you go?" she asked. "Oh, young gentleman, must you go?"

I told her that I had to go, because I had important business with the Count.

"Do you know what day it is?" she asked.

"It's the fourth of May," I said.

"Yes, I know. But do you know what day it is? Today is the eve of Saint George's Day. Tonight, when the clock strikes midnight, all the evil things in the world will have full power. Do you know where you are going, and what you are going to?"

She was so worried that I tried to calm her, but it was no good. She fell on her knees and begged me not to go — or at least to wait one or two days. It was all very silly, but I did not feel comfortable. I thanked her and told her that I had to go. Then she stood up, took a **crucifix** from her neck, and offered it to me.

I did not know what to do. As an English Protestant, I have been taught that such things are a kind of superstition. But it seemed rude to refuse an old lady who was trying to help me. She saw my doubt, put the crucifix around my neck and said, "For your mother's sake." Then she left the room.

I am writing this while I wait for the coach. The crucifix is still around my neck. I don't know why, but I don't feel as calm as usual.

*5 May. The Castle.* When I got on the coach, the driver was talking to the hotel owner's wife. They often looked at me, and some of the people outside looked at me with pity. I heard some words again and again, and I looked them up in my little dictionary. They meant "Satan", "hell", "witch" and "**vampire**". When we started, all the people made the sign of the cross and pointed two fingers towards me. A man explained that this was protection against the evil eye.

We drove through a green country full of forests and orchards. In the distance there were great mountains covered with snow. As the evening came, the light on the mountains turned purple and gold. The other passengers became nervous as it grew dark. They kept telling the driver to go faster. He used his long whip on the horses, and we went at a terrible speed.

The passengers gave me small presents: a wild rose, some garlic, a dried flower. They did it kindly, but seriously, as if they wanted to protect me. Each of them made the sign of the cross.

At last we came to the Borgo Pass. It was dark, and the air was cold. The passengers looked out of the windows, and everybody was waiting for something. But there was no carriage. The driver looked at his watch and said to me in German, "There is no carriage here. The gentleman is not expected after all. He will now come on to Bukovina and return tomorrow."

Just then the horses began to shake and kick. A carriage with four black horses came up behind us and stopped. The driver was a tall man with a long brown beard and a big black hat, so I could not see his face. When the lamps shone on him, I saw that his eyes were very bright and seemed red. He said to our driver:

"You are early tonight, my friend."

"The English gentleman was in a hurry," said our driver nervously.

"That is why you wanted him to go on to Bukovina, I suppose," said the stranger. "You cannot deceive me, my friend. I know too much, and my horses are fast." He smiled, and I saw that his mouth was very red and his teeth were sharp and white as ivory. One of the passengers whispered a line from a famous poem: *"For the dead travel fast."*

The stranger took my bags and helped me into his carriage with a hand like steel. He was extremely strong. Then he put a heavy blanket over my knees and said, in excellent German, "The night is cold, sir, and my master the Count told me to look after you."

We drove away into the darkness. I felt that we were going along the same road again and again, but I could not be sure. Then dogs began to howl at farmhouses far away, and soon a wolf howled, and then many wolves. The horses were terribly afraid, but the driver spoke to them softly and they became calm.

Suddenly, on the left, I saw a faint blue flame. The driver stopped the carriage, jumped down and disappeared into the darkness. Soon he returned. This happened several times. Once, the flame appeared very close to the road, and the driver stood between me and the flame. Strangely, I could still see the flame through his body. It was as if he was not there at all.

Then the wolves came. They stood in a circle around us, with white teeth and red tongues, long legs and thick hair. They were silent, and this was even more frightening than their howling. The horses screamed with fear. I shouted and hit the side of the carriage, hoping to frighten them away. Then the driver appeared. He stood on the road, raised his arms and spoke to the wolves with a voice of command. They moved back slowly, and then a great cloud covered the moon, and we were in darkness again.

When I could see again, the driver was climbing back into the carriage, and the wolves had gone. It was all so strange that I was very afraid, and I was not able to speak.

We drove on for a long time. Then the driver stopped in the courtyard of a great ruined castle. Its tall black windows had no light in them, and its broken walls stood against the sky in the moonlight.

## Chapter 2 — The Count

**Jonathan Harker's Journal (continued)**

*5 May.* The driver helped me out of the carriage. Again I noticed how strong his hand was. He put my bags on the ground beside me, near a great old door with heavy iron nails. Then he jumped back onto the carriage and drove away into the darkness.

I stood there in silence. There was no bell and no knocker. What a strange adventure! Was this the usual experience of a young lawyer's clerk? I had just passed my exams in London, and now I was a real lawyer. I rubbed my eyes and pinched myself to see if I was awake. It all seemed like a terrible dream.

Then I heard heavy steps behind the door and saw a light through the cracks. There was the sound of chains and of a great key turning in the lock. The door opened.

Inside stood a tall old man. He had a long white moustache and was dressed in black from head to foot. He held an old silver lamp in his hand.

"Welcome to my house!" he said in excellent English, but with a strange accent. "Enter freely and of your own free will!"

He did not move to meet me. He stood like a statue. But the moment I stepped over the doorway, he came forward and shook my hand. His hand was as cold as ice — more like the hand of a dead man than a living one.

"Welcome to my house. Come freely. Go safely. And leave something of the happiness you bring!"

His strength reminded me of the driver, and for a moment I wondered if they were the same person. To be sure, I asked, "Count Dracula?"

He bowed politely. "I am Dracula. And I welcome you, Mr Harker, to my house. Come in. The night air is cold, and you must need to eat and rest." He picked up my bags himself before I could stop him. "No, sir, you are my guest. It is late, and my servants are not available. Let me look after you myself."

He carried my bags along a passage and up a great staircase. At the end there was a room where a warm fire was burning and a table was set for supper. Next to it was a bedroom with another fire.

"You will need to wash and change after your journey," he said. "When you are ready, come into the other room. Your supper is waiting."

The light, the warmth and the Count's kind welcome made me forget my fears. I realised I was very hungry. When I came back, the Count was standing by the fire. He pointed to the table.

"Please sit and eat as you like. I hope you will excuse me if I don't join you. I have already eaten, and I never have supper."

I gave him a letter from Mr Hawkins. In it, Mr Hawkins explained that he was ill and could not travel, and that he had complete trust in me. The Count read it and smiled.

The supper was excellent: roast chicken, cheese, salad and an old wine. While I ate, the Count asked me many questions about my journey. After the meal, I sat by the fire and looked at him carefully.

His face was strong — very strong. He had a thin nose with a high bridge and unusual arched nostrils. His forehead was high, and his hair was thin around his head but thick elsewhere. His eyebrows were very large and almost met over his nose. His mouth, under the heavy moustache, looked cruel, and his teeth were sharp and white. They came out over his lips, which were very red for a man of his age. His ears were pale and pointed at the top. His face was extremely white.

I had noticed his hands in the firelight. They seemed white and fine. But now that I saw them close to me, they looked rough and wide, with short thick fingers. Strangely, there was hair in the middle of his palms. His nails were long and fine, cut to a sharp point. When he leaned towards me and his hands touched me, I could not help shaking. His breath was terrible, and I felt sick. The Count noticed this and moved back. He smiled, showing his sharp teeth again, and sat on the other side of the fire.

We were silent for a while. Outside, I heard wolves howling in the valley below. The Count's eyes shone.

"Listen to them," he said. "The children of the night. What music they make!" He must have seen a strange look on my face, because he added, "Ah, sir, you city people cannot understand the feelings of the hunter." Then he stood up. "But you must be tired. Your bedroom is ready. Tomorrow you may sleep as late as you wish. I have to be away until the afternoon. Sleep well and dream well!"

*7 May.* It is early morning again. I have rested and enjoyed the last twenty-four hours. Yesterday I slept late and woke up by myself. When I was dressed, I found breakfast ready in the room where we had eaten supper. The coffee was being kept hot by the fire. There was a card on the table:

> *I have to be away for a while. Do not wait for me. — D.*

I ate a good breakfast. Afterwards I looked for a bell to call a servant, but there was no bell anywhere in the house. This is strange, because the Count is obviously very rich. The table was gold, and the furniture was covered in the most beautiful and expensive old materials. But there are no mirrors anywhere — not even in my room. I had to use the small mirror from my bag to shave. And I have not seen a single servant, or heard any sound near the castle except the howling of wolves.

I found a library full of English books, magazines and newspapers. There were books on history, geography, politics, economics and law — all about England. There were even the London Directory, the Army and Navy lists, and the Law List.

While I was looking at the books, the Count came in. "I am glad you found your way here," he said. "These friends" — he put his hand on some of the books — "have been good friends to me. For many years they have given me many, many hours of pleasure. Through them I have learned about your great England, and I love it. I want to walk through the crowded streets of mighty London, and be part of its life, its change, its death. But I only know your language through books. I hope you will help me to speak it better."

"But, Count," I said, "you speak English perfectly!"

He bowed seriously. "Thank you, my friend, but I am afraid I am still a long way from perfect. Here, I am a nobleman. Everybody knows me, and I am a master. But a stranger in a strange land is nobody. People do not know him. I want to be like everyone else, so that no one stops when they see me or hear my words and says, 'Ha! A stranger!' I have been a master for so long that I want to stay a master — or at least, nobody must be master of me."

Then he told me that I could go anywhere in the castle that I wanted — except into rooms where the doors were locked. "There is a reason that all things are as they are," he said. "If you saw with my eyes and knew with my knowledge, perhaps you would understand better."

I asked him about some of the strange things that had happened on my journey — for example, the blue flames. He explained that, according to a local belief, on one night of the year — the night I had travelled — evil spirits have complete power. On that night, a blue flame shows the place where treasure has been hidden. "Gold," he said, "hidden by peasants and soldiers during the wars. This land has been fought over for centuries, by Romans, Hungarians, Turks and others."

Then we talked about the house in London. I had brought photographs and plans. The Count asked me about every detail of the place and its neighbourhood. He knew much more than I expected.

The house is called Carfax. It is in Purfleet, east of London. It is an old house with high walls around it, in about twenty acres of land, with many trees. Near it there is an old chapel, and there are only a few other houses nearby. One of them is a large private hospital for the mentally ill.

"I am glad that it is old and big," said the Count. "I come from an old family, and to live in a new house would kill me. And I am glad there is a chapel. We Transylvanian nobles do not like to think that our bones might lie among ordinary dead people. I don't need light and happiness. I am no longer young, and my heart is tired after many years of mourning the dead. I love the shadows, and I want to be alone with my thoughts, when I can."

He signed the papers, and then he left me. When he came back later, it was evening. We talked late into the night. Suddenly, a cock crowed outside, and the Count jumped up.

"Why, it is morning again! How bad of me to keep you awake so long. You must make our conversation about your dear England less interesting, so that I do not forget how time passes." With a polite bow, he quickly left me.

I went to my room and opened the curtains. I could see very little; my window looks over the courtyard. I opened the curtains on the other side and saw the dawn. There is something about this place that makes me uneasy. I wish I were safe at home, or that I had never come here.

## Chapter 3 — A Prisoner

**Jonathan Harker's Journal (continued)**

*8 May.* When I started writing this journal, I worried that I was writing too much. Now I am glad that I wrote everything, because there is something so strange about this place that I am afraid.

This morning I hung my little mirror by the window and began to shave. Suddenly I felt a hand on my shoulder and heard the Count's voice: "Good morning." I was surprised, because I had not seen him in the mirror — and the mirror showed the whole room behind me. I cut myself a little, but I did not notice it at first. I answered him and turned back to the mirror. There was no mistake: he was standing right behind me, I could see him over my shoulder, but there was no **reflection** of him in the mirror! The whole room was there, and I was there, but not the Count.

Then I saw that the cut was bleeding a little, and a drop of blood was running down my chin. I put down the razor and turned to find something to stop the blood. When the Count saw my face, his eyes burned with a kind of wild madness. He suddenly tried to grab my throat. I moved back, and his hand touched the string of beads which held the crucifix. Immediately he changed. The anger disappeared so fast that I could hardly believe it had been there.

"Be careful," he said. "Be careful how you cut yourself. It is more dangerous than you think in this country." Then he took the mirror. "And this is the terrible thing that has done the damage. It is a foul object of man's vanity. Away with it!" He opened the heavy window and threw the mirror out. It broke into a thousand pieces on the stones of the courtyard far below. Then he left without a word. It is very annoying, because now I don't know how I will shave.

When I went into the dining room, breakfast was ready, but the Count was not there. It is strange, but I have never seen him eat or drink.

After breakfast I explored the castle. I found a room with a window that looked to the south, and the view was magnificent. The castle stands on the edge of a terrible cliff. A stone falling from the window would fall a thousand feet without touching anything! As far as I could see, there was a sea of green treetops, with deep valleys and silver rivers.

But I did not stay long. I continued to explore, and I found doors, doors, doors everywhere. All of them were locked. Except for the windows in the castle walls, there is no way out.

The castle is a prison, and I am a prisoner!

*Later.* When I understood this, I ran up and down the stairs like a rat in a trap, trying every door and looking out of every window. After a while I stopped, because I felt helpless. I sat down and thought carefully. I am still thinking, and I have not yet decided what to do. I know one thing: I must not tell the Count what I know. He is the one who has made me a prisoner, and he would only lie to me. I must keep my eyes open.

I had just reached this conclusion when I heard the great door below close. The Count had come back. He did not come immediately to the library, so I went quietly to my room. There I found him making my bed! This was strange, but it confirmed what I had thought: there are no servants in the house. Later, I saw him through a crack in the door laying the table in the dining room. And if he does all the work himself, then it was the Count who drove the carriage that brought me here! It is a terrible thought. If that is true, what does it mean that he could control the wolves just by raising his hand?

And why did the people at Bistritz and on the coach fear for me so much? Why did they give me the crucifix, the garlic, the wild rose? Bless that good woman who hung the crucifix around my neck! It gives me comfort and strength every time I touch it.

*Midnight.* I have had a long talk with the Count. I asked him about the history of Transylvania, and he spoke about it with great passion. He spoke as if he had been there for all of it. He talked about the battles and the great families, and he always said "we" and "us", like a king speaking of his people.

"We Székelys have the right to be proud," he said. "In our veins flows the blood of many brave races who fought like lions. When the Hungarians came, they found us here. When the Turks came from the south, we drove them back. Who more gladly than we fought the Turk? Was it not this Dracula who crossed the Danube and beat the Turk on his own ground? Ah, young sir, the Székelys — and the Dracula as their heart's blood, their brain and their sword — can boast of a history that new families like the Habsburgs and the Romanoffs can never have. The days of war are over. Blood is too precious a thing in these days of dishonourable peace. The glories of the great races are like a story that is told."

It was nearly morning, and we went to bed. (This diary seems as strange as the *Arabian Nights*, because everything has to stop when the cock crows.)

*12 May.* Last night the Count asked me many questions about English law. Can a man have two lawyers? Could he arrange to send goods by ship to different places in England with different lawyers? I explained everything.

Then he asked me, "Have you written since your first letter to our friend Mr Peter Hawkins, or to any other?" I said that I had not, because I had not had a chance to send any letters.

"Then write now, my young friend," he said, putting a heavy hand on my shoulder. "Write to our friend and to any other. And say, if it will please you, that you will stay with me until a month from now."

"Do you want me to stay so long?" I asked, and my heart grew cold.

"I want it very much. Indeed I will take no refusal."

What could I do? I was working for Mr Hawkins, not myself, and the Count was his client. I agreed. While he spoke, there was something in his eyes that reminded me I was a prisoner, and that even if I wanted to, I had no choice.

He gave me paper and envelopes and said, "I ask you, my good young friend, to speak only of business in your letters. It will please your friends to know that you are well." I understood that he would read everything. So I decided to write only short formal letters now, and to write secretly to Mina later, in shorthand, which the Count cannot read.

When I had written my two letters, the Count left the room. I looked at the letters on the table that he had written. One was addressed to Samuel F. Billington, of Whitby; another to Herr Leutner, in Varna; the third to Coutts & Co., London; and the fourth to Herren Klopstock & Billreuth, bankers, in Budapest.

When the Count returned, he said, "I trust you will forgive me, but I have much work to do in private this evening. I have a warning for you, my dear young friend. If you leave these rooms, do not, under any circumstances, go to sleep in any other part of the castle. It is old and has many memories, and there are bad dreams for those who sleep unwisely. If you feel sleepy, then hurry to your own bedroom, or to these rooms, and you will be safe."

I understand his warning. But no dream could be more terrible than the dark mystery that is closing around me like a net.

*Later.* I have hung the crucifix over my bed, and I imagine that I will sleep more peacefully because of it. And there it will remain.

When he left me, I went to my room. After a while, I went to look out of the south window. I saw something moving on the castle wall below me. I drew back a little and watched carefully. It was the Count's head coming out of a window. I could not see his face, but I knew him by his neck and the movement of his back and arms. Then, to my horror, I saw the whole man slowly come out of the window and begin to crawl down the castle wall, face down, over that terrible drop. His cloak spread out around him like great wings. At first I could not believe my eyes. But it was true. His fingers and toes held on to the corners of the stones, and he moved downwards with great speed, just as a lizard moves along a wall.

What kind of man is this, or what kind of creature in the shape of a man? I feel the terror of this horrible place overpowering me. I am afraid — terribly afraid — and there is no escape for me.

*15 May.* Once again I saw the Count go out in his lizard way. He moved down and to the left, about a hundred feet, and disappeared into a hole or window. When I knew he had gone, I decided to explore more of the castle. I took a lamp and tried all the doors. They were locked, as I expected. But at last I found one door at the top of a stairway which, though it seemed locked, gave a little when I pushed it hard. I pushed again and was able to open it.

I was now in a wing of the castle further to the right and one floor lower. From the windows I could see that the rooms were along the south side of the castle, above the cliff. This was where the ladies had lived in the old days. The furniture was old and covered in dust. The moonlight came in through the windows. Here I felt more comfortable than in the Count's rooms. I sat at a little oak table and wrote in my diary. It is the nineteenth century, but here the old centuries seem to have powers of their own which modern ideas cannot kill.

*Later: the morning of 16 May.* God help me to keep my mind, because I am afraid I am going mad.

After writing, I felt sleepy. I remembered the Count's warning, but I decided to disobey it. The sleep came over me with all the strength of my tiredness. I lay on a sofa in a corner of the room, and I think I slept.

I was not alone. The room was the same, unchanged in any way. But in the moonlight there were three young women. They were ladies, by their dress and manner. I thought I was dreaming at first, because although the moonlight was behind them, they threw no shadow on the floor. They came close to me and looked at me, and then whispered together. Two were dark, with high noses like the Count and great dark eyes that seemed almost red. The other was fair, with masses of golden hair and eyes like pale sapphires. I seemed to know her face, but I could not remember where I had seen it. All three had brilliant white teeth that shone like pearls against their red lips.

There was something about them that made me uneasy, but also something that I desired. I felt a wicked, burning wish that they would kiss me with those red lips. They whispered together and laughed — a silvery, musical laugh, but hard, like the sound of glass.

"Go on!" said one of the dark ones to the fair one. "You are first, and we shall follow. Yours is the right to begin."

"He is young and strong," said the other. "There are kisses for us all."

I lay quiet, looking out from under my eyelashes, excited and afraid at the same time. The fair girl came closer and bent over me until I could feel her breath on me. It was sweet, like honey, but behind the sweetness there was a bitter, unpleasant smell, like the smell of blood.

She went down on her knees and bent over me. I could see her lick her lips like an animal. Lower and lower went her head. I felt the soft, shivering touch of her lips on the skin of my throat, and the hard points of two sharp teeth, just touching and waiting. I closed my eyes in a strange, sleepy pleasure and waited — waited with a beating heart.

But at that moment, another feeling swept through me as quick as lightning. I felt that the Count was in the room, and that he was in a terrible rage. I opened my eyes and saw his strong hand grab the fair woman's neck and pull her away with the power of a giant. Her blue eyes changed with fury, her white teeth bit with rage, and her face turned red with passion. But the Count! I never imagined such anger, even in the devils of hell. His eyes burned. He threw the woman away and pointed at the others, as he had done with the wolves.

"How dare you touch him, any of you?" he said in a low voice that seemed to cut through the air. "How dare you look at him when I had forbidden it? Back, I tell you all! This man belongs to me!"

The fair girl laughed and turned to answer him. "You yourself never loved. You never love!" The other women joined in her cruel laughter.

The Count looked at my face carefully and said softly, "Yes, I too can love. You yourselves can tell it from the past. Is it not so? Well, now I promise you that when I am done with him, you shall kiss him as you wish. Now go! Go! I must wake him, for there is work to be done."

"Are we to have nothing tonight?" asked one of them, pointing to a bag which he had thrown on the floor. The bag moved as if there were something alive inside it. He nodded. One of the women jumped forward and opened it. If my ears did not deceive me, I heard a gasp and a low cry, like that of a half-smothered child. The women closed around it, while I was frozen with horror. Then they disappeared. They seemed to pass through the window and fade into the moonlight like dust. Then the horror overcame me, and I lost consciousness.

## Chapter 4 — The Boxes of Earth

**Jonathan Harker's Journal (continued)**

I woke up in my own bed. If I had not dreamed it all, the Count must have carried me there. I tried to find proof. My clothes were folded on the chair, but not in my usual way. My watch had not been wound, and I always wind it before I go to bed. But these things prove nothing. Perhaps I was simply very upset. One thing makes me happy: if the Count carried me here and undressed me, he must have been in a hurry, because my journal was still in my pocket. He would have taken it — or destroyed it — if he had found it.

I look around this room, which was so full of fear for me, and now it seems like a safe place. Nothing could be more terrible than those awful women who were waiting to suck my blood.

*18 May.* I went down to look at that room again in daylight. The door at the top of the stairs was closed. It had been closed so hard that the wood was broken. The lock had not been turned, but the door was fastened from the inside. I am afraid it was not a dream, and I must act on that belief.

*19 May.* I am certainly in a trap. Last night the Count asked me, in his most polite voice, to write three letters. One said that my work here was nearly done and that I would start for home in a few days. The second said that I was leaving the next morning. The third said that I had left the castle and arrived at Bistritz. I wanted to refuse, but I knew that it would be madness to argue with the Count while I was in his power. He would become suspicious and angry.

He explained that the post was slow and unreliable here, and that my friends would not worry if they received the letters at the right time. He would keep them at Bistritz and send them later. I asked him what dates I should write.

He thought for a moment. "The first should be June 12, the second June 19, and the third June 29."

Now I know how long I have to live. God help me!

*28 May.* There is a chance of escape — or at least of sending a message home. A band of **Szgany** (gypsies) have come to the castle and are camping in the courtyard. They are like the gypsies all over the world, but they are attached to the great nobles and call themselves by their names. They are fearless and have no religion except superstition.

I wrote two letters: one to Mr Hawkins, and one to Mina. To Mina I wrote in shorthand, and I explained my situation, but not all the horrors, because they would frighten her too much. I threw the letters and a gold coin through the bars of my window to one of the gypsies, and made signs for him to post them. He took them, put them in his hat and bowed. I could do no more.

I went back to the library and began to read. The Count came in. He sat down beside me and said in a very soft voice, opening two letters: "The Szgany have given me these. Although I don't know where they came from, I will look after them. See!" He looked at the first one. "One is from you, to my friend Peter Hawkins. The other —" He saw the strange symbols of my shorthand, and his face grew dark and his eyes burned with anger. "The other is a terrible thing, an insult to friendship and hospitality! It is not signed. Well, so it cannot matter to us." And he calmly held the letter and envelope in the flame of the lamp until they were burned.

Then he said, "The letter to Hawkins I will, of course, send, since it is yours. Your letters are sacred to me. Excuse me, my friend, that I opened it without knowing." He gave me a new envelope, and I wrote the address again and gave it to him in silence. When he went out of the room, I heard the key turn softly. A minute later I tried the door, and it was locked.

*31 May.* This morning when I woke, I decided to take some paper and envelopes from my bag to keep in my pocket. But they had all gone! And also all my notes, my railway timetables, my letter of credit — in fact, everything that might be useful to me outside the castle. I sat and thought for a while. Then I looked in the cupboard where I had put my clothes. My suit, my coat and my blanket had gone too. I could find no sign of them anywhere. This seemed like a new plan of evil.

*17 June.* This morning I heard the crack of whips and the sound of horses' feet on the rocky path outside the courtyard. With joy I hurried to the window and saw two great wagons drive in, each pulled by eight strong horses. At the head of each pair was a Slovak, with his wide hat and big belt. I ran to the door, hoping to get down to them through the main hall. But again my door was locked from the outside.

Then I ran to the window and shouted to them. They looked up at me stupidly and pointed. Then the leader of the gypsies came out, and seeing them pointing at my window, said something, and they laughed. After that, nothing I could say, no cry or desperate request, would make them even look at me.

The wagons contained great square boxes with handles of thick rope. These were obviously empty, because the Slovaks carried them so easily. When they had all been unloaded and placed in a great heap in one corner of the yard, the gypsies gave the Slovaks some money. The Slovaks spat on it for luck, and each one went back to his horses. Shortly afterwards, I heard the crack of their whips die away in the distance.

*24 June, before morning.* Last night the Count left me early and locked himself in his own room. As soon as I dared, I ran up the winding stair and looked out of the window which opened to the south. I wanted to watch for the Count, because something is going on. The gypsies are working somewhere in the castle. I know it, because now and then I hear the far-away sound of digging.

I had been at the window less than half an hour when I saw something coming out of the Count's window. I drew back and watched carefully, and saw the whole man come out. It was a new shock to me to see that he was wearing my suit of clothes, which I had worn when I travelled here. And over his shoulder he carried the terrible bag which I had seen the women take away.

Now I understood his evil plan. He wants people to see me — or rather, to think that they see me — in the towns and villages, posting my own letters. And if he commits any crime, the local people will believe that I did it.

It makes me furious to think that this can continue while I am shut up here, a real prisoner, without the protection of the law.

I decided to watch for the Count's return, and sat for a long time at the window. Then I noticed that there were some small specks floating in the moonlight. They were like tiny grains of dust, and they turned around and gathered in clusters. I watched them with a feeling of calm, and leaned back in the window. Then something made me jump. I heard the low, sad howling of dogs somewhere far below in the valley. The floating dust seemed to take new shapes as it danced in the moonlight. I felt myself struggling to wake to some call of my instincts. Then I understood: the dust was slowly taking the shape of those three ghostly women. I ran, screaming, to my own room, where there was no moonlight and the lamp was burning brightly.

A few hours later I heard something moving in the Count's room, like a sharp cry quickly stopped. Then there was silence, deep, awful silence, which made me cold. With a beating heart, I tried the door, but I was locked in my prison and could do nothing.

Then I heard in the courtyard the cry of a woman in agony. I ran to the window and looked out. There, indeed, was a woman with long, wild hair, holding her hands over her heart. She was leaning against a corner of the gateway. When she saw my face at the window, she threw herself forward and shouted in a voice full of hatred:

"Monster, give me my child!"

She threw herself on her knees and raised her hands, crying the same words again and again. Then she beat her head against the ground.

At last, high above me, probably on the tower, I heard the voice of the Count calling in his harsh, metallic whisper. His call seemed to be answered from far and wide by the howling of wolves. Before many minutes had passed, a pack of them poured, like water out of a broken dam, through the wide entrance into the courtyard.

There was no cry from the woman, and the howling of the wolves was short. Before long they went away, licking their lips.

I could not feel sorry for her, because now I knew what had happened to her child, and she was better dead.

What shall I do? What can I do? How can I escape from this terrible thing of night, gloom and fear?

*25 June, morning.* Nobody knows how sweet the morning can be until he has suffered through a night like that. When the sun rose, my fear disappeared like snow in the sun. I must act now, while I have the courage of the day.

I must not wait. He always comes to me at night, and he has never been seen during the day. Can it be that he sleeps when others wake, so that he may be awake while they sleep? If only I could get into his room! But there is no possible way. The door is always locked.

Yes, there is a way, if one dares to take it. Where his body has gone, why may not another body go? I have seen him crawl from his window. Why should I not imitate him, and go in by his window? The chances are desperate, but my need is more desperate still. At the worst, it can only be death.

*Same day, later.* I have made the effort, and God helped me. I have come safely back to this room. I took off my boots, climbed out of the window and moved along the wall, using the stones as a ladder. I did not look down. I reached the Count's window, which was open, and climbed in.

The room was empty. There was strange old furniture, all covered with dust. In one corner there was a great heap of gold — Roman, British, Austrian, Hungarian, Greek and Turkish money, all at least three hundred years old. There were also chains and jewels, all old and stained.

At one corner of the room there was a heavy door. I tried it, and it opened. It led to a stone passage, and then to a circular stairway which went steeply down. I went down the stairs carefully, because they were dark. At the bottom there was a dark tunnel, and from it came a deathly, sick smell — the smell of old earth newly turned. As I went through the passage, the smell grew stronger. At last I pulled open a heavy door and found myself in an old ruined chapel, which had obviously been used as a graveyard. The roof was broken, and in two places there were steps leading to vaults.

The ground had recently been dug over, and the earth had been put into the great wooden boxes, the ones brought by the Slovaks. There were fifty of them. There was nobody there. I went down into the vaults, though it filled my soul with horror. In the third one I made a discovery.

There, in one of the great boxes, on a pile of newly dug earth, lay the Count! He was either dead or asleep — I could not say which, because his eyes were open and stony, but without the glassiness of death. His cheeks had the warmth of life through all their paleness, and his lips were as red as ever. But there was no sign of movement, no pulse, no breath, no beating of the heart. I bent over him and tried to find any sign of life, but in vain. Then I saw his eyes, and in them, though they were dead, such a look of hate that I ran from the place. I went back to the Count's room, climbed out of the window and crawled back up the castle wall. When I reached my room, I threw myself on the bed and tried to think.

*29 June.* Today is the date of my last letter. The Count has taken steps to prove that it was real, because again I saw him leave the castle by the same window, and in my clothes. As he went down the wall, like a lizard, I wished I had a gun or some deadly weapon, so that I might destroy him. But I fear that no weapon made by man would have any effect on him.

When I woke up in the evening, the Count was there. He looked at me as grimly as a man can look, and said:

"Tomorrow, my friend, we must part. You return to your beautiful England, I to some work which may have such an end that we may never meet. Your letter home has been sent. Tomorrow I shall not be here, but all shall be ready for your journey. In the morning come the Szgany, who have some work of their own here, and also come some Slovaks. When they have gone, my carriage shall come for you, and shall take you to the Borgo Pass to meet the coach from Bukovina to Bistritz."

I did not believe him, so I decided to test him. "Why may I not go tonight?"

"Because, dear sir, my coachman and horses are away on a mission."

"But I would walk with pleasure. I want to get away at once."

He smiled, such a soft, smooth, devilish smile that I knew there was some trick behind it. He said, "Come with me, my dear young friend. Not an hour shall you wait in my house against your will. Come!" With great dignity, he took the lamp and went before me down the stairs and along the hall. Suddenly he stopped.

"Listen!"

Close at hand came the howling of many wolves. It was almost as if the sound came when he raised his hand, just as the music of a great orchestra seems to begin at the movement of the conductor's stick. After a moment he went on to the door, pulled back the heavy bolts, removed the great chains and began to open it.

To my great surprise, I saw that it was unlocked. As the door began to open, the howling of the wolves outside grew louder and angrier. Their red jaws and claws came through the opening of the door. I understood then that to struggle at the moment against the Count was useless. With such allies as these under his command, I could do nothing.

"Shut the door!" I shouted. "I shall wait till morning." And I covered my face with my hands to hide my tears of bitter disappointment. With one movement of his powerful arm, the Count threw the door shut, and the great bolts clanged as they shot back into their places.

In silence we returned to the library, and after a minute or two I went to my own room. The last I saw of Count Dracula was his kissing his hand to me, with a red light of triumph in his eyes.

When I was in my room and about to lie down, I thought I heard a whispering at my door. I went to it softly and listened. Unless my ears deceived me, I heard the voice of the Count:

"Back! Back to your own place! Your time is not yet come. Wait! Have patience! Tonight is mine. Tomorrow night is yours!"

There was a low, sweet ripple of laughter. In rage, I threw open the door and saw outside the three terrible women licking their lips. As I appeared, they all joined in a horrible laugh and ran away.

I went back to my room and threw myself on my knees. Is it then so near the end? Tomorrow! Tomorrow! Lord, help me, and those to whom I am dear!

*30 June, morning.* These may be the last words I ever write in this diary. I slept until just before dawn. When I woke, I threw myself on my knees, because I decided that if Death came, he should find me ready. At last I felt the change in the air and knew that the morning had come. Then came the welcome cock-crow, and I felt that I was safe. With a glad heart, I opened my door and ran down to the hall. I had seen that the door was unlocked, and now escape was before me. With hands that trembled with eagerness, I unhooked the chains and drew back the heavy bolts.

But the door would not move. Despair seized me. I pulled and pulled at the door and shook it until, heavy as it was, it rattled in its frame. I could see the bolt shot. It had been locked after I left the Count.

Then a wild desire took me to get the key at any risk, and I decided then and there to climb the wall again and get into the Count's room. He might kill me, but death now seemed the happier choice. Without a pause I rushed up to the east window and climbed down the wall, as before, into the Count's room. It was empty, but that was as I expected. I could not see a key anywhere, but the heap of gold remained. I went through the door in the corner and down the winding stair and along the dark passage to the old chapel. I knew now well enough where to find the monster I was looking for.

The great box was in the same place, close against the wall, but the lid was laid on it, not fastened down, but with the nails ready in their places to be hammered in. I knew I must reach the body for the key, so I raised the lid and laid it back against the wall. And then I saw something which filled my very soul with horror.

There lay the Count, but he looked younger. His white hair and moustache were now dark grey. His face was fuller, and his skin was pink under the whiteness. His mouth was redder than ever, because there were drops of fresh blood on his lips. The blood ran from the corners of his mouth, down his chin and onto his neck. The whole horrible creature seemed full of blood. He lay there like a fat **leech**, tired after his meal.

I shook as I bent over to touch him. Everything in me hated the contact. But I had to search him, or I was lost. The next night, those three horrible women might drink my blood. I searched the whole body, but I could not find the key. Then I looked at the Count's face. There was a smile on it, and that smile almost drove me mad. This was the creature I was helping to move to London! There, among millions of people, he could drink blood for centuries, and make more and more creatures like himself.

I felt a terrible wish to free the world from this monster. I had no weapon, but there was a **shovel** that the workmen had used to fill the boxes. I lifted it high and hit down at the hateful face with its sharp edge. But as I did it, the head turned, and the eyes looked straight at me. Their terrible look seemed to freeze me. The shovel turned in my hand and only made a deep cut above his forehead. Then it fell from my hand. As I pulled it away, it caught the lid of the box, and the lid fell back over the monster. The last thing I saw was his swollen face, covered in blood, with an evil smile on it.

I tried to think what to do next, but my head was burning. Then, far away, I heard happy voices singing a gypsy song, and the sound of heavy wheels and whips. The gypsies and the Slovaks were coming. I took a last look at the box and ran back up to the Count's room. I planned to run out of the castle the moment the great door was opened.

I heard a key in the great lock below, and the heavy door opened. I turned to run down to the chapel, hoping to find another way out there. But at that moment a strong wind seemed to blow, and the door to the stairs shut with a bang. When I tried to open it, it was locked. I was a prisoner again.

As I write, I can hear many feet in the passage below, and the sound of heavy things being put down. These must be the boxes of earth. Now I hear hammering. They are nailing down the box with the Count in it. Then I hear heavy feet in the hall again.

The great door shuts, and the chains rattle. A key turns in the lock. Then another door opens and shuts.

Listen! In the courtyard and down the rocky road, I hear the heavy wheels, the whips and the singing of the gypsies as they go away into the distance.

I am alone in the castle with those horrible women. Mina is a woman too, but she has nothing in common with them. They are devils from hell!

I will not stay alone with them. I will try to climb down the castle wall, further than I have ever tried before. I will take some of the gold with me, in case I need it later. Perhaps I can find a way out of this terrible place.

And then home! Home on the quickest train! Away from this evil place and this evil land, where the devil and his children still walk on the earth!

God's mercy is better than the mercy of these monsters. The cliff is steep and high. If I fall, at least I will die as a man. Goodbye, everyone! Mina!

## Chapter 5 — Three Proposals

**Letter from Miss Mina Murray to Miss Lucy Westenra**

*9 May.*

My dearest Lucy,

I am sorry I have not written for so long, but I have been very busy with my work at the school. I am longing to be with you by the sea, where we can talk freely and build our castles in the air.

I have been working very hard, because I want to help Jonathan with his work. I am practising **shorthand** very carefully. When we are married, I will be able to write down what he says and type it for him. He and I sometimes write letters to each other in shorthand. He is keeping a shorthand journal of his travels abroad. When I am with you, I will keep a diary in the same way. It will not be a diary of the usual kind, with two pages for each week and Sunday squeezed into a corner. It will be a kind of journal that I can write in whenever I want.

I have just had a few hurried lines from Jonathan in Transylvania. He is well and will return in about a week. I am longing to hear all his news. It must be nice to see strange countries. I wonder if we — I mean Jonathan and I — will ever see them together.

There is the ten o'clock bell. Goodbye.

Your loving, Mina

Tell me all the news when you write. You have not told me anything for a long time. I hear rumours, especially about a tall, handsome, curly-haired man???

**Letter from Lucy Westenra to Mina Murray**

*Wednesday.*

My dearest Mina,

You are unfair to say I am a bad correspondent! I have written to you twice since we last met, and your last letter was only your second.

There is nothing to tell you. Really, there is nothing that would interest you. The town is very pleasant just now. We go a lot to picture galleries and for walks and rides in the park.

As to the tall, curly-haired man, I suppose it was the one who was with me at the last concert. Someone has obviously been telling stories. That was Mr Holmwood. He often comes to see us, and he and Mamma get on very well together. They have so many things to talk about in common.

We met a man some time ago who would be *just right for you*, if you were not already engaged to Jonathan. He is an excellent man, handsome, rich and of good family. He is a doctor and really clever. Just imagine! He is only twenty-nine, and he is in charge of an enormous **lunatic asylum**. Mr Holmwood introduced him to me. He called here to see us, and he often comes now. I think he is one of the most decided men I ever saw, and yet the calmest. He seems absolutely unmovable. I can imagine what a wonderful power he must have over his patients. He has a strange habit of looking you straight in the face, as if trying to read your thoughts. He tries this on me very much, but I like to think he has a difficult job.

Mina, we have told each other all our secrets since we were children. We have slept together and eaten together, and laughed and cried together. And now, though I have spoken, I would like to speak more. Oh, Mina, can't you guess? I love him. I am blushing as I write, because although I think he loves me, he has not told me so in words. But oh, Mina, I love him. I love him! There, that does me good.

I wish I were with you, dear, sitting by the fire undressing, as we used to sit, and I would try to tell you what I feel. I don't know how I am writing this even to you. I am afraid to stop, or I would tear up the letter, and I don't want to stop, because I do so want to tell you all. Let me hear from you *at once*, and tell me all that you think about it.

Good night. Lucy

P.S. — I need not tell you this is a secret. Good night again. L.

**Letter from Lucy Westenra to Mina Murray**

*24 May.*

My dearest Mina,

Thanks, and thanks, and thanks again for your sweet letter. It was so nice to be able to tell you and to have your sympathy.

My dear, it never rains but it pours. How true the old proverbs are! Here am I, who will be twenty in September, and yet I never had a proposal until today — not a real proposal. And today I had three. Just fancy! THREE proposals in one day! Isn't it awful! I feel sorry, really and truly sorry, for two of the poor fellows. Oh, Mina, I am so happy that I don't know what to do with myself. And three proposals! But, for goodness' sake, don't tell any of the girls, or they would be getting all sorts of silly ideas.

Well, my dear, number one came just before lunch. I told you of him: Dr John Seward, the lunatic-asylum man, with the strong jaw and the good forehead. He was very cool outwardly, but was nervous all the same. He had obviously been practising all sorts of little things, but nearly sat down on his silk hat, which men don't generally do when they are cool. And then, when he wanted to appear at ease, he kept playing with a little knife in a way that made me nearly scream.

He spoke to me, Mina, very straightforwardly. He told me how dear I was to him, though he had known me so little, and what his life would be with me to help and cheer him. He was going to tell me how unhappy he would be if I did not care for him, but when he saw me cry, he said that he was a brute and would not add to my present trouble. Then he stopped and asked if I could love him in time. When I shook my head, his hands trembled. Then, with some hesitation, he asked me if I already cared for anyone else. I felt it was my duty to tell him that there was someone. I only told him that much, and then he stood up, and he looked very strong and very serious as he took both my hands in his and said he hoped I would be happy, and that if I ever wanted a friend, I must count him one of my best.

Oh, Mina dear, I can't help crying. You must excuse this letter being all blotted. Being proposed to is all very nice, but it isn't at all a happy thing when you have to see a poor fellow, whom you know loves you honestly, going away and looking all broken-hearted, and to know that, no matter what he may say at the moment, you are passing quite out of his life.

*Evening.*

Arthur has just gone, and I feel in better spirits than when I left off, so I can go on telling you about the day.

Well, my dear, number two came after lunch. He is such a nice fellow, an American from Texas, and he looks so young and so fresh that it seems almost impossible that he has been to so many places and has had so many adventures. His name is Quincey P. Morris. He tells such wonderful stories, and he uses American slang, which makes me laugh.

He sat down beside me and tried to look cheerful, but I could see that he was nervous. He took my hand and said very sweetly, "Miss Lucy, I know I'm not good enough for you. But if you wait for a man who is good enough, you'll wait for ever. Won't you marry me, and let us go down the long road of life together?"

He looked so good-humoured and so happy that it was much easier to refuse him than poor Dr Seward. So I answered him as lightly as I could, with a little joke, and said that I was not ready to marry anyone.

Then he became serious. He asked me if my heart was free. I told him, through my tears, that there was someone I loved, although that person had not yet told me that he loved me. And then — oh, Mina — he was so brave. He held out his hand and said, "That's my brave girl. Don't cry, my dear. I'm a strong man, and I can take it. If that other fellow doesn't know how lucky he is, he'd better find out soon, or he'll have to deal with me! Little girl, your honesty has made me your friend, and a friend is rarer than a lover. I'm going to have a lonely walk through life now. Won't you give me one kiss?"

I kissed him. He stood up, holding my two hands, and looked down into my face. I was blushing very much. He said, "Little girl, I hold your hand, and you've kissed me. If that doesn't make us friends, nothing will. Thank you for being so honest with me. Goodbye." He took his hat and went straight out of the room without looking back. And I am crying like a baby.

Oh, why must a man like that be made unhappy, when there are lots of girls about who would worship the very ground he walks on? I know I would, if I were free — only I don't want to be free.

My dear, this quite upset me, and I feel I cannot write of happiness at once, after telling you of it. And I don't wish to tell of number three until it can be all happy.

Ever your loving, Lucy

P.S. — Oh, about number three — I needn't tell you of number three, need I? Besides, it was all so confused. It seemed only a moment from his coming into the room till both his arms were round me, and he was kissing me. I am very, very happy, and I don't know what I have done to deserve it. I must only try in the future to show that I am not ungrateful to God for all His goodness to me in sending to me such a lover, such a husband and such a friend.

Goodbye.

**Dr Seward's Diary**

*(Kept on a **phonograph**.)*

*25 May.* I have no appetite today. I cannot eat, I cannot rest, so I am using my diary instead. Since my rejection of yesterday, I have a sort of empty feeling. Nothing in the world seems important enough to be worth doing. As I knew that the only cure for this sort of thing was work, I went down amongst the patients.

I picked out one who has given me a study of much interest. He is so strange that I am determined to understand him as well as I can. Today I seemed to get nearer than ever before to the heart of his mystery.

I asked him more questions than ever before, because I wanted to understand his madness. Now I see that I was a little cruel. I kept him talking about the subject of his madness — something I normally never do with my patients.

*R. M. Renfield, aged 59.* He is a very strong man. Sometimes he is very excited, and sometimes very sad and quiet. I think there is one fixed idea in his mind, but I cannot discover what it is. I believe he could be dangerous. I must watch him carefully.

## Chapter 6 — Whitby

**Mina Murray's Journal**

*24 July. Whitby.* Lucy met me at the station. She looked sweeter and lovelier than ever. We drove up to the house at the Crescent where she and her mother are staying. This is a lovely place. The little river, the Esk, runs through a deep valley and becomes wider near the harbour. The houses of the old town have red roofs and seem piled one above the other.

On the top of a hill above the town there are the ruins of Whitby Abbey. It is a very beautiful ruin, large and romantic. There is a legend that a white lady is seen in one of the windows. Between the abbey and the town there is another church, the parish church, with a big graveyard full of tombstones. In my opinion, this is the nicest spot in Whitby, because it is right over the town and you can see the harbour and the bay. There are seats beside the paths, and people go and sit there all day long, looking at the beautiful view.

I am sitting here now, with my book on my knee, listening to the talk of three old men who are sitting beside me. They seem to do nothing all day but sit here and talk.

One of them is a very old man. His face is brown and wrinkled, like the bark of a tree. He told me that he is nearly a hundred, and that he was a sailor in the Greenland fishing fleet many years ago. His name is Mr Swales. I am afraid he is a very sceptical person. When I asked him about the white lady, he said it was all nonsense — "bans and wafts and boh-ghosts". He believes none of the old stories.

*1 August.* I came up here an hour ago with Lucy, and we had a very interesting talk with my old friend. He laughed at the tombstones.

"Look at them," he said. "Half of them are lies! They say *'Here lies the body of'* such-and-such a sailor, but the man was lost at sea, and his body was never found. The graves are empty. How will they all come to the Last Judgement? Will they come out of the sea, carrying their stones with them, to prove how good they were?"

Lucy pointed to the seat where we were sitting and asked whose grave it was. Mr Swales told us that it was the grave of a young man who had killed himself. His mother hated him, and he hated her. He shot himself so that she would not get his money. "And you're sitting on him now," said the old man. Lucy was upset, and we changed the subject.

I am anxious about Jonathan. I have had no letter from him for a long time. I wish I knew where he is.

**Dr Seward's Diary**

*5 June.* Renfield's case grows more interesting the more I understand him. He has certain qualities very highly developed: selfishness, secrecy and purpose. I wish I could discover what is the purpose. He seems to have some settled plan of his own, but what it is I do not know. He loves animals, but in a very strange way. At present his hobby is catching flies. He has so many now that I had to speak to him about it. To my surprise, he did not become angry. He only asked me for three days to clear them away.

*18 June.* He has now turned his mind to spiders, and has got several very big ones in a box. He feeds them with his flies, and the number of flies is becoming smaller, although he uses half his food to attract more flies from outside.

*1 July.* His spiders are now becoming as much of a nuisance as his flies, and today I told him that he must get rid of them. He looked very sad, so I said that he must get rid of some of them, at least. He agreed. While I was with him, a horrible fly, fat with eating dead things, flew into the room. He caught it, held it between his fingers for a few moments, and then, before I knew what he was going to do, put it in his mouth and ate it.

I told him off for it, but he argued quietly that it was very good and very healthy. It was life, strong life, and it gave life to him. This gave me an idea. I must watch how he gets rid of his spiders.

He has obviously some deep problem in his mind, because he keeps a little notebook in which he is always writing. Whole pages of it are filled with numbers, generally single numbers added up in groups, and then the totals added again, as if he were doing some kind of accounts.

*8 July.* There is a method in his madness. My idea is growing. I left him alone for a few days. Now he has kept only a few spiders, but he has a new pet. He has managed to get a sparrow, and has already partly tamed it. His method of taming is simple: the spiders are getting fewer. Those that remain are well fed, because he still brings in the flies by tempting them with his food.

*19 July.* We are making progress. My friend now has a whole colony of sparrows, and his flies and spiders are almost gone. When I came in, he ran to me and said he wanted to ask me a great favour — a very, very great favour. He asked if he could have a kitten — a nice, little, sleek, playful kitten that he could play with, and teach, and feed, and feed, and feed!

I was not unprepared for this request, because I had noticed how his pets became bigger and more lively. But I did not want his pretty family of sparrows to be eaten in the same way as the flies and spiders. So I said I would think about it. Then I asked him if he would not prefer a cat to a kitten.

He answered eagerly, "Oh yes, I would like a cat! I only asked for a kitten in case you refused me a cat. No one would refuse me a kitten, would they?"

I shook my head and said that at present I was afraid it would not be possible. His face fell, and I saw a quick look of danger in it, a sudden fierce look which meant killing. The man is an undeveloped homicidal maniac. I shall test him with his present craving and see how it will work out. Then I shall know more.

*20 July.* I visited Renfield very early, before the attendant went his rounds. I found him up and humming a tune. He was spreading out his sugar, which he had saved, at the window, and was beginning to catch flies again.

I looked around for his birds, and not seeing them, asked him where they were. He replied, without turning round, that they had all flown away. There were a few feathers about the room, and on his pillow a drop of blood. I said nothing, but went and told the keeper to report to me if there was anything odd about him during the day.

*11 a.m.* The attendant has just been to see me. Renfield has been very sick and has vomited up a whole lot of feathers. "My belief is, doctor," the attendant said, "that he has eaten his birds, and that he just took and ate them raw!"

*11 p.m.* I gave Renfield a strong sleeping drug tonight, enough to make even him sleep, and took away his notebook to look at it. My idea was correct. My homicidal maniac is of a peculiar kind. I shall have to invent a new classification for him. I shall call him a **zoophagous** (life-eating) maniac. What he desires is to absorb as many lives as he can, and he has planned to achieve it in a cumulative way. He gave many flies to one spider and many spiders to one bird, and then wanted a cat to eat the many birds. What would his later steps have been?

**Mina Murray's Journal**

*26 July.* I am anxious, and it calms me to express myself here. It is like whispering to myself and listening at the same time. I have not heard from Jonathan for some time, and I am very worried. But yesterday Mr Hawkins, who is always so kind, sent me a letter from him. It was only a line, dated from Castle Dracula, and said that he was just starting for home. That is not like Jonathan. I do not understand it, and it makes me uneasy.

Then, too, Lucy, although she is so well, has lately taken to her old habit of walking in her sleep. Her mother has spoken to me about it, and we have decided that I am to lock the door of our room every night. Mrs Westenra has an idea that sleepwalkers always go out on roofs of houses and along the edges of cliffs, and then wake suddenly and fall with a despairing cry that echoes all over the place.

Poor dear, she is naturally anxious about Lucy. She tells me that her husband, Lucy's father, had the same habit. Lucy is to be married in the autumn, and she is already planning her dresses and how her house will be arranged. Arthur — Mr Holmwood — is coming up here very soon, as soon as he can leave town, because his father is not very well.

*27 July.* No news from Jonathan. I am getting quite uneasy about him, although why I should I do not know. But I do wish that he would write, if it were only a single line.

Lucy walks more than ever, and each night I am awakened by her moving about the room. Fortunately, the weather is so hot that she cannot get cold.

*6 August.* Another three days, and no news. This suspense is getting dreadful. If I only knew where to write to or where to go to, I should feel easier. But no one has heard a word of Jonathan since that last letter. I must only pray to God for patience.

Today is a grey day. The sea is dark, and fog is coming in from the sea. The horizon is lost in a grey mist. Everything is vastness. The clouds are piled up like giant rocks, and there is a sound over the sea like the sound of a coming disaster.

I met old Mr Swales on the cliff. He came and sat beside me and said, in a very gentle way, "I want to say something to you, miss. I'm afraid I said some things that shocked you, when I talked about the dead. My time is nearly finished. Death is waiting for me, and I'm not afraid of it. I don't mind dying. It may come at any moment." Then he looked out to sea. "I can feel it in the wind," he said. "There's something in that wind that sounds and smells like death. Look! Look!" he cried suddenly. "There's a ship out there, and she's in trouble."

The coastguard came along with his telescope. He told us that the ship looked like a Russian one, and that she was moving very strangely. "It's as if she doesn't know her own mind," he said. "She seems to see the storm coming, but can't decide whether to run up north into the open sea or to come in here. We'll hear more of her before this time tomorrow."

## Chapter 7 — The Ship Without a Crew

**From a newspaper, *The Dailygraph*, 8 August (pasted in Mina Murray's journal)**

*From a correspondent. Whitby.*

One of the greatest and most sudden storms ever seen here has just happened, with strange and unique results.

The weather had been hot and calm. On Saturday evening many people were walking on the cliffs, enjoying the beautiful sunset. But an old fisherman, who has watched the weather from the cliff for more than fifty years, said that a sudden storm was coming. A few people noticed a strange ship with all her sails up, moving towards the west. The coastguard signalled to her to bring down her sails because of the danger, but she did not answer.

At about midnight the storm came. The wind roared like thunder. The waves rose higher and higher, and in a few minutes the calm sea became a monster. White waves beat on the sand and rushed up the cliffs. Some of them broke over the piers. Thick fog came in from the sea — cold, wet clouds that moved like ghosts.

A great searchlight on the East Cliff was turned on the sea. Several fishing boats came racing into the harbour, and each time one arrived safely, the crowd on the shore shouted with joy. Then the light found the strange ship again, with all her sails still up. Everyone understood the danger. Between the ship and the harbour there was a dangerous rocky area where many ships had been lost. It seemed impossible for her to enter the harbour safely.

Then, just as the ship came close, the wind suddenly changed. The fog disappeared, and the ship rushed past the rocks, through the waves and into the harbour. She struck the sand and stone in the corner of the harbour, under the East Cliff.

The searchlight showed a terrible sight. A dead man was tied to the ship's wheel. His head moved horribly from side to side with every movement of the ship. There was nobody else on the deck at all.

At that moment, a great dog jumped from the ship onto the sand. It ran up the steep cliff, towards the old churchyard, and disappeared into the darkness.

The coastguard was the first person on board. He found the dead man's hands tied to the wheel, one on top of the other. Between the hands and the wood was a crucifix, and the beads of the crucifix were around his wrists. The poor man must have tied himself there. The doctor who came later said that he had been dead for about two days.

In the dead man's pocket was a bottle with a rolled piece of paper inside. It was the ship's log.

*9 August.* The ship is a Russian schooner called the *Demeter*, from Varna. She was carrying only a few boxes of sand and silver, and some large wooden boxes filled with earth. These boxes were addressed to a Whitby lawyer, Mr S. F. Billington, of 7 The Crescent. This morning he came on board and took possession of them.

The big dog has not been found. Many people are looking for it. Early this morning a big dog — a **mastiff** belonging to a coal merchant — was found dead in the road near its owner's yard. Its throat had been torn open, as if by a wild animal.

**The Log of the *Demeter***

*Varna to Whitby. (Written on 18 July.)* Things so strange happened that I shall keep an accurate note from now until we land.

*6 July.* We finished taking our cargo — silver sand and boxes of earth. At noon we set sail. Crew: five men, two mates, cook, and myself (captain).

*11 July.* At dawn we entered the Bosphorus. Turkish customs officers came on board. Everything was in order. We left at 4 p.m.

*12 July.* Through the Dardanelles. More customs officers. They were very quick. They wanted us to go away quickly.

*13 July.* Passed Cape Matapan. The crew seemed unhappy about something. They seemed afraid, but would not say why.

*14 July.* I was worried about the crew. They are all steady men who have sailed with me before. The mate could not discover what was wrong. They only told him that there was *something* on board, and they made the sign of the cross.

*16 July.* In the morning the mate reported that one of the crew, Petrofsky, was missing. Nobody could explain it. He had taken the watch from eight to midnight, but did not come to his bed. The men were more upset than ever. They said they expected something like this, but would not say more than that there was *something* on board.

*17 July.* Yesterday one of the men, Olgaren, came to my cabin and told me in a frightened voice that he thought there was a strange man on board. He said that during his watch he had seen a tall, thin man, who was not like any of the crew, come up on deck and walk along it, and then disappear. He followed carefully, but when he got to the front of the ship, he found nobody, and all the doors were closed.

I searched the entire ship with the mate, from one end to the other. We had lamps. There was nobody there. The cargo was only the large wooden boxes, and there were no hiding places where a man could be. The men were relieved when the search was over and went back to work happily.

*24 July.* There seems to be some doom over this ship. Already one man is lost, and as we entered the Bay of Biscay with wild weather ahead, another man disappeared in the night. Like the first, he came off his watch and was not seen again. The men are all in a panic of fear. They have asked to have double watches, because they are afraid to be alone.

*28 July.* Four days in hell, going round and round in a storm. No sleep for anyone. Everyone is very tired. Nobody can steer the ship properly. The second mate came to take the watch and let the others sleep. When the next watch came, only the steersman was there. The second mate had disappeared.

*29 July.* Another tragedy. We had a single watch tonight, because the crew are too tired. When the morning watch came on deck, they found nobody except the steersman. They raised the alarm, and everyone came on deck. A thorough search was made, but nobody was found. Now there are only the mate, two men and myself.

*1 August.* Two days of fog. No sails can be seen. We hoped to see help when we were in the English Channel, but we have not seen anything. The men are beyond fear. They are working steadily and patiently, but they seem to expect the worst.

*2 August, midnight.* I woke from a few minutes' sleep when I heard a cry outside my cabin. I could see nothing in the fog. I rushed onto the deck and ran against the mate. He told me that he had heard the cry and run, but there was no sign of the man on watch. One more gone. Lord help us!

*3 August.* At midnight I went to take the place of the man at the wheel, but there was nobody there. The wind was steady, and the ship was moving straight ahead, so I called the mate. After a few seconds he rushed up on deck in his underwear. He looked wild-eyed and terrible, and I fear his reason has gone. He came close to me and whispered, as if he was afraid that the air might hear: "*It* is here. I know it now. On the watch last night, I saw It, like a man, tall and thin, and as white as a ghost. It was at the front of the ship, looking out. I crept behind It and pushed my knife into It, but the knife went through It, empty as the air."

Then he said, "But It is here, and I'll find It. It is in the hold, perhaps in one of those boxes. I'll open them one by one and see. You work the wheel." And with a warning look, he went below. I could not leave the wheel. I heard him hammering at the boxes below.

Then, suddenly, there came from below a scream that made my blood run cold. Up on the deck he came, as if shot from a gun — a raging madman, with his eyes rolling and his face twisted with fear. "Save me! Save me!" he cried, and then he looked round on the blanket of fog. His horror turned to despair, and he said in a steady voice, "You had better come too, captain, before it is too late. *He* is there! I know the secret now. The sea will save me from Him, and it is all that is left!" Before I could say a word or move forward to seize him, he jumped into the sea.

I suppose I know the secret too, now. It was this madman who had got rid of the men one by one, and now he has followed them himself. God help me! How am I to explain all these horrors when I get to port?

*4 August.* Still fog, which the sunrise cannot pierce. I know there is sunrise because I am a sailor; otherwise I could not know it. I dared not go below. I dared not leave the wheel. So here all night I stayed, and in the dimness of the night I saw It — Him! God forgive me, but the mate was right to jump overboard. It was better to die like a man. But I am captain, and I must not leave my ship. I shall defeat this monster. I shall tie my hands to the wheel when my strength begins to fail, and with them I shall tie that which He — It! — dare not touch. And then, come good wind or foul, I shall save my soul and my honour as a captain. I am growing weaker, and the night is coming on. If He can look me in the face again, I may not have time to act. If we are wrecked, perhaps this bottle may be found, and those who find it may understand. If not — well, then all men shall know that I have been true to my trust. God and the Blessed Virgin and the saints help a poor ignorant soul trying to do his duty.

*(End of the log.)*

Of course, nobody knows what really happened. Many people believe the captain was a hero. There will be a public funeral for him. The big dog still cannot be found.

## Chapter 8 — The Night in the Churchyard

**Mina Murray's Journal**

*10 August.* The funeral of the poor sea captain today was very moving. Every boat in the harbour seemed to be there, and the coffin was carried by captains all the way up to the churchyard. Lucy and I sat on our old seat and watched.

Poor Lucy seemed very upset. She has been restless all the time, and I believe that her dreams at night are troubling her. She will not admit that there is anything wrong.

There is sad news too. Our old friend Mr Swales was found dead this morning on our seat, with his neck broken. The doctor said that he must have fallen back on the seat in some sort of fright, because there was a look of fear and horror on his face. Poor dear old man! Perhaps he had seen Death with his dying eyes!

Lucy is so sweet and sensitive that she feels these things more than other people do. She was very upset by this, and also by a little thing that I did not think about much, although I love animals. A man who often comes up here to look for the boats always brings his dog. Today the dog would not come to its master. It stood howling near our seat. The man became angry and kicked it, and then picked it up and threw it down on the tombstone where the seat is fixed. The poor thing was shaking with fear.

*11 August, 3 a.m.* No sleep now, so I may as well write. I am too upset to sleep. We have had such an adventure, such an agonising experience.

I woke suddenly and sat up, with a horrible sense of fear and emptiness. The room was dark, so I could not see Lucy's bed. I went across and felt for her. The bed was empty. I lit a match and found that she was not in the room. The door was shut, but not locked, as I had left it. Her mother had been ill lately, so I did not want to wake her. I put on some clothes and went to look for Lucy.

As I was leaving the room, I noticed that her clothes were still there. "Thank God," I said to myself. "She cannot be far, because she is only in her nightdress." I ran downstairs and looked in the sitting room. Not there! Then I looked in all the other open rooms of the house, with a growing fear. Finally I came to the hall door and found it open. Lucy must have gone out as she was.

There was no time to think of what might happen. I took a big, heavy shawl and ran out. The clock was striking one as I was in the Crescent, and there was not a soul in sight. I ran along the North Terrace, but could see no sign of the white figure I expected. At the edge of the West Cliff, above the pier, I looked across the harbour to the East Cliff, wondering whether Lucy was in our favourite seat.

There was a bright full moon, with heavy black clouds. For a moment I could see nothing, because a cloud covered the church and the churchyard. Then, as the cloud passed, I saw the ruins of the abbey, and then the church and the churchyard. There, on our favourite seat, the silver light of the moon showed a half-reclining figure, snowy white. The cloud came too quickly for me to see much. But it seemed to me that something dark stood behind the seat where the white figure shone, and bent over it. Whether it was a man or an animal, I could not tell.

I did not wait. I ran down the steep steps to the pier and along by the fish market to the bridge, which was the only way to reach the East Cliff. The town seemed dead. There was not a single person anywhere. I was glad, because I did not want anyone to see poor Lucy. The time and distance seemed endless, and my knees trembled as I climbed the endless steps to the abbey. When I got almost to the top, I could see the seat and the white figure. I was quite near now, and I could see clearly, even through the darkness of the shadows. There was something, long and black, bending over the half-reclining white figure.

I called out in fright, "Lucy! Lucy!" Something raised a head, and from where I was, I could see a white face and red, gleaming eyes.

Lucy did not answer, and I ran on to the entrance of the churchyard. As I entered, the church was between me and the seat, and for a minute or so I lost sight of her. When I came into view again, the cloud had passed, and the moonlight struck so brilliantly that I could see Lucy half reclining with her head lying over the back of the seat. She was quite alone, and there was not a sign of any living thing about.

When I bent over her, I could see that she was still asleep. Her lips were parted, and she was breathing — not softly as usual, but in long, heavy gasps, as if trying to fill her lungs with every breath. As I came close, she put up her hand in her sleep and pulled the collar of her nightdress close around her throat. Whilst she did so, there came a little shiver through her, as though she felt the cold.

I put the warm shawl over her and drew the edges tight around her neck, because I was afraid she would get a deadly cold from the night air, unclad as she was. I fastened the shawl at her throat with a big safety pin. But I must have been clumsy in my anxiety and pinched or pricked her with it, because by and by, when her breathing became quieter, she put her hand to her throat again and moaned.

When I had her carefully wrapped up, I put my shoes on her feet and then began very gently to wake her. At last she woke up. She did not seem surprised to see me, because, of course, she did not realise at once where she was. When she understood that she was in the churchyard at night in her nightdress, she was a little frightened, but she did not cry. She came home with me at once.

Thankfully, we met nobody. When we got home, I washed our feet and said a prayer of thanks together with her. Then I tucked her into bed. Before falling asleep, she begged me not to say a word to anyone, even her mother, about her sleepwalking adventure. I promised.

*Same day, noon.* All goes well. Lucy slept until I woke her, and she seems not to have even changed her side. The adventure of the night does not seem to have harmed her. On the contrary, it has benefited her, because she looks better this morning than she has done for weeks.

But I was sorry to notice that my clumsiness with the safety pin hurt her. Indeed, it might have been serious, because the skin of her throat was pierced. I must have pinched up a piece of loose skin and transfixed it, because there are two little red points like pinpricks, and on the band of her nightdress was a drop of blood. When I apologised and was concerned about it, she laughed and said she did not even feel it. Fortunately, the marks cannot leave a scar, as they are so tiny.

*12 August.* Twice during the night I was woken by Lucy trying to get out. Even in her sleep, she seemed a little impatient at finding the door shut. She went back to bed under a sort of protest.

*13 August.* Another quiet day, and to bed with the key on my wrist as before. Again I woke in the night and found Lucy sitting up in bed, still asleep, pointing to the window. I got up quietly and, pulling aside the curtain, looked out. It was brilliant moonlight. Between me and the moonlight flew a great bat, moving round and round in great circles. Once or twice it came quite close, but it was, I suppose, frightened at seeing me, and flew away across the harbour towards the abbey. When I came back from the window, Lucy had lain down again and was sleeping peacefully. She did not stir again all night.

*14 August.* On the East Cliff, reading and writing all day. Lucy seems to have become as much in love with the spot as I am. This afternoon she said a strange thing. We were coming home for dinner, and the sun was setting. It shone on the windows of St Mary's Church, and Lucy murmured as if to herself: "His red eyes again! They are just the same."

It was such an odd thing to say, coming apropos of nothing, that it quite startled me. I looked at her carefully. She had a strange, half-dreamy look on her face. I said nothing, but followed her eyes. She seemed to be looking at our own seat, where a dark figure was sitting alone. I was a little startled myself, because it seemed for an instant as if the stranger had great eyes like burning flames. But a second look dispelled the illusion. The red sunlight was shining on the windows of St Mary's Church behind our seat.

*15 August.* Lucy is weaker and more tired than ever. I cannot understand it. She eats well and sleeps well, and enjoys the fresh air. But all the time the roses in her cheeks are fading, and she gets weaker and more tired day by day. At night I hear her gasping as if for air.

*17 August.* No diary for two days. I have not had the heart to write. Some sort of shadowy darkness seems to be coming over our happiness. No news from Jonathan, and Lucy seems to be growing weaker, while her mother's hours are numbering to a close. Lucy's mother has told me a secret: she has a serious heart disease and may not live long. Lucy does not know, and I must not tell her.

I looked at the little wounds on Lucy's throat. They are still open, and if anything, larger than before. The edges of them are faintly white. They are like little white dots with red centres. Unless they heal within a day or two, I shall insist on the doctor seeing them.

"I don't remember much," she said. "I remember walking through the streets and over the bridge. I heard a lot of dogs howling — the whole town seemed full of dogs. Then I have a vague memory of something long and dark with red eyes, just as we saw in the sunset. And something very sweet and very bitter all around me at once. Then I seemed to be sinking into deep green water, and there was a singing in my ears. Then everything seemed passing away from me. My soul seemed to go out of my body and float about in the air. And then I came back and found you shaking me."

**Letter from Sister Agatha, Hospital of St Joseph and St Mary, Budapest, to Miss Mina Murray**

*12 August.*

Dear Madam,

I write at the wish of Mr Jonathan Harker, who is himself not strong enough to write, though he is getting better. He has been under our care for nearly six weeks, suffering from a violent **brain fever**. He wishes me to send his love, and to say that he has written to Mr Peter Hawkins, Exeter, to say that he is sorry for the delay, and that all his work is completed. He will need to stay some weeks to rest in our hospital in the hills, but will then return.

He has had some fearful shock. In his fever, his ravings have been dreadful — of wolves and poison and blood, of ghosts and demons, and I fear to say of what. Be careful with him always, so that there may be nothing to excite him of this kind for a long time to come.

He came to us on the train from Klausenburg. The guard was told by the station master there that he rushed into the station shouting for a ticket home. Seeing from his violent manner that he was English, they gave him a ticket for the furthest station on the way there that the train reached.

Be assured that he is well cared for. He has won all hearts by his sweetness and gentleness. He is truly getting on well, and I have no doubt will in a few weeks be all himself. But be careful of him, for safety's sake.

Sister Agatha

**Dr Seward's Diary**

*19 August.* A strange and sudden change in Renfield last night. At about eight o'clock he began to get excited and sniff about as a dog does when setting. The attendant was struck by his manner, and knowing my interest in him, encouraged him to talk. He is usually respectful to the attendant, but tonight he was quite proud. All he would say was:

"I don't want to talk to you. You don't count now. The Master is at hand."

At nine o'clock I visited him myself. He was in a strange state of excitement. He did not seem to see me. He kept saying, "The Master is at hand." I was tired, so I went to bed. At two in the morning the night attendant woke me. Renfield had escaped through the window!

I threw on my clothes and ran down. The attendant had seen him running along the road towards the old empty house next door — Carfax. We followed. I climbed over the wall and saw him on the far side of the old house. He was pressed against the old iron-bound door of the chapel, and he was talking, apparently to somebody. I was afraid to go close, in case I frightened him and he ran off again. I heard him say:

"I am here to do Your bidding, Master. I am Your slave, and You will reward me, for I shall be faithful. I have worshipped You long and far off. Now that You are near, I await Your commands, and You will not pass me by, will You, dear Master, in Your distribution of good things?"

He is a selfish old beggar anyhow. He thinks of the loaves and fishes even when he believes he is in a Real Presence. When we closed in on him, he fought like a tiger. He is immensely strong, and he was more like a wild beast than a man. However, we caught him in time and put him in a **straitjacket**. He is now in the padded room, chained to the wall.

His cries are at times awful, but the silences that follow are more deadly still, because he means murder in every turn and movement.

Just now he spoke coherent words for the first time: "I shall be patient, Master. It is coming — coming — coming!"

So I took the hint and came too. I was too excited to sleep, but this diary has quieted me, and I feel I shall get some sleep tonight.

## Chapter 9 — A Wedding and an Illness

**Letter from Mina Harker to Lucy Westenra**

*Budapest, 24 August.*

My dearest Lucy,

I know you want to hear everything that has happened since we said goodbye at Whitby station.

I found my dear Jonathan so thin and pale and weak-looking. All the strength has gone from his dear eyes. He is only a wreck of himself, and he does not remember anything that has happened to him for a long time. At least, he wants me to believe so, and I shall never ask.

He has had some terrible shock, and I am afraid it might hurt his poor brain if he tried to remember it. Sister Agatha, who is a good creature and a born nurse, tells me that he raved of dreadful things while he was out of his mind. I wanted her to tell me what they were, but she only made the sign of the cross and said she would never tell. "The ravings of the sick are the secrets of God," she said.

When he woke up, he asked for his coat, because he wanted something from the pocket. I asked Sister Agatha, and she brought all his things. Among them was his notebook. I was going to ask him to let me look at it, because I thought I might find some clue to his trouble. But he must have seen it in my eyes, because he sent me to the window, saying he wanted to be quite alone for a moment.

Then he called me back and said very seriously, with his hand on the notebook: "Wilhelmina, you know, dear, my ideas of the trust between husband and wife. There should be no secret, no concealment. I have had a great shock. When I try to think of what it is, I feel my head spinning, and I do not know if it was all real or the dreaming of a madman. The secret is here, and I do not want to know it. I want to take up my life here, with our marriage. Are you willing, Wilhelmina, to share my ignorance? Here is the book. Take it and keep it. Read it if you will, but never let me know, unless some solemn duty should come upon me to go back to the bitter hours, asleep or awake, sane or mad, recorded here."

Then he fell back exhausted. I put the book under his pillow and kissed him.

And then, Lucy dear, we were married! I have asked Sister Agatha to ask the Mother Superior to let our wedding be this afternoon. A priest came, and we were married in the hospital room, with Jonathan sitting up in bed. Oh, Lucy, it was the most solemn and the sweetest moment of my life.

When it was over, I took the notebook, wrapped it in white paper, tied it with a little bit of pale blue ribbon, and sealed it with wax. Then I showed it to Jonathan and told him I would keep it so, and never open it unless it was for his own dear sake or for the sake of some stern duty.

Goodbye, my dear. Write to me soon, and tell me all about yourself.

Your ever-loving, Mina Harker

**Letter from Lucy Westenra to Mina Harker**

*Whitby, 30 August.*

My dearest Mina,

Oceans of love and millions of kisses, and may you soon be in your own home with your husband. I wish you were coming home soon enough to stay with us here. The strong air would soon restore Jonathan; it has quite restored me. I have an appetite like a horse, and I sleep well. You will be glad to know that I have quite given up walking in my sleep. I think I have not stirred out of my bed for a week.

Arthur says I am getting fat. Oh, I forgot to tell you that Arthur is here. We have such walks and drives, and rides and rowing. I love him more than ever. We are to be married on 28 September.

Your loving, Lucy

**Dr Seward's Diary**

*20 August.* The case of Renfield grows even more interesting. He is now so far quieted that there are periods when his passion is calmer. For the first week after his attack, he was violent all the time. Then one night, just as the moon rose, he grew quiet and kept murmuring to himself, "Now I can wait. Now I can wait."

The attendant came to tell me, so I ran down at once. He was still in the straitjacket and in the padded room, but the angry look had gone from his face. His eyes had something of their old pleading — I might almost say "cringing" — softness. I was satisfied with his present condition and told them to free him.

For three nights now the same thing has happened: violent all day, then quiet from moonrise to sunrise.

*23 August.* Last night he escaped again. Once more he ran to the door of the old chapel at Carfax. When he saw us, he became furious, and he would have attacked us if we had not caught him in time. But then something strange happened. Suddenly he became calm. I looked around and saw only a big bat flying silently towards the west in the moonlight. Bats usually fly round and round, but this one seemed to go straight on, as if it knew where it was going.

The patient grew calmer every moment and at last said, "You needn't tie me. I shall go quietly!" Without trouble we came back to the house. I feel there is something ominous in his calm, and shall not forget this night.

**Letter from Arthur Holmwood to Dr Seward**

*Albemarle Hotel, 31 August.*

My dear Jack,

I want you to do me a favour. Lucy is ill. That is, she has no special disease, but she looks awful, and is getting worse every day. I have asked her if there is any cause. I dare not ask her mother, because I know she has a weak heart, and to worry her about her daughter's health would be fatal now. Mrs Westenra has told me that her doom is spoken — disease of the heart — though poor Lucy does not know it yet.

I am sure that there is something preying on my dear girl's mind. I am almost distracted when I think of her. To look at her gives me a pang. I told her I would ask you to see her, and though she objected at first — I know why, old fellow — she finally agreed. It will be a painful task for you, I know, old friend, but it is for her sake. Please come to lunch at Hillingham tomorrow, two o'clock, so as not to arouse any suspicion in Mrs Westenra.

Yours, Arthur

**Letter from Dr Seward to Arthur Holmwood**

*2 September.*

My dear old fellow,

I have examined Lucy, and I must tell you at once that I could find nothing wrong with her physically. At the same time, I am not at all satisfied with her appearance. She is very pale. She told me that she breathes with difficulty at night, sometimes, and that she has bad dreams which frighten her, but which she cannot remember.

I took some of her blood and tested it. The blood is healthy. There is no sign of any disease. But since there must be a cause somewhere, I have come to the conclusion that it must be something mental.

I have written to my old friend and master, Professor Van Helsing, of Amsterdam, who knows as much about obscure diseases as anyone in the world. I have asked him to come over. He is a philosopher and one of the best scientists of our time. He has nerves of steel, he is always calm, and he never gives up. And he has the kindest and most honest heart in the world.

Yours always, John Seward

**Letter from Professor Abraham Van Helsing to Dr Seward**

**Letter from Dr Seward to Arthur Holmwood**

*3 September.*

My dear Art,

Van Helsing has come and gone. He came with me to Hillingham. Lucy's mother was lunching out, so we were alone with her. Van Helsing made a very careful examination of the patient. He told me afterwards that this is a matter of life and death, perhaps more.

"What do you mean by that?" I asked, because he was very serious.

"She has lost blood," he said. "There is no disease, but there has been much loss of blood. And this is a thing that I do not yet understand."

He is going back to Amsterdam tonight, but will return in a few days. He asked me to watch her carefully and to send him a telegram at once if anything changes. He also asked me not to tell you that he is worried. "Do not be anxious," he said to me at the door. "I am not yet sure. But I shall be. I shall come back."

**Dr Seward's Diary**

*4 September.* Renfield still interests me. He had only one outburst, and that was yesterday at an unusual time. Just before noon he began to grow restless. The attendant knew the symptoms and called for help. Fortunately the men came at a run, because at the stroke of noon he became so violent that it took all their strength to hold him. ## Chapter 10 — Blood and Garlic

**Dr Seward's Diary**

*7 September.* Van Helsing arrived quickly. When we met at Liverpool Street station, his first words were, "Have you said anything to our young friend, the lover of her?"

"No," I said. "I was waiting until I had seen you."

"Good. It is better he does not know yet. Perhaps he will never know. I pray so. But if it is necessary, then he shall know all."

When we arrived at Hillingham, Mrs Westenra met us. She was worried, but not as much as I expected. She did not understand how ill her daughter was, and perhaps that was a kindness of nature. We decided that she must not see Lucy, because any shock could kill her.

We went up to Lucy's room. If I was shocked when I saw her yesterday, I was horrified when I saw her today. She was as white as chalk. The red seemed to have gone even from her lips and gums, and the bones of her face stood out clearly. Her breathing was painful to see or hear. Van Helsing's face became hard as stone, and his eyebrows met over his nose. Lucy lay without moving and did not seem to have the strength to speak.

Van Helsing called me out of the room. "My God!" he said. "This is dreadful. There is no time to lose. She will die for lack of blood to keep the heart's action as it should be. There must be a **transfusion** of blood at once. Is it you or me?"

"I am younger and stronger, Professor. It must be me."

"Then get ready at once. I will bring up my bag. I am prepared."

I went downstairs with him. As we were going, there was a knock at the hall door. When we reached the hall, the maid had just opened the door, and Arthur was stepping quickly in. He rushed up to me, saying in an eager whisper:

"Jack, I was so anxious. I read between the lines of your letter, and have been in an agony. The Dad was better, so I ran down here to see for myself. Is not that gentleman Dr Van Helsing? I am so thankful to you, sir, for coming."

At first the Professor looked annoyed at the interruption. But then he saw how young and strong Arthur was, and his eyes shone. He held out his hand.

"Sir, you have come in time. You are the lover of our dear miss. She is bad, very, very bad. Nay, my child, do not go like that." Arthur had suddenly grown pale and sat down in a chair, almost fainting. "You are to help her. You can do more than any that live, and your courage is your best help."

"What can I do?" asked Arthur. "Tell me, and I shall do it. My life is hers, and I would give the last drop of blood in my body for her."

The Professor smiled. "My young sir, I do not ask so much as that — not the last!"

"What shall I do?" There was fire in his eyes.

Van Helsing put his hand on Arthur's shoulder. "Come! You are a young, strong man, and that is what we need. Miss Lucy is very ill. She needs blood, or she will die. We are going to do a transfusion: we will take blood from your full veins and put it into her empty ones. John was going to give his blood. But your blood is better — you are young and healthy, and you do not spend your life in books like us."

Arthur turned to him and said, "If you only knew how gladly I would die for her, you would understand —" He stopped, his voice choking.

"Good boy!" said Van Helsing. "One day you will be happy that you did everything you could for her. Come now, and be quiet. You may kiss her once before we begin, but then you must leave when I tell you. And say nothing to her mother. You know about her heart."

We all went up to Lucy's room. Arthur was told to wait outside. Lucy turned her head and looked at us, but said nothing. She was not asleep, but she was simply too weak to make the effort. Her eyes spoke to us, and that was all.

Van Helsing took some things from his bag and laid them on a little table out of sight. Then he mixed a sleeping drug and, coming over to the bed, said cheerily, "Now, little miss, here is your medicine. Drink it off, like a good child." She drank it, and soon she was asleep.

Then the Professor called Arthur in and told him to take off his coat. "You may give her that one little kiss while I bring the table. John, help me!"

So neither of us looked while he bent over her.

Van Helsing did the operation quickly and well. As Arthur's blood went into her, some life seemed to come back into Lucy's cheeks. Arthur grew paler, but his face shone with joy. After a while I began to worry, because even a strong man like Arthur was getting weak. And Lucy was only a little better. How much blood must she have lost!

The Professor stood with his watch in his hand, looking first at Lucy and then at Arthur. At last he said softly, "Don't move. It is enough. You look after him. I will look after her."

When it was over, Arthur was very weak. I put a bandage on his arm. Van Helsing lifted Lucy's head to fix her pillow. Lucy always wore a narrow black velvet band around her throat, with an old diamond buckle that Arthur had given her. As the Professor moved her head, the band moved up a little, and I saw a red mark on her throat.

Arthur did not notice it, but I heard Van Helsing take a sharp breath. That is what he does when he is shocked. But he only said to me, "Now take our brave young man downstairs. Give him some port wine and let him lie down for a while. Then he must go home, sleep a lot and eat a lot, to get back the blood he has given. You have saved her life this time, sir. You can go home and rest."

When Arthur had gone, I went back to the room. Lucy was sleeping gently, but her breathing was stronger. By the bedside sat Van Helsing, looking at her intently. The velvet band again covered the red mark. I asked the Professor in a whisper:

"What do you make of that mark on her throat?"

"What do you make of it?"

"I have not examined it yet," I answered, and I opened the band. Over the big vein in her neck there were two small holes. They were not large, but they did not look healthy. Their edges were white, as if they had been rubbed. For a moment I thought that perhaps this was how she had lost her blood. But that was impossible. If she had lost so much blood through those little holes, her whole bed would be red with it.

"Well?" said Van Helsing.

"Well," I said, "I can make nothing of it."

The Professor stood up. "I must go back to Amsterdam tonight," he said. "There are books and things there which I want. You must remain here all night, and you must not let your sight pass from her."

"Shall I have a nurse?" I asked.

"We are the best nurses, you and I. You keep watch all night. See that she is well fed, and that nothing disturbs her. You must not sleep all the night. Later on we can sleep, you and I. I shall be back as soon as possible. And then we may begin."

"May begin?" I said. "What on earth do you mean?"

"We shall see!" he answered, as he hurried out.

*8 September.* I sat up all night with Lucy. In the evening the drug stopped working, and she woke up. She seemed like a different person. She was cheerful and lively, but I could see that she was still very weak.

I noticed something strange: she was afraid to go to sleep. When I asked her why, she said that sleep brought her terrible dreams. "I don't want to sleep," she said. "Sleep has some horror for me." I promised to stay awake and watch over her, and that I would wake her if I saw any sign of bad dreams. Then she slept peacefully all night.

*9 September.* I was very tired when I got to Hillingham. For two nights I had hardly slept, and my brain was beginning to feel numb. Lucy was up and in cheerful spirits. She insisted that I should rest in the room next to hers, and that she would call me if anything happened. I agreed, because I was exhausted. I lay down on the sofa and forgot everything.

*10 September.* I was woken by the Professor's hand on my head. "And how is our patient?" he asked.

"Well, when I left her — or rather when she left me," I answered.

"Come, let us see," he said, and together we went into the room.

The blind was down, and I went over to raise it gently, while Van Helsing stepped, with his soft, cat-like tread, over to the bed.

As the morning sunlight came into the room, I heard the Professor take that sharp breath again, and fear went through my heart. He stepped back from the bed and cried out in German, "Gott in Himmel!" ("God in heaven!"). His face was grey with horror. He pointed to the bed.

Poor Lucy lay on the bed, unconscious, whiter than ever. Even her lips were white, and her gums seemed to have moved back from her teeth, like those of a dead person.

"Quick!" he said. "Bring the brandy."

I ran downstairs and came back with the bottle. He put a little brandy on her lips, and together we rubbed her hands and wrists and chest. He felt her heart, and after some terrible moments he said:

"It is not too late. It beats, though but feebly. All our work is undone. We must begin again. There is no young Arthur here now. I have to call on you yourself this time, friend John." As he spoke, he was opening his bag and taking out the instruments for transfusion. I had taken off my coat and rolled up my shirt sleeve.

It was a terrible thing. Lucy, as before, received the blood well, and some colour came back to her cheeks. Van Helsing's face showed relief. He looked at her throat. The marks were still there, and they seemed fresher.

When the operation was done, Van Helsing said to me: "Do not tell Arthur about this. It would frighten him and make him jealous, too. There must be no such thing."

*11 September.* This afternoon I went over to Hillingham. Lucy was much better, and Van Helsing was in excellent spirits. Shortly after I arrived, a big parcel from abroad came for the Professor. He opened it with much excitement and held up a great bunch of white flowers.

"These are for you, Miss Lucy," he said.

"For me? Oh, Dr Van Helsing!"

"Yes, my dear, but not for you to play with. These are medicines."

Lucy made a face. "Oh, but they are only common **garlic** flowers!"

To my surprise, Van Helsing stood up and spoke very seriously. "Don't joke with me! I never joke. Everything I do has a serious reason. Do what I tell you — for other people's sake, if not for your own." Then he saw that Lucy was frightened, and he spoke more gently. "Oh, my dear little miss, don't be afraid of me. I only want to help you. These common flowers are very good for you. I will put them in your room myself, and I will make a **wreath** for you to wear around your neck. But don't tell anyone about it."

Then the Professor did a strange thing. He closed the windows and locked them. He took some flowers and rubbed them all over the window frames, so that any air coming in would smell of garlic. Then he rubbed them around the door and the fireplace too. It all seemed very strange to me, so I said:

"Well, Professor, I know you always have a reason for what you do, but this certainly puzzles me. It is well we have no sceptic here, or he would say that you were working some spell to keep out an evil spirit."

"Perhaps I am!" he answered quietly, as he began to make the wreath which Lucy was to wear round her neck.

We waited while Lucy got ready for the night. When she was in bed, he came and fixed the wreath of garlic round her neck himself. The last words he said to her were:

"Take care you do not disturb it. And even if the room feel close, do not tonight open the window or the door."

"I promise," said Lucy. "And thank you both a thousand times for all your kindness to me! Oh, what have I done to be blessed with such friends?"

As we left in my carriage, Van Helsing said, "Tonight I can sleep in peace, and I need sleep! Two nights of travel, a lot of reading, a lot of worry, and a night without sleep. Come for me early tomorrow, and we will visit our pretty patient together. She will be much stronger because of my 'spell'! Ho, ho!"

## Chapter 11 — The Wolf

**Lucy Westenra's Diary**

*12 September.* How good they all are to me! I love that dear Dr Van Helsing. I wonder why he was so worried about these flowers. He really frightened me, he was so fierce. But he must be right, because I feel better already. Tonight I am not afraid to be alone, and I can go to sleep without fear. I don't mind the flapping outside the window. Oh, those terrible nights, when I fought against sleep! Well, here I am tonight, hoping for sleep. I never liked garlic before, but tonight it is delightful. Good night, everybody.

**Dr Seward's Diary**

*13 September.* I called at the Berkeley Hotel for Van Helsing, and we drove to Hillingham. It was a lovely morning. Mrs Westenra met us in the hall. She was smiling.

"You will be glad to know that Lucy is better," she said. "She is still asleep. I looked into her room, but I didn't go in, because I didn't want to wake her."

The Professor smiled. "Aha! I thought I had diagnosed the case. My treatment is working."

"You must not take all the credit, doctor," she said. "Lucy's health this morning is partly because of me. I went into her room in the night. She was sleeping soundly, but the room was terribly stuffy. There were lots of those horrible, strong-smelling flowers everywhere, and she even had a bunch of them around her neck. I was afraid the heavy smell would be too much for her in her weak state, so I took them all away and opened the window a little to let in some fresh air. You will be pleased with her, I am sure."

She went to her bedroom, where she usually had breakfast. As she spoke, I watched the Professor's face, and saw it turn grey. He controlled himself while she was there, because he knew a shock could kill her. But the moment she had gone, he pulled me into the dining room and closed the door.

Then, for the first time in my life, I saw Van Helsing break down. He raised his hands in silent despair, and then sat down on a chair, put his face in his hands and began to cry — loud, dry sobs that seemed to come from his broken heart. Then he raised his arms again, as if he were asking the whole universe.

"God! God! God!" he said. "What have we done to deserve this? This poor mother, who knows nothing, thinks she is doing the best for her child. But she may lose her daughter, body and soul! And we cannot tell her, or the shock will kill her. All the powers of the devil are against us!" Suddenly he jumped up. "Come," he said. "We must act. Devils or no devils, we will fight him all the same."

We went up to Lucy's room. Again I pulled up the blind, and Van Helsing went to the bed. Her face was as white as wax again. This time he was not surprised. He only looked very sad and full of pity.

"As I expected," he murmured. Without a word he went and locked the door, and then began to set out on the little table the instruments for another transfusion of blood. I had seen the need long before, and began to take off my coat, but he stopped me.

"No! Today you must operate. I shall provide. You are weakened already." As he spoke, he took off his coat and rolled up his shirt sleeve.

Again the operation. Again the sleeping drug. Again some colour came back to her grey cheeks, and she slept peacefully. This time I watched while Van Helsing rested and got his strength back.

Afterwards, he spoke to Mrs Westenra. He told her very gently that she must not take anything out of Lucy's room without asking him. He said the flowers were medicine, and that breathing their smell was part of the treatment. Then he took charge of the case himself, and said he would watch that night and the next, and would send me word when to come.

*15 September.* For the next few days Lucy got better. The Professor rubbed the flowers on the windows and the door every evening, and put a fresh wreath around her neck. Her colour came back, and she began to eat. "I feel so much stronger," she said. "And I have no bad dreams at all."

**From *The Pall Mall Gazette*, 18 September**

*THE ESCAPED WOLF*

Our reporter has interviewed the keeper of the wolves at the Zoological Gardens in Regent's Park. Yesterday a big grey wolf called Bersicker escaped from its cage.

"It was a very strange thing," said the keeper, Mr Thomas Bilder. "The wolves were quiet all day. But at about half past ten at night, they started howling, all of them. I went out to see what was wrong. Beside the cage there was a tall, thin man with a hooked nose and a pointed beard. His eyes were hard and cold, and they seemed red. I didn't like him at all. He said to me, 'Keeper, these wolves seem upset at something.' I said, 'Maybe it's you.' He smiled and showed his sharp white teeth. Then, to my surprise, he put his hand through the bars and touched the old wolves' ears. They didn't bite him. They seemed to like it.

"Later, at about midnight, the howling started again, worse than ever. I went out, and there was the cage, with the bars broken and twisted, and Bersicker gone. I haven't any idea how it happened."

The next morning, the wolf came back by himself. He walked into the zoo as if nothing had happened. His head was cut and full of broken glass. He must have climbed a wall or broken through a window somewhere.

**Dr Seward's Diary**

*17 September.* I was busy in my study after dinner when the door suddenly opened, and Renfield rushed in. His face was twisted with anger. He had escaped again. He ran at me with a dinner knife in his hand. I saw that he was dangerous, and I tried to keep the table between us. But he was too quick and too strong. Before I could defend myself, he cut my left wrist quite badly.

Before he could strike again, I hit him with my right hand, and he fell on his back on the floor. My wrist bled freely, and a little pool of blood ran onto the carpet. When the attendants rushed in and we turned to him, he was lying on his stomach on the floor, licking up, like a dog, the blood that had fallen from my wounded wrist. He was easily caught, and to my surprise went with the attendants quite calmly, simply repeating over and over again: "The blood is the life! The blood is the life!"

I cannot afford to lose blood just now. I have lost too much of it lately for my physical good, and then the long strain of Lucy's illness is telling on me. I am over-excited and weary, and I need rest, rest, rest.

**Telegram from Van Helsing, Antwerp, to Seward, Carfax** *(sent to Carfax, Sussex, by mistake, and delivered twenty-two hours late)*

*17 September.* Do not fail to be at Hillingham tonight. If not watching all the time, frequently visit and see that flowers are as placed. Very important. Do not fail. Shall be with you as soon as possible after arrival.

**A note left by Lucy Westenra**

*17 September, night.* I am writing this and leaving it to be seen, so that no one may get into trouble through me. This is an exact record of what took place tonight. I feel I am dying of weakness, and I have barely the strength to write, but it must be done if I die in the doing.

I went to bed as usual, taking care that the flowers were placed as Dr Van Helsing told me. Soon I fell asleep.

I was woken by the flapping at the window, which had started after that sleepwalking on the cliff at Whitby, when Mina saved me. I was not afraid, but I did wish that Dr Seward was in the next room, as Dr Van Helsing said he would be. I tried to go back to sleep, but I couldn't. Then the old fear of sleep came to me, and I decided to stay awake.

Suddenly I heard a howl outside, like a dog's, but more fierce and deeper. I went to the window and looked out, but I could see nothing, except a big bat, which had obviously been beating its wings against the window. So I went back to bed, but decided not to sleep. Then the door opened, and Mother looked in. Seeing that I was awake, she came in and sat by me.

"I was worried about you, darling," she said, "and came in to see that you were all right."

I was afraid she might catch cold sitting there, so I asked her to come in and sleep with me. She came into bed and lay down beside me. She did not take off her dressing gown, because she said she would only stay a while and then go back to her own bed.

As she lay there in my arms, the flapping and beating came to the window again. She was frightened and cried out, "What is that?" I tried to calm her, and at last succeeded, and she lay quiet. But I could hear her poor dear heart still beating terribly.

After a while there was the low howl again outside in the shrubbery, and shortly after there was a crash at the window, and a lot of broken glass was thrown on the floor. The blind blew back with the wind, and in the opening of the broken window there was the head of a great, thin grey wolf.

Mother cried out in fright, and struggled up into a sitting position, and clutched wildly at anything that would help her. Among other things, she grabbed the wreath of flowers that Dr Van Helsing insisted on my wearing round my neck, and tore it away from me. For a second or two she sat up, pointing at the wolf, and there was a strange and horrible gurgling in her throat. Then she fell over as if struck with lightning, and her head hit my forehead and made me dizzy for a moment or two.

The room seemed to spin around me. I kept my eyes on the window. The wolf pulled his head back, and then a cloud of tiny specks came in through the broken window, turning round and round like a column of dust in the desert. I tried to move, but I seemed to be under a spell. My dear mother's body lay on top of me, and it was already growing cold, because her poor heart had stopped. I remembered nothing more for a while.

When I woke up, I heard the bell of a passing church, and the dogs all around the neighbourhood were howling. In the bushes outside a nightingale was singing. I was dazed and stupid with pain and terror and weakness, but the sound of the nightingale seemed like the voice of my dead mother come back to comfort me.

The sounds seemed to have woken the maids, too, because I could hear their bare feet outside my door. I called to them, and they came in. When they saw what had happened, and what it was that lay over me on the bed, they screamed. The wind rushed in through the broken window, and the door slammed shut.

I got up, and they lifted my dear mother's body and laid it on the bed, covered with a sheet. They were all so frightened that I sent them to the dining room to drink a glass of wine each. I put the garlic flowers on my mother's body. I remembered Dr Van Helsing's words, but I did not want to take the flowers away from her. And soon the servants would be back to sit with me.

The maids did not come back. I called them, but got no answer, so I went to the dining room to look for them. My heart sank when I saw what had happened. They all four lay helpless on the floor, breathing heavily. The bottle of sherry was on the table half full, but there was a strange smell about. I was suspicious, and examined the bottle. It smelt of **laudanum**, and looking on the sideboard, I found that the bottle which Mother's doctor uses for her — oh! did use — was empty.

What am I to do? What am I to do? I am back in the room with Mother. I cannot leave her, and I am alone, except for the sleeping servants, whom someone has drugged. Alone with the dead! I dare not go out, because I can hear the low howl of the wolf through the broken window.

The air seems full of specks, floating and circling in the draught from the window, and the lights burn blue and dim. What am I to do? God shield me from harm this night! I shall hide this paper in my breast, where they shall find it when they come to lay me out. My dear mother is gone! It is time that I go too. Goodbye, dear Arthur, if I should not survive this night. God keep you, dear, and God help me!

## Chapter 12 — Lucy's Last Days

**Dr Seward's Diary**

*18 September.* Van Helsing's telegram arrived a day late. I went immediately to Hillingham and arrived early in the morning. I knocked and rang the bell many times, but nobody answered. I was afraid. Why was nobody awake?

I went round the house to see if I could find a way in. Then I heard a carriage stop at the gate, and a moment later I met Van Helsing running up the path.

"Then it was you, and just arrived," he said. "How is she? Are we too late? Did you not get my telegram?"

I explained quickly. His face went pale. "Then I fear we are too late," he said. "God's will be done!" But then he became strong again. "Come. If there is no way open to get in, we must make one. Time is all in all to us now."

We went round to the kitchen window at the back of the house. The Professor took a small surgical saw from his bag and cut through the iron bars. Then we opened the window and climbed in. There was nobody in the kitchen. In the dining room we found the four servant women lying on the floor. They were breathing heavily, and the smell of laudanum told us why. We did not stop to help them, because they were in no danger. We ran up to Lucy's room.

How can I describe what we saw? On the bed lay two women, Lucy and her mother. The mother lay further in, covered with a white sheet. Her face was white and frozen in terror. Lucy lay beside her, with the garlic flowers on her mother's body, not around her own neck. Her face was white and drawn. Her throat was bare, and we saw the two little wounds, looking horribly white and torn.

Without a word, the Professor bent over the bed. Then he jumped up. "It is not yet too late!" he cried. "Quick! Quick! Bring the brandy!"

I ran down and returned with it. We rubbed brandy on her lips and gums, on her wrists and the palms of her hands. Then I went down and woke the maids by putting wet towels on their faces. They were very upset and cried a lot. I told them to make a hot bath for Lucy, and to light the fires.

We put Lucy into the hot bath, and slowly some warmth came back into her. While we were doing this, there was a knock at the hall door. One of the maids came up and whispered to me that a gentleman had arrived with a message from Mr Holmwood. I told her to ask him to wait.

We carried Lucy to another bedroom and put her in a warm bed. Van Helsing looked at me very seriously. "She needs more blood," he said. "But she has had so much from you and me. Who can we ask now?"

Then a voice came from the doorway. "What's the matter with me, anyhow?"

It was Quincey Morris. Arthur had sent him, because he could not leave his dying father, and he had had no news from me. Van Helsing looked at the tall American and said, "A brave man's blood is the best thing on this earth when a woman is in trouble. You are a man, and no mistake. Well, the devil may work against us for all he's worth, but God sends us men when we want them."

Once again we went through that terrible operation. Quincey gave his blood, and Lucy's heart became stronger. Her breathing was more regular.

Later, I found a piece of paper on the floor of Lucy's old room. It was the note that Lucy had written in the night, which had fallen from her nightdress. I gave it to Van Helsing. He read it and was silent for a long time.

"In God's name, what does it all mean?" I asked. "Was she, or is she, mad? Or what sort of horrible danger is it?"

Van Helsing put the paper in his pocket. "Do not trouble about it now," he said. "Forget it for the present. You shall know and understand it all in good time, but it will be later."

That evening Quincey and I talked. "Jack Seward," he said, "I don't want to push into anything where I've no right to be. But this is no ordinary case. What is it? You and the Professor each gave blood to her, didn't you? And Arthur too?"

"Yes," I said.

"And how long has this been going on?"

"About ten days."

"Ten days! Then I guess, Jack Seward, that the poor pretty creature that we all love has had put into her veins within that time the blood of four strong men. Man alive, her whole body wouldn't hold it." Then, coming close to me, he spoke in a fierce half-whisper: "What took it out?"

I shook my head. "That is the question. Van Helsing is simply frantic about it, and I don't know what to think."

Quincey held out his hand. "Count me in," he said. "You and the Dutchman will tell me what to do, and I'll do it."

*19 September.* All last night Lucy slept badly. She was always afraid to sleep. When she slept, she moved her hands strangely. Several times she tried to pull the garlic flowers away from her neck. When she was awake, she held them close.

Arthur received a telegram: his father had died. Now he is Lord Godalming. Poor Arthur! He has lost his father, and he may lose the woman he loves.

*20 September.* Today Lucy is much worse. She is so weak that she can hardly turn her head. Her gums have moved back from her teeth, and her teeth look longer and sharper than before. In her sleep she looks strong, but when she wakes, she looks tired and weak.

Van Helsing watched her for a long time. Then he said quietly to me, "Send for Arthur. She is dying. It will not be long now."

I sent a telegram, and Arthur came at once. When he saw her, he could not speak. He sat by the bed and held her hand. She woke up, and when she saw him, she smiled and whispered, "Arthur! Oh, my love, I am so glad you have come!" He bent down to kiss her, but Van Helsing stopped him. "No," he whispered. "Not yet! Hold her hand. It will comfort her more."

Later that night, I watched Lucy and saw a strange change in her. Her breathing became heavy and her mouth opened. Her pale gums had moved back, so her teeth looked longer and sharper than ever. Half asleep, she opened her eyes. They looked dull and hard. Then she spoke in a soft, sweet voice that I had never heard from her before:

"Arthur! Oh, my love, I am so glad you have come! Kiss me!"

Arthur bent eagerly over to kiss her. But at that instant Van Helsing, who, like me, had been startled by her voice, rushed at him. With a strength I never thought he could have, he caught Arthur by the neck with both hands and threw him almost across the room.

"Not on your life!" he said. "Not for your living soul and hers!" And he stood between them like a lion.

Arthur was so surprised that for a moment he did not know what to do or say.

Van Helsing and I watched Lucy's face. For a moment, a look of terrible anger passed over it like a shadow. Her sharp teeth bit together. Then her eyes closed, and she breathed heavily.

A little later she opened her eyes again, and now they were soft and kind. She took Van Helsing's big brown hand in her thin white one, and kissed it. "My true friend," she said weakly, "my true friend, and his! Oh, protect him, and give me peace!"

"I promise!" he said seriously. He knelt beside her and held up his hand, as if he were making an oath. Then he turned to Arthur. "Come, my child. Take her hand and kiss her on the forehead — only once."

Their eyes met instead of their lips, and so they said goodbye.

Lucy's eyes closed. Van Helsing, who had been watching closely, took Arthur's arm and drew him away.

Then Lucy's breathing became heavy again, and all at once it stopped.

"It is all over," said Van Helsing. "She is dead!"

I took Arthur by the arm and led him away to the drawing room, where he sat down and covered his face with his hands, sobbing in a way that nearly broke me down to see.

I went back to the room and found Van Helsing looking at poor Lucy. His face was more serious than ever. Her body had changed. Death had given back some of her beauty. Her face looked soft again, and even her lips were not so pale. She looked as if she were only sleeping.

I stood beside the Professor and said, "Ah, well, poor girl, there is peace for her at last. It is the end!"

He turned to me and said with great seriousness, "Not so. Alas! Not so. It is only the beginning!"

When I asked him what he meant, he only shook his head and answered, "We can do nothing as yet. Wait and see."

## Chapter 13 — The Funeral

**Dr Seward's Diary (continued)**

The funeral was arranged for the next day but one, so that Lucy and her mother could be buried together. I did all the work. The undertaker's men were very polite and careful. Even the woman who prepared the body said to me, "She makes a very beautiful corpse, sir. It's quite a privilege to look after her."

I noticed that Van Helsing never stayed far away from the room. Lucy's mother had no other relatives, and Arthur had to go home the next day for his own father's funeral. So we could not tell anyone about the deaths. Van Helsing and I looked through Lucy's papers ourselves. He wanted to see if there was anything about the strange things that had happened to her.

In the evening we went into the room where Lucy lay. The room was full of white flowers, and death looked as beautiful there as it could. Lucy's face was even more lovely than when she was alive. Hours had passed, but instead of showing the signs of death, she looked like a sleeping girl. The Professor looked very stern. He had not loved her as I had, and there was no need for tears in his eyes. He said to me, "Stay here till I return," and left the room.

He came back with a handful of wild garlic from the box in the hall, and he put the flowers among the others on and around the bed. Then he took from his neck, inside his collar, a little gold crucifix and placed it over her mouth. He put the sheet back and we came away.

I was undressing in my own room when, with a knock at the door, he entered and at once began to speak.

"Tomorrow I want you to bring me, before night, a set of **post-mortem** knives."

"Must we make an autopsy?" I asked.

"Yes and no. I want to operate, but not as you think. Let me tell you now, but not a word to another. I want to cut off her head and take out her heart. Ah! You a surgeon, and so shocked! You, whom I have seen with no tremble of hand or heart do operations of life and death that make the rest shudder."

"But why do it at all? The girl is dead. Why cut her poor body without need?"

He put his hand on my shoulder and said with infinite tenderness, "Friend John, I pity your poor bleeding heart, and I love you the more because it does so bleed. If I could, I would take on myself the burden that you do bear. But there are things that you know not, but that you shall know. And bless me for knowing, though they are not pleasant things. John, my child, you have been my friend now many years. Have you ever known me to do anything without good cause? I may err — I am but man — but I believe in all I do."

The next morning, I went early to the room. To my surprise and distress, Van Helsing's crucifix was gone. One of the maids had stolen it in the night. Van Helsing was furious when I told him, and then very sad. "She stole it because it was gold," he said. "Poor woman. She does not know what she has done."

In the afternoon, a lawyer came. He was from the firm of Mr Hawkins's friends. He told us that Lucy's mother had left everything to Arthur. Then he left us.

Arthur came back on Tuesday evening, after his father's funeral. He was very unhappy, as was natural. He came to see Lucy, and I went with him into the room. When he saw her lying there so beautiful, he turned to me and whispered, "Jack, is she really dead?"

I told him sadly that she was. He knelt beside her for a long time. When he stood up, he kissed her, and then went out of the room without looking back.

Later, Van Helsing asked Arthur if he could look at Lucy's papers and letters. Arthur agreed. "Do what you will," he said. "I know that she would have approved."

The funeral was held the next day. Lucy and her mother were buried together in the family tomb, in a lonely churchyard near Hampstead Heath. It is a quiet place, with old trees.

When the funeral was over and Arthur had gone home, Van Helsing and I took the train back to London. I saw him start to laugh in the carriage. He laughed and laughed, until he cried. I had to pull down the blinds, so that no one could see him. Then, when he had stopped, he cried again, just like a woman. I tried to be stern with him, as one is with a woman in such a state, but it had no effect.

"Ah, you don't understand, friend John," he said. "Don't think I am not sad, although I laugh. I laughed because King Laugh came to me. He comes when he wants. He says, 'I am here, I am here.' Oh, it is a hard world, a sad world, full of misery and trouble. And yet when King Laugh comes, he makes them all dance to the tune he plays. That poor girl, so young, so sweet, lies now in a tomb in that lonely churchyard. Our poor Arthur gave his blood to her, and now he thinks that he and she are like married, because his blood is in her veins. And you gave blood, and I gave blood, and Quincey gave blood. So she is married to us all! Ha, ha! It is all so terrible that I must laugh, or I would go mad."

I felt sorry for him. "Then she is dead," I said, "and the matter is finished."

He looked at me very seriously. "Not so," he said. "It is only the beginning."

I returned to my hospital and to my work. Van Helsing went back to Amsterdam, but he promised to return soon. "There is much to do," he said, "and I shall be back tomorrow night."

**Mina Harker's Journal**

*22 September.* In the train to Exeter. Jonathan sleeping.

It seems only yesterday that I was writing in my diary at Whitby, and now so much has happened. I am married to Jonathan. Jonathan is a lawyer, a partner, rich, master of his business. Mr Hawkins is dead and buried, and Jonathan is ill again.

Mr Hawkins was so good to us. He died suddenly, and he left everything to Jonathan, who was like a son to him. We came up to London for the funeral. It was a very simple, solemn service. Afterwards, we walked along Piccadilly together. Jonathan was holding my arm, as he used to before I went to school.

I was looking at a very beautiful girl in a big hat, sitting in a carriage outside a shop, when I felt Jonathan hold my arm so tight that he hurt me. He said under his breath, "My God!"

I turned to him quickly and asked him what was wrong. He was very pale, and his eyes seemed to be bulging out as he stared with half in terror and half in amazement at a tall, thin man, with a beaky nose and black moustache and pointed beard, who was also looking at the pretty girl. He was looking at her so hard that he did not see either of us. I got a good view of him. His face was not a good face. It was hard and cruel, and his big white teeth, which looked all the whiter because his lips were so red, were pointed like an animal's.

Jonathan kept staring at him, until I was afraid that the man would notice. He asked me, "Do you see who it is?"

"No, dear," I said. "I don't know him. Who is it?"

His answer seemed to shock and thrill me, because it was said as if he did not know that it was to me, Mina, to whom he was speaking. "It is the man himself!"

The poor dear was evidently terrified at something — very greatly terrified. I do believe that if he had not had me to lean on and to support him, he would have sunk down. He kept staring. A man came out of the shop with a small parcel and gave it to the lady, who then drove off. The dark man kept his eyes fixed on her, and when the carriage moved up Piccadilly, he followed in the same direction and called a cab.

Jonathan kept looking after him, and said, as if to himself, "I believe it is the Count, but he has grown young. My God, if this be so! Oh, my God! My God! If I only knew! If I only knew!" He was so upset that I was afraid to keep his mind on the subject by asking him any questions, so I remained silent.

We walked to Green Park and sat on a seat. After a few minutes Jonathan fell asleep, with his head on my shoulder. When he woke up after about twenty minutes, he said to me quite cheerfully, "Why, Mina, have I been asleep? Oh, do forgive me for being so rude. Come, and we'll have a cup of tea somewhere."

He had obviously forgotten all about the dark stranger. I don't like this forgetting. It may cause, or continue, some injury to the brain. I must not ask him, because I am afraid it will do more harm than good. But I must somehow learn the facts of his journey abroad. The time has come, I fear, when I must open that parcel and know what is written. Oh, Jonathan, you will, I know, forgive me if I do wrong, but it is for your own dear sake.

*Later.* A sad homecoming in every way. The house is empty of the dear soul who was so good to us. Jonathan is still pale and dizzy, and now a telegram from Van Helsing, whoever he may be:

> *You will be grieved to hear that Mrs Westenra died five days ago, and that Lucy died the day before yesterday. They were both buried today.*

Oh, what a lot of sorrow in a few words! Poor Mrs Westenra! Poor Lucy! Gone, gone, never to return to us! And poor, poor Arthur, to have lost such sweetness out of his life! God help us all to bear our troubles.

**Dr Seward's Diary**

*22 September.* It is all over. Arthur has gone back to his home and has taken Quincey Morris with him. What a fine fellow Quincey is! I believe in my heart that he suffered as much about Lucy's death as any of us, but he bore it like a Viking.

Van Helsing is lying down, resting before his journey. He goes to Amsterdam tonight, but says he will return tomorrow night. He only wants to make some arrangements which can only be made personally. He is to stop with me then, if he can. He says he has work to do in London which may take him some time.

And now, what am I to do? I am closing this diary. God only knows if I shall ever begin another. Sad and hopeless, I say FINIS.

**From *The Westminster Gazette*, 25 September**

*A HAMPSTEAD MYSTERY*

The area of Hampstead is at present troubled by a series of events. During the past two or three days, several small children have gone missing from home, or have not come back from playing on the Heath. In all these cases the children were too young to give any proper explanation of what happened. But they all said that they had been with a "**bloofer lady**" (a child's way of saying "beautiful lady").

The children were always found late in the evening, and on two occasions not until early the next morning. Some of the children have been slightly hurt. They had small wounds in the throat, such as might be made by a rat or a small dog. The police have been asked to keep a lookout for any stray dog in the area, especially near Hampstead Heath.

## Chapter 14 — Mina Meets Van Helsing

**Mina Harker's Journal**

*23 September.* Jonathan is better after a bad night. I am so glad that he has plenty of work to do, because that keeps his mind off the terrible things. He will be busy all day at the office, so I will open his journal and read it.

*24 September.* I could not write last night. I was too upset by Jonathan's terrible journal. Poor dear! How he must have suffered, whether it was real or only in his imagination. I wonder if there is any truth in it at all. Did he get his brain fever first and then write all those terrible things? Or did he have some cause for it all? I suppose I shall never know, because I dare not ask him.

And yet that man we saw yesterday! Jonathan seemed quite certain of him. Poor fellow! I suppose it was the funeral that upset him and sent his mind back to those thoughts.

He believes it all himself. I remember how on our wedding day he said: "Unless some solemn duty come upon me to go back to the bitter hours." There seems to be through it all some thread of continuity. That fearful Count was coming to London. If it should be, and he came to London, with its millions of people... There may be a solemn duty, and if it comes, we must not shrink from it. I shall be prepared. I shall get my typewriter this very hour and begin typing the journal. Then we shall be ready for other eyes if required.

**Letter from Van Helsing to Mrs Harker**

*24 September. (Confidential.)*

Dear Madam,

I ask you to forgive me for writing, because I am the friend who sent you the sad news of Miss Lucy Westenra's death. With the kindness of Lord Godalming, I have been allowed to read her letters and papers, because I am deeply worried about certain matters of great importance. Among them I find some letters from you, which show how great friends you were and how you love her. Oh, Madam Mina, by that love, I beg you, help me. It is for others' good that I ask — to stop a terrible evil.

May I see you? You can trust me. I am a friend of Dr John Seward and of Lord Godalming (that was Arthur of Miss Lucy). I must keep it private for the present from all. I should come to Exeter to see you at once if you tell me I am allowed to come, and where and when.

Van Helsing

**Telegram from Mrs Harker to Van Helsing**

*25 September.* Come today by quarter-past-ten train if you can catch it. Can see you any time you call. Wilhelmina Harker.

**Mina Harker's Journal**

*25 September.* I cannot help feeling terribly excited as the time draws near for the visit of Dr Van Helsing. Somehow I expect that it will throw some light on Jonathan's sad experience. And as he looked after poor dear Lucy in her last illness, he can tell me all about her. That is the reason of his coming. It is about Lucy and her sleepwalking, and not about Jonathan.

How silly of me! He would want to know about Lucy's sleepwalking, and I told Lucy not to tell anyone! I hope he does not think I was wrong. Jonathan has gone to the office, so I am alone.

*Later.* He has come and gone. Oh, what a strange meeting, and how it all makes my head spin! I feel like one in a dream.

At half-past two, the knock came. The maid opened the door and announced "Dr Van Helsing". I rose and bowed, and he came towards me. He is a man of medium height, strongly built, with his shoulders set back over a broad, deep chest. His head is large and his forehead is broad. His face is clean-shaven, and his big dark blue eyes are set widely apart. They are quick and tender or stern by turns.

"Mrs Harker, is it not?" he said. I nodded.

"That was Miss Mina Murray?" Again I nodded.

"It is Mina Murray that I came to see, that was friend of that poor dear child Lucy Westenra. Madam Mina, it is on account of the dead I come."

I told him everything I knew about Lucy — the sleepwalking at Whitby, the night in the churchyard, the marks on her throat. I had written it all in my diary, in shorthand, and he could not read it. So I told him I had typed a copy, and I gave it to him. He read it at once. When he had finished, he stood up and took both my hands.

"Oh, Madam Mina," he said, "how can I say what I owe to you? This paper is as sunshine. It opens the gate to me. I am dazed with so much light." Then he asked me about my husband, and I told him that Jonathan had been ill after a shock.

"A shock, and after brain fever?" he said. "That is not good. What kind of shock was it?"

I suddenly felt I could not hold it any longer. I burst into tears and told him everything. "Dr Van Helsing, what I have to tell you is so strange that you must not laugh at me or my husband. Since yesterday I have been in a sort of fever of doubt. You must be kind to me, and not think me foolish that I have even half believed some very strange things."

"Oh, my dear," he said, "if you only knew how strange is the matter regarding which I am here, it is you who would laugh. I have learned not to think little of anyone's belief, no matter how strange it may be."

I gave him the typed copy of Jonathan's journal. "Read it," I said. "It is long, but I have typed it out. It will tell you my trouble and Jonathan's. Then when you have read it, you can tell me what you think."

He promised to read it that night at his hotel and to write to me in the morning. He left, and I sat thinking, thinking — I don't know what.

**Letter from Van Helsing to Mrs Harker**

*25 September, 6 o'clock.*

Dear Madam Mina,

I have read your husband's so wonderful diary. You may sleep without doubt. Strange and terrible as it is, it is *true*! I will pledge my life on it. It may be worse for others, but for him and you there is no dread. He is a noble fellow. Let me tell you from experience of men, that one who would do as he did in going down that wall and to that room — ay, and going a second time — is not one to be injured in permanence by a shock. His brain and his heart are all right. This I swear, before I have even seen him. So be at rest.

I shall have much to ask him of other things. I am blessed that today I come to see you, for I have learned all at once so much that again I am dazzled — dazzled more than ever, and I must think.

Yours the most faithful, Abraham Van Helsing

**Jonathan Harker's Journal**

*26 September.* I thought never to write in this diary again, but the time has come. When I got home last night, Mina had supper ready. She showed me Van Helsing's letter. She told me that she had read my journal, and that the Professor said it was all true. It seems to have made a new man of me. It was the doubt as to the reality of the whole thing that knocked me over. I felt helpless, and in the dark, and distrustful. But now that I know, I am not afraid, even of the Count.

This morning Van Helsing came to breakfast. When he looked at me, he said, "But Madam Mina told me you were ill, that you had had a shock." It was so funny to hear my wife called "Madam Mina" by this kindly, strong-faced old man.

I smiled and said, "I was ill. I have had a shock. But you have cured me already."

"And how?"

"By your letter to Mina last night. I was full of doubt. Everything seemed unreal, and I could not even trust my own eyes and ears. So I did not know what to do. But now I know."

Then I told him that I had seen the Count in London, and that he looked younger.

He was very interested in this. "Ah," he said. "Younger? Then he has been feeding. That is very bad news." Then he asked me if I would help him, and I said I would do anything. "Then come to London with me," he said. "I have much to show you."

When I saw him off at the station, he asked me for a copy of the newspapers, and I gave him the Westminster Gazette of yesterday. He read the report about the "bloofer lady" and the children of Hampstead, and his face became deadly pale. "Mein Gott! Mein Gott!" he said. "So soon! So soon!" Then the train moved off.

**Dr Seward's Diary**

*26 September.* Truly there is no such thing as finality. Not a week since I said "Finis", and yet here I am starting fresh again.

Van Helsing came back from Exeter today. He burst into my room at about five o'clock and threw last night's Westminster Gazette into my hand.

"What do you think of that?" he asked, standing back with his arms folded.

I looked over the paper, because I really did not know what he meant. But he took it from me and pointed out a paragraph about children being lured away at Hampstead. It did not mean much to me until I reached a passage where it described small punctured wounds on their throats. An idea struck me, and I looked up.

"Well?" he said.

"It is like poor Lucy's."

"And what do you make of it?"

"Simply that there is some cause in common. Whatever it was that injured her has injured them."

I did not quite understand his answer. "That is true indirectly, but not directly."

"What do you mean, Professor?" I asked.

He sat down, put his hands together, and spoke very seriously. "Friend John, do you believe in things you cannot understand? Do you not think that there are things which you cannot understand, and yet which are? That some people see things that others cannot? Ah, it is the fault of our science that it wants to explain all; and if it explain not, then it says there is nothing to explain."

Then he asked me a long series of questions. Did I believe in hypnotism? In reading thoughts? Did I know that some animals live for hundreds of years? That some spiders grow huge in old churches by drinking the oil from the lamps? That there are bats in South America that come at night and drink the blood of cattle and horses — and of men?

I was confused. "Professor," I said, "let me be your student again. Tell me what you mean. Are you trying to say that Lucy was bitten by such a bat, and that such a thing is here in London?"

He stood up. "No," he said. "I want you to believe."

"To believe what?"

"To believe in things that you think are impossible. I once heard an American say that faith is the power to believe things that we know are not true. I agree with him, in a way. He meant that we must keep an open mind. We must not let a small truth stop us from seeing a big truth."

"So you want me to keep an open mind, and not let my old ideas stop me from accepting something strange. Is that your lesson?"

"Ah, you are my favourite pupil still. Now that you are willing to understand, you have taken the first step."

"And what is the next?" I asked. "What do you think those wounds on the children's throats were made by?"

He looked at me with very serious eyes. "They were made by Miss Lucy!"

## Chapter 15 — The Empty Coffin

**Dr Seward's Diary (continued)**

For a moment I was so angry that I could not speak. It was as if he had hit Lucy in the face while she was alive. I hit the table hard with my hand and stood up.

"Dr Van Helsing, are you mad?"

He raised his head and looked at me, and the kindness of his face calmed me at once. "I wish I were," he said. "Madness would be easy to bear compared with truth like this. Oh, my friend, why do you think I waited so long to tell you so simple a thing? Was it because I hate you? No. It was because I wanted to be gentle with you, because I know you loved that sweet lady."

"Forgive me," I said. He went on:

"My friend, it was because I did not want to hurt you that I did not tell you. But now I am going to prove it. Tonight, will you come with me?"

I was not sure, but I agreed. I asked him what he wanted to do first.

"First, we go to see the child in the hospital — the one who was found yesterday. Then we spend the night, you and I, in the churchyard where Lucy lies. Here is the key that locks the tomb. I had it from the coffin-man to give to Arthur."

My heart sank, because I felt that there was some fearful ordeal before us. But I could do nothing, so I said that we had better hurry.

We found the child awake at the North Hospital. It had had a sleep and taken some food, and altogether was going on well. Dr Vincent took the bandage from its throat and showed us the punctures. There was no mistaking the similarity to those which had been on Lucy's throat. They were smaller, and the edges looked fresher, but that was all.

We asked Dr Vincent what he thought had made them. He thought they were bites of some animal, perhaps a rat, but he was not sure. "There are many bats in that area," he said. "Perhaps a sailor has brought a bat from a foreign country, and it has escaped." He said the child would be kept in hospital for at least a week.

It was dark when we arrived at the churchyard. We climbed over the low wall, so that no one would see us. Van Helsing unlocked the door of the tomb. The tomb in the daytime, decorated with fresh flowers, had looked grim enough. But now, some days afterwards, when the flowers hung brown and dead, and spiders and beetles had come back, it was terrible beyond what I can describe.

Van Helsing lit a candle and found Lucy's coffin. He took out a screwdriver and began to remove the screws from the lid.

"What are you going to do?" I asked.

"To open the coffin. You shall yet be convinced."

He took off the wooden lid, and underneath it was a lead coffin. Van Helsing took a small saw and cut a hole in the lead. He made it big enough to see inside. Then he held the candle over the hole and told me to look.

I drew near and looked. The coffin was empty.

It was a great shock to me. But Van Helsing was calm. Now he was even more sure that he was right. "Are you satisfied now, friend John?" he asked.

I felt all the stubbornness in me awake as I answered him: "I am satisfied that Lucy's body is not in that coffin. But that only proves one thing."

"And what is that, friend John?"

"That it is not there."

"That is good logic," he said, "as far as it goes. But how do you explain it?"

"Perhaps a body-snatcher," I suggested. "Some of the undertaker's people may have stolen it." I felt that I was speaking foolishness, and yet it was the only real cause which I could suggest.

The Professor sighed. "Ah well!" he said. "We must have more proof. Come with me."

He put back the lid of the coffin, collected his things, put out the candle, and we left the tomb. He locked the door behind us. Then he told me to watch at one side of the churchyard, while he watched at the other.

It was a long, lonely wait. I heard a clock far away strike twelve, then one, then two. I was cold and nervous. I was angry with the Professor for bringing me, and angry with myself for coming.

Suddenly I thought I saw something white moving between two dark trees on the far side of the churchyard. At the same moment, a dark shape — the Professor — moved quickly towards it. I ran too, but I had to go round tombstones, and I fell over graves in the dark. Far away, a cock crowed.

Not far away, behind some trees, a white figure moved towards the tomb. I could not see where it went. When I reached the place where I had first seen it, I found the Professor holding a small child in his arms. He held it out to me and said, "Are you satisfied now?"

"No," I said, in a way that I felt was aggressive.

"Do you not see the child?"

"Yes, it is a child, but who brought it here? And is it wounded?"

"We shall see," said the Professor. We went out of the churchyard, he carrying the sleeping child. When we had gone some little distance, we struck a match and looked at the child's throat. There was no scratch or mark of any kind.

"Was I right?" I asked triumphantly.

"We were just in time," said the Professor thankfully.

We did not want to take the child to a police station, because we would have to explain what we were doing in the churchyard at night. So we took it to the edge of Hampstead Heath, and when we heard a policeman's heavy steps, we laid it on the path. We waited and watched until he saw it as he flashed his lantern to and fro. We heard his exclamation of surprise, and then we went away silently.

*27 September.* We had to wait until two o'clock in the afternoon. There was a funeral at noon, and we waited until all the people had gone home. Then we watched the man in charge of the churchyard lock the gate and leave. Now we were alone.

Van Helsing again opened the tomb and the coffin. Again I looked into the coffin, and a shock of surprise and dismay shot through me.

There lay Lucy, just as she looked the night before her funeral. She was even more beautiful than before. I could not believe she was dead. Her lips were red — redder than ever — and her cheeks were pink.

"Is this a trick?" I said to him.

"Are you convinced now?" said the Professor in response. As he spoke, he put out his hand and, in a way that made me shudder, pulled back the dead lips and showed the white teeth.

"See," he went on, "they are even sharper than before. With this and this" — he touched one of the canine teeth and that below it — "the little children can be bitten. Are you of belief now, friend John?"

Again I felt that I wanted to argue. I could not accept such a terrible idea. So I said something that I was ashamed of even as I said it: "Perhaps someone put her here after last night."

"Indeed? That is so, and by whom?"

"I do not know. Someone has done it."

"And yet she has been dead one week. Most people in that time would not look so."

I had no answer, so I said nothing. Van Helsing was looking carefully at the dead woman's face. He lifted her eyelids and looked at her eyes, and opened her lips again to look at her teeth. Then he turned to me and said:

"Her case is different from all others I have read about. The vampire bit her while she was sleepwalking, in a kind of **trance**. Yes, you are surprised — you did not know that, but you will learn everything later. She died in a trance, and now she is in a trance as one of the **Un-Dead**. That is why she looks so sweet. Usually, when the Un-Dead sleep in their coffins, their faces show what evil creatures they are. But she looks like an innocent sleeping girl. That makes it very hard for me to kill her as she sleeps."

His words made my blood turn cold, and I began to realise that I was starting to believe him. But if she was really dead, why was it so terrible to talk of killing her?

He looked up at me, and evidently saw the change in my face, because he said almost joyously, "Ah, you believe now?"

I answered, "Do not press me too hard all at once. I am willing to accept. How will you do this bloody work?"

"I shall cut off her head and fill her mouth with garlic, and I shall drive a **stake** through her body."

I shook to think of cutting up the body of the woman I had loved. But the feeling was not as strong as I expected. In fact, I was beginning to feel horror at this creature, this Un-Dead, as Van Helsing called it. I was beginning to hate it.

Van Helsing stood for a long time, thinking. At last he closed his bag with a snap and said:

"I have made up my mind. If I followed my feelings, I would do it now, at this moment. But it is not so simple. Arthur must know. If he does not see it with his own eyes, he will never believe it. He will think that we killed the woman he loved because of some mad idea. So he must see, and then he must help. And there is something more. The one who made her Un-Dead is still free. If we do not stop him, there will be many more like her."

He locked the coffin and the tomb, and we left. "Tomorrow night," he said, "you will bring Arthur and Quincey to me at the Berkeley Hotel at ten o'clock. And now, I will go back here alone tonight and watch, so that she does not go out again. I have garlic and a crucifix. I will put them on the door of the tomb. She will not be able to leave."

## Chapter 16 — Freeing Lucy's Soul

**Dr Seward's Diary (continued)**

*28 September.* Arthur and Quincey came to the hotel at ten o'clock. Van Helsing explained very carefully what he wanted. He asked them to promise to come with us that night and do what he asked, without asking questions.

Arthur was uneasy. "I will come," he said, "but I must know what you want to do. It is about Lucy, isn't it?"

"Yes," said Van Helsing. "I want to go to her tomb tonight, and open her coffin."

Arthur jumped up, his face red with anger. "Professor, are you mad? To do such a thing to her grave? I will not allow it!"

Van Helsing spoke to him very gently. "My dear friend, I would give my life to save you this pain. But there is a terrible duty. Miss Lucy is dead. That is true. But if I am right, she is not at peace. Come with us, and see with your own eyes. If I am wrong, then I will say so, and you may do what you like with me. If I am right — then you will understand."

Arthur was silent for a long time. Then he said, "I trust you, Professor. I will come."

Just before midnight, we climbed the churchyard wall. The night was dark, with only a little moonlight through the clouds. Van Helsing opened the tomb. We went in, and he lit a lamp. He took the screwdriver and opened the coffin, as before. Arthur watched, white and silent.

The coffin was empty.

For several minutes, no one spoke. Then Quincey said quietly, "Professor, I trust you completely. I will not ask if you have done this yourself. But have you?"

"I swear to you by all that I hold sacred that I have not removed her or touched her," said Van Helsing. "Two nights ago my friend Seward and I came here. I opened the coffin, and it was empty. We waited and saw something white come through the trees. The next day we came here in daylight, and she lay there. Did she not, friend John?"

"Yes," I said.

"Now, it is night, and she is not here. Come outside, and wait."

Van Helsing took some things from his bag. There was a thin white wafer, like the bread used in church, wrapped in a white cloth. He broke it into small pieces and mixed it with some soft, sticky material. Then he pressed it into all the cracks around the door of the tomb.

"What is that?" asked Quincey.

"I am closing the tomb," said Van Helsing, "so that the Un-Dead may not enter."

"And is it that stuff you put there that will stop her?"

"It is."

"What is it?"

Van Helsing took off his hat and answered with respect: "The **Host**. I brought it from Amsterdam. I have special permission."

It was an answer that frightened the most sceptical of us. We understood that he was very serious about his purpose. Then we hid behind the trees and waited.

Never did tombs look so ghostly white. Never did trees look so dark and gloomy. Then we heard, far away, the cry of a child. The Professor touched my arm. Between the trees we saw a white figure coming towards us. It was a woman, dressed in the white clothes of the grave. We could not see her face, because she was bending over something that she held against her breast. Then the figure stopped, and in the moonlight we saw that it was Lucy Westenra.

Lucy Westenra, but how changed! Her sweetness had turned to cruelty, and her purity to evil. Her lips were red with fresh blood, and it ran down her chin and stained her white dress.

We shook with horror. Van Helsing stepped out, and we followed him. Lucy saw us, and she stepped back with an angry noise, like a cat surprised. Then her eyes fell on Arthur. They were Lucy's eyes in shape and colour, but full of hell-fire instead of the pure, gentle eyes we knew. At that moment, whatever love I still had for her turned to hate.

She threw the child down on the ground, as cruelly as an animal drops its food, and the child cried out and lay there moaning. Then she came towards Arthur with her arms open and a terrible, sweet smile.

"Come to me, Arthur," she said in a soft, musical voice. "Leave these others and come to me. My arms are hungry for you. Come, and we can rest together. Come, my husband, come!"

There was something so sweet and so evil in her voice that even we, who only heard it, felt its power. Arthur seemed to be under a spell. He opened his arms. She was jumping towards him when Van Helsing sprang forward and held up his little gold crucifix between them. She stepped back, and with a sudden twisted face full of rage, she rushed past him towards the tomb, as if to go in.

But when she was a few feet from the door, she stopped, as if held by some invisible power. She turned round, and her face was clear in the moonlight. Never have I seen such hate and anger on a face. I hope no one will ever see it again.

"Answer me, my friend!" said Van Helsing to Arthur. "Am I to go on with my work?"

Arthur fell to his knees and covered his face with his hands. "Do as you will, friend," he cried. "Do as you will. There can never be anything as horrible as this again!"

Quincey and I went to him and held his arms. Van Helsing walked to the tomb and began to remove some of the Host from the cracks around the door. When he stepped back, we all watched with horror as the woman, with a body as real as ours, passed through a space where a knife blade could hardly have gone. Then Van Helsing put the Host back in the cracks.

We picked up the child. It was not badly hurt. We left it in a place where a policeman would find it, and we went home.

"Tomorrow," said Van Helsing, "we will come back at noon, when she sleeps. Then we must do what must be done."

*29 September.* At about a quarter to twelve, we four came to the churchyard. It was a different place in the daylight. The sun was shining, and birds were singing. Van Helsing had brought his long bag, and also a round wooden stake, about three feet long, with a sharp point. It had been hardened in the fire. He also had a heavy hammer.

We went into the tomb, and he opened the coffin. There lay Lucy, as we had seen her the night before, with her beautiful face and her red mouth. It was the same Lucy, and yet it was a cruel, wicked imitation. Arthur looked at her and said to Van Helsing, "Is this really Lucy's body, or only a devil in her shape?"

"It is her body, and yet not her body. But wait a while, and you shall see her as she was, and is."

Then Van Helsing explained. "Before we begin, listen. People who have studied the Un-Dead for centuries all say the same thing. When someone becomes Un-Dead, they are cursed: they cannot die. They live on for ages, making new victims and spreading evil in the world. Everyone who dies from the bite of the Un-Dead becomes Un-Dead too. And so the circle grows wider and wider.

"Friend Arthur, if you had kissed Lucy before she died, or last night when you opened your arms to her, you would also have become Un-Dead after your death — a **Nosferatu**, as they say in Eastern Europe.

"Poor Lucy has only just begun this terrible life. The children she has bitten are not badly hurt yet. But if she continues, they will lose more and more blood, and she will call them to her. If she truly dies now, it will all stop. The little wounds will disappear, and the children will forget everything.

"And best of all, when she is truly dead, her soul will be free. She will go to God with the angels. The hand that strikes the blow will set her free. I am willing to do it. But is there someone here with a better right? Arthur, when you cannot sleep at night in the future, will it not be a comfort to think: 'It was my hand that sent her to heaven — the hand of the man who loved her most'?"

We all looked at Arthur. He stepped forward bravely, although his hand shook and his face was as white as snow. "My true friend, I thank you with all my broken heart. Tell me what to do, and I will do it."

Van Helsing put his hand on his shoulder. "Brave boy! A moment of courage, and it is done. You must drive this stake through her heart. It will be terrible — I will not lie to you — but it will be short, and afterwards you will feel great peace. Once you begin, you must not stop. Remember that we, your friends, are around you, and we are praying for you."

Arthur took the stake and the hammer. Van Helsing opened his prayer book and began to read, and Quincey and I followed as well as we could. Arthur placed the point over the heart. Then he struck with all his might.

The thing in the coffin twisted and shook. A terrible scream came from the red lips. The sharp teeth bit together until the lips were cut, and red foam covered the mouth. But Arthur never stopped. He looked like the god Thor as his strong arm rose and fell, driving the stake deeper and deeper, while blood came up around it. His face was hard and determined.

And then the twisting and shaking of the body became less, and the teeth stopped biting, and the face stopped trembling. At last it lay still. The terrible task was over.

The hammer fell from Arthur's hand. He would have fallen too if we had not caught him. Great drops of sweat stood on his forehead, and his breath came in broken gasps.

When we looked into the coffin again, we cried out with joy. The horrible creature had gone. There lay Lucy as we had known her in life, with her sweet, pure face. It was thin and tired, as it had been during her illness, but that only made it dearer to us. A holy calm lay over her face like sunshine, and we knew that she was at peace for ever.

Van Helsing came and laid his hand on Arthur's shoulder and said to him, "And now, Arthur my friend, dear lad, am I not forgiven?"

Arthur took the old man's hand in his and raised it to his lips. "Forgiven! God bless you that you have given my dear one her soul again, and me peace."

"Now, my child, you may kiss her, as she would want. She is not a devil now. She is not Un-Dead any more. She is truly dead, and her soul is with God!"

Arthur bent and kissed her, and then we sent him and Quincey out of the tomb. The Professor and I sawed the top off the stake, leaving the point of it in the body. Then we cut off the head and filled the mouth with garlic. We closed the lead coffin, screwed on the coffin lid, and gathering up our belongings, came away. When the Professor locked the door, he gave the key to Arthur.

Outside, the air was sweet, the sun was shining and the birds were singing. The whole world seemed different. We felt peaceful and glad, although our joy was mixed with sadness.

Before we left, Van Helsing said: "My friends, the first part of our work is done, and it was the most painful for us. But a greater task is still ahead: we must find the one who caused all this sorrow, and destroy him. I have some clues. It will be long and difficult and dangerous. Will you all help me? We have all learned to believe. So we can all see our duty. Do we promise to go on to the end?"

One by one, we shook his hand and promised. As we left, the Professor said, "In two days we will all have dinner together at seven o'clock at John's house. I will invite two other people you do not know yet. Then I will explain everything and tell you our plans. Tonight I go to Amsterdam, but I will be back tomorrow night. And then our great hunt begins."

## Chapter 17 — Putting the Story Together

**Dr Seward's Diary (continued)**

*29 September.* Van Helsing asked me to meet Mrs Harker at Paddington station, because Jonathan was away on business. I recognised her at once from Lucy's descriptions. She is a sweet-faced, intelligent young woman. She came straight to me and said, "Dr Seward, isn't it?" She already knew about Lucy's death from Van Helsing.

I took her to my house at Purfleet. When she heard that the hospital was so close to Carfax, she looked worried. She knew from Jonathan's journal that the Count had bought that house.

In the afternoon she came into my study while I was using my phonograph. "Do you keep your diary on this machine?" she asked. I said yes. She asked if she could hear the part about Lucy's death. I did not want her to hear everything, so I said I could not find the right place easily. Then I realised that I had made a mistake: my face showed her that I was hiding something.

She looked at me with her honest eyes. "Dr Seward, you do not know me, but I loved Lucy. I think you have recorded things that I should know. Let me read your diary, and you can read Jonathan's journal and mine. I have typed them all. If we put all our knowledge together, we may be able to fight this evil."

I gave her the phonograph cylinders. She took them to her room to listen and type them. I sat and read the typed copy of Jonathan's journal and her own diary. Now I understand everything better. The Count came to England on the *Demeter*. He went from Whitby to London. His boxes of earth are in Carfax, next door to my hospital! And Renfield has been talking to his "Master" all this time!

**Mina Harker's Journal**

*29 September.* I have listened to Dr Seward's diary. It was terrible. Poor, poor Lucy! I cried a lot, but I typed it all. When I finished, I said to Dr Seward, "We must put all these papers in order of date. Then we will see the whole story clearly, like one long line." He agreed. We have started work on it.

I have also sent a telegram to Jonathan, who is in Whitby. He has gone there to find out more about the boxes. He will come here tonight.

*30 September.* Jonathan came at nine o'clock. He had found out a lot. He spoke to Mr Billington, the lawyer in Whitby, who showed him all the papers. There were fifty boxes of "common earth, to be used for experimental purposes". They had been sent by train to King's Cross station in London, and then a company called Carter, Paterson & Co. had taken them by wagon to Carfax.

So all fifty boxes went to Carfax. Or did they? We must find out if they are all still there.

While I sorted the papers, Jonathan read everything. Now he knows the whole story too. He looks stronger and more determined than I have seen him for a long time. He has a purpose now.

**Dr Seward's Diary**

*30 September.* I went to see Renfield today. He was very calm. When I told him that some ladies and gentlemen had come to stay, he seemed interested. He asked if he could meet Mrs Harker. I did not want to allow it, but she asked to see him, and I agreed, because I was curious.

When she went in, he was sitting on his bed. He stood up politely. "Good evening, Dr Seward," he said. Then he looked at Mina very carefully. "You're not the girl the doctor wanted to marry, are you? You can't be, you know, because she's dead."

Mina smiled sweetly. "Oh no! I have a husband of my own. I am Mrs Harker."

They talked for a few minutes. Renfield spoke very sensibly and politely. When she said goodbye, he said to her, in a sad voice, "Goodbye, my dear. I pray God I may never see your sweet face again. May He bless and keep you!"

I was very surprised. I did not understand what he meant.

**Mina Harker's Journal**

*30 September.* Lord Godalming and Mr Morris arrived before dinner. Dr Seward told them who I was, and I shook hands with them. I saw how unhappy Arthur was. I told him I had read everything, and that I knew how much he had loved Lucy.

Later, he and I were alone for a moment. Suddenly he broke down. He sat on the sofa and cried like a child. I sat beside him and took his hand. I hope he did not think it too forward of me. But I could see that he needed someone to comfort him. Men cannot cry in front of other men. He told me how much he had loved Lucy and how terrible her death had been.

"I know," I said. "I know. I loved her too. Let me be like a sister to you."

He held my hand and said, "You will never know what you have done for me tonight. I could not speak of these things to anyone. God bless you!"

Later, Mr Morris came to me in the corridor. He saw my red eyes and understood. "How is Art?" he asked quietly. Then, seeing that I had been crying, he said, "Ah, I see you have been comforting him. Poor old fellow! He needs it. No one but a woman can help a man when he is in trouble of the heart, and he had no one to comfort him."

He was so brave about his own sorrow that my heart bled for him. I knew from Lucy's letter that he too had loved her. I took his hand and said, "I wish I could comfort all who suffer from the heart. Will you let me be your friend, and come to me for comfort if you need it?"

He bent and kissed my hand. "Little girl," he said, "you will never regret that true-hearted kindness." Then he went into the study to join his friend.

"Little girl!" — the very words he had used to Lucy. And oh, but he proved himself a friend!

## Chapter 18 — The Enemy

**Dr Seward's Diary**

*30 September.* After dinner we all met in my study: Van Helsing, Arthur, Quincey, Jonathan, Mina and myself. Van Helsing sat at the head of the table. Mina sat next to him as secretary, and everyone had read the typed papers.

"Then I may take it," said the Professor, "that we are all acquainted with the facts. Let me now tell you what kind of enemy we are fighting.

"There are such beings as vampires. Some of us have seen them with our own eyes. They are known in many countries — in Greece, in Rome, in Germany, in China, in Iceland. And this vampire is the most powerful of all. He has the strength of twenty men. He is cleverer than any man, because he has lived for centuries and has learned much. He can control the dead around him. He can control animals: the rat, the owl, the bat, the fox and the wolf. He can change himself into a wolf or a bat, as we know from the ship at Whitby and from Miss Lucy's window. He can come in fog or mist, which he makes himself. He can become dust in the moonlight, as Jonathan saw the three women do. He can become so small that he passes through a crack, as we saw Lucy do at the tomb. He can see in the dark. He can grow younger when he drinks blood.

"He has no shadow, and no reflection in a mirror, as Jonathan noticed. He does not eat as we do.

"But he is not free. He is a prisoner too, in a way. He cannot enter a house unless someone in the house first invites him in. After that, he can come whenever he likes. His power stops when the day comes. He can only change his shape at sunrise, at noon or at sunset. At other times he must stay in the shape he has. He can only cross running water at the slack or flood of the tide. And he must rest in the earth of his own country — the earth from the grave where he lay. That is why he brought those boxes of earth.

"There are things that take away his power. Garlic, as we know. The crucifix. The Holy Wafer. A branch of wild rose on his coffin keeps him inside it. A sacred bullet fired into his coffin kills him. And to destroy him completely, we must drive a stake through his heart or cut off his head, as we did for Miss Lucy.

"I have asked my friend Arminius, of Budapest University, about his history. He tells me that this Count was in life the famous Voivode Dracula, who won great battles against the Turks hundreds of years ago. He was a soldier, a politician and a scholar — the cleverest of his family. But the Draculas were said to have had dealings with the Evil One. They learned the secrets of dark magic at a school in the mountains, near the lake of Hermanstadt. That is how he became what he is."

While he was speaking, Quincey had been looking out of the window. Suddenly he got up and quietly left the room. A moment later we heard a gunshot outside, and the glass of the window broke. Mina screamed. Then Quincey called from outside: "Sorry! I'm afraid I frightened you. There was a big bat sitting on the window sill. I've seen a lot of bats around here lately. I shot at it, but I missed. It flew away into the wood."

When he came back in, Van Helsing continued. "Now we must make our plans. There were fifty boxes of earth. We must find each one and destroy its power. Then the monster will have no safe place to rest. We will place the Holy Wafer in each box, and then the earth will be useless to him. Then he must either return to his own country or be caught.

"Tonight, we go to Carfax to see how many boxes are still there."

Then the Professor turned to Mina. "And you, Madam Mina — tonight your part in this work ends. You are too precious to us, and the danger is too great. After tonight, please do not ask questions. We will tell you everything later. We are men, and we can face these horrors. You must be our hope, and we will be able to act more freely if we know you are safe."

All the men, even Jonathan, seemed relieved. But it did not seem good to me that they should face danger, perhaps without Mina's help. However, she agreed.

Just as we were about to leave, an attendant came to tell me that Renfield wanted to see me urgently. "He says it is very important," said the man. I was busy, but the others wanted to come too, so we all went.

I was very surprised. Renfield was calmer and more sensible than I had ever seen him. He spoke to Arthur and Quincey and Van Helsing like a gentleman. He knew Arthur's father, the late Lord Godalming. He talked about the Monroe Doctrine with Quincey. He seemed completely sane.

Then he turned to me and said, "Dr Seward, I ask you to let me go home at once — tonight. I am completely cured. I appeal to you, as a gentleman, to let me go."

I wanted to agree, because he seemed so normal. But I remembered his sudden changes of mood, and I said I would think about it in the morning.

His face changed. He became desperate. "Let me go now!" he cried. "Tonight! Tonight, before it is too late! You don't know what you are doing by keeping me here. I ask you as a sane man. Let me go, or I shall be responsible for what happens!"

"What do you mean?" asked Van Helsing. "Why do you want to go tonight?"

"I cannot tell you," said Renfield. "I am not free to say. But if you knew, you would let me go. I swear I am not asking for myself. I am asking for others. Please believe me!"

He fell on his knees and raised his hands, begging me. Tears ran down his cheeks. But I refused. "Come," I said. "No more of this. Go to bed and try to behave more discreetly."

He stood up slowly and went to his bed. As we left, he said in a quiet, polite voice, "Dr Seward, please remember later that I did everything I could to convince you tonight."

## Chapter 19 — Carfax

**Jonathan Harker's Journal**

*1 October, 5 a.m.* I went with the others to Carfax with an easy mind, because I have never seen Mina so strong and well. I am so glad that she agreed to stay behind and leave this terrible work to us men.

Before we left, Van Helsing gave each of us a small silver crucifix to wear, a wreath of garlic flowers, a revolver and a knife, and a small electric lamp. Most important of all, he gave each of us a piece of the Holy Wafer in an envelope. "These are the weapons against the enemy," he said. "The revolver and knife are for other enemies — men or animals. The rest are for him."

We climbed over the wall and walked across the dark garden to the house. I had brought the keys that I had taken to Transylvania for the Count. One of them opened the door. It was stiff and creaked loudly, as if no one had opened it for a long time.

Inside, there was dust everywhere, many inches thick, and old spiders' webs hung from the walls. On a table in the hall there was a bunch of keys, each with a label. Someone had used them recently, because there were marks in the dust.

We knew from my plans where the old chapel was. We went along a passage to a heavy oak door with iron bars. As we opened it, a terrible smell came out to meet us. It was the smell of old earth, of dead things, of blood — the same smell I had smelt at Castle Dracula. It was so terrible that we nearly turned back. But we knew our duty, and we went in.

We began to count the boxes. There were only twenty-nine of the fifty! Twenty-one were missing.

Once I was frightened. I saw Arthur turn suddenly and look out of the door into the dark passage. I looked too, and for a moment I thought I saw the Count's evil face in the shadows — the red eyes, the white skin, the red lips. But when Arthur shone his lamp there, there was nothing.

A few minutes later, I saw Quincey step back suddenly from a corner. We all looked, and we saw a great number of tiny lights, shining like stars. They were the eyes of rats! In a moment, the whole room was alive with them — hundreds, thousands of rats, running over the floor and the boxes.

Arthur was ready for this. He ran to the great oak door, unlocked it and blew a small silver whistle. From behind my house came the barking of dogs. A minute later, three big terriers came running round the corner of the house. Arthur had brought them in case of rats.

When the dogs came to the door, they suddenly stopped and began to howl. They would not come in. Arthur picked up one of them and carried it inside. The moment its feet touched the floor, it seemed to get its courage back. It rushed at the rats, and the other dogs followed. The rats ran away so fast that soon there were only a few dead ones left. As they disappeared, it seemed as if some evil presence had gone with them. The dogs ran around happily and barked.

We found nothing more in the house except a lot of dust. We locked the door and went home. On the way, Van Helsing said, "We have learned a lot tonight. Twenty-nine boxes are here, but twenty-one have gone somewhere else. We must find them. And we have seen that the Count was not here himself, or the rats would not have been sent. He controls them, but he was not with them."

When we got back to the house, it was nearly dawn. I went quietly into our room. Mina was asleep, but she was breathing so softly that I had to put my ear close to hear her. She looked paler than usual. I hope the meeting tonight has not upset her too much. I am very thankful that she is not going to work with us any more.

**Dr Seward's Diary**

*1 October.* At midday I went to see Renfield. He was very quiet. I asked him about the flies, but he was not interested in them any more. "I don't care about flies now," he said. "I don't want any more lives. Not little ones."

"So you want big ones now?" I asked.

He did not answer. He only looked at me with a strange, sly look, and changed the subject.

**Mina Harker's Journal**

*1 October.* It is strange to me to be kept in the dark as I am today. After all this time of Jonathan's full confidence, it hurts me to see him avoid certain subjects. I know it is for my own good, but I cried a little last night when I went to bed.

Last night I had a strange dream. I went to bed when the men left, but I could not sleep at first. I heard a lot of noise from Renfield's room, which is somewhere under mine. Then there was silence. I got up and looked out of the window. Everything was dark and silent. But a thin white mist was moving slowly over the grass towards the house. It seemed almost alive.

I went back to bed and fell asleep. I dreamed that I was asleep and waiting for Jonathan. Then the room seemed full of mist. The gas lamp, which I had left burning low, looked like a tiny red spark in the fog. The mist came in through the crack under the door, and it grew thicker and thicker until it was like a column of cloud in the room. Through it I could see the red light of the lamp. It looked like two red eyes. Then the red eyes came closer, and I remembered what Lucy had said at Whitby about red eyes. I was terribly frightened. Then everything went black.

When I woke up this morning, I was very tired and weak. My face was white. I must be careful about these dreams. I must not tell Jonathan, because he is already worried enough.

## Chapter 20 — Following the Boxes

**Jonathan Harker's Journal**

*1 October, evening.* I found Thomas Snelling, one of the men from Carter, Paterson & Co. who had carried the boxes to Carfax. He was in his home in Bethnal Green, and he was not in a good state — he had been drinking a lot. He could not tell me much. But he gave me the name of his friend, Joseph Smollet, who had worked with him.

I found Smollet at home. He is a decent, intelligent man and a good worker. He remembered the job clearly. He said that they had taken six boxes from Carfax to a house at 197 Chicksand Street, Mile End New Town. Then he had taken another six to Jamaica Lane, Bermondsey.

"Were there any other boxes taken from Carfax?" I asked.

He scratched his head. "There was another man," he said. "His name is Sam Bloxam. He told me that he and another man took some boxes from that old house. I think he took them somewhere in Piccadilly. You'll find him in the evenings at Poplar, at a lodging house called Corcoran's."

I gave him half a sovereign and thanked him. On my way home I wrote to Mina, but I did not tell her about my search. I don't want her to worry.

*2 October.* I went to Poplar. At Corcoran's I found Sam Bloxam. He was a big, rough man, but he was willing to talk when I offered him some money.

"I remember those boxes all right," he said. "Me and my mate took them from Carfax to a house in Piccadilly. There were nine of them. They were terribly heavy. The house was an old one, near the corner of a street, and it had a big front door."

"Who let you in?" I asked.

"An old man. Tall and thin, with a white moustache. He lifted those boxes as easily as if they were empty — and they weighed a ton! I was out of breath after carrying just one. But he picked them up by himself, like they were nothing."

I asked him if he remembered the number of the house. He did not, but he told me the street, and that the house had a black front with a high step at the door. I went to Piccadilly at once and looked at every house near the corner. At last I found one. It had dirty windows and a dark, empty look. There was a sign on it that said "For Sale" — but someone had taken it down recently. I found the name of the estate agents, Mitchell, Sons & Candy. They told me that it had been sold to a foreign gentleman named Count de Ville. They would not tell me more.

So we know where the boxes have gone: six to Mile End, six to Bermondsey, and nine to Piccadilly. That makes twenty-one. If we can find them all and destroy them, the Count will have nowhere to hide.

When I got home, Mina was asleep. She looked very pale and tired. I am beginning to worry about her. She is not as strong as she was. But I must not ask her questions, because I want to keep her out of all this.

**Dr Seward's Diary**

*1 October.* Renfield is strange today. His moods change so fast that I cannot keep up with them. When I came in, he was sitting in a corner, looking very unhappy. I spoke about his flies and spiders, but he did not care.

"I don't want life any more," he said. "I have all I want. I have given up eating flies."

I did not understand. Earlier he had wanted lives — more and more lives. Now he said he did not want them. Had someone promised him something? Or had someone disappointed him?

Then he looked at me and said: "The fact is, doctor, that I don't want any more souls. Life is all I want."

"Life?"

"Yes, life. But not souls. I have no use for souls. They would only be a trouble to me."

*2 October.* We all met tonight to share our news. Jonathan told us what he had found out about the boxes. Van Helsing was very pleased.

"Excellent work, friend Jonathan," he said. "Now we know where the boxes are. Tomorrow we will go to the house in Piccadilly. It is the most important, because it is in the middle of London, where he can easily find victims. But first, we will go back to Carfax in the morning and make sure that the twenty-nine boxes there are made useless."

Before I went to bed, I passed Renfield's door. All was quiet. I heard him praying softly. Then I went to bed.

Late in the night, at about three o'clock, an attendant ran into my room. "Dr Seward!" he cried. "Come quickly! Renfield has had a terrible accident. He's badly hurt!"

## Chapter 21 — The Attack on Mina

**Dr Seward's Diary**

*3 October.* When I reached Renfield's room, I found him lying on the floor in a pool of blood. His face was badly cut, as if it had been beaten against the floor. His back was broken. I sent the attendant to call Van Helsing, and he came at once in his dressing gown. The others came soon after.

Van Helsing looked at Renfield's injuries and said quietly to me, "This is very bad. His back is broken, and there is an injury to the brain. We must operate at once, or he will die. But first I want him to speak. He knows something."

We operated quickly, and after a few minutes Renfield opened his eyes. He looked at us wildly and said, "I'll be quiet, doctor. Tell them to take off the straitjacket. I have had a terrible dream, and it has left me so weak that I cannot move. What's wrong with my face? It feels swollen."

Then he looked around and saw us. Suddenly he remembered. "It was no dream," he said. "It was all real. Quick, doctor, quick! I am dying! I feel that I have only a few minutes. Give me some water. I must tell you before I die."

Quincey gave him some brandy. He drank it and began to talk in a weak, fast voice.

"He came to me — the Master. Not just tonight. Many nights. He came up to my window in the mist, as I had often seen him before. But this time he was real. His eyes were fierce and red, like a man's when he is angry. He promised me things. He showed me rats — hundreds, thousands, millions of them — and dogs and cats, all of them alive, all of them full of blood and life. 'All these lives will I give you,' he said, 'and many more, through countless ages, if you will fall down and worship me!' And I said, 'Come in, Lord and Master!' And so I let him in.

"But then he did not give me what he promised. He didn't even send me a fly. And tonight he came in again, through the window, like mist. He did not even knock or ask. He just came in, as if he owned the place. And then Mrs Harker came in to see me this afternoon. She wasn't the same as before. She was pale, like tea when the water has been poured in too many times. I don't like pale people. I like them with lots of blood in them. Hers had all run out. I knew that he had been taking her life.

"So tonight, when he came, I was ready for him. I wanted to stop him. I had heard that madmen have great strength. I took hold of him and held on. I was winning — I didn't want him to take any more of her life — until I saw his eyes. They burned into me, and my strength became like water. He threw me down and slipped through my hands. When I tried to hold on to him, he lifted me up and threw me down on the floor. There was a red cloud before me, and a noise like thunder, and the mist seemed to go under the door."

His voice became weaker and weaker, and then he was silent. Van Helsing stood up. "Now we know the worst," he said. "He is here, and we know what he wants. Perhaps it is not too late. Take your weapons, as on the other night. Quickly! There is not a moment to lose."

We ran to our rooms and took our crucifixes, our Holy Wafers, our garlic and our weapons. Then we hurried up to the Harkers' bedroom door. Arthur and Quincey stopped. "Should we disturb her?" said Quincey.

"We must," said Van Helsing grimly. "If the door is locked, I shall break it in." He turned the handle, but the door did not open. We threw ourselves against it, and with a crash it burst open. We almost fell into the room.

What I saw filled me with horror. The moonlight was so bright that we could see everything. On the bed beside the window lay Jonathan Harker. His face was red, and he was breathing heavily, as if he were in a deep trance. Kneeling on the edge of the bed was the white-clad figure of his wife. By her side stood a tall, thin man, dressed in black. His face was turned away from us, but we all knew it was the Count.

With his left hand he held both of Mina's hands away from him. With his right hand he held the back of her neck and pushed her face against his chest. Her white nightdress was covered in blood, and blood was running down the man's bare chest through his open shirt. It looked horribly like a child pushing a kitten's nose into a bowl of milk to make it drink.

As we rushed in, the Count turned his face towards us. His eyes burned red with anger. His nostrils opened wide, and his sharp white teeth, behind his bloody lips, bit together like a wild animal's. He threw Mina back on the bed and jumped towards us.

But by this time the Professor had moved forward. He was holding out the envelope which contained the Holy Wafer. The Count suddenly stopped, just as poor Lucy had done outside the tomb, and stepped back. He went further and further back as we came towards him, lifting our crucifixes.

Then a great black cloud covered the moon. When Quincey lit the gas lamp, we saw only a thin mist, and it moved away under the door. We went to Mina. She took a breath and gave a scream so wild and so full of despair that I think I will hear it until the day I die.

For a few seconds she lay helpless. Her face was terrible to see. It was ghostly white, and her lips, cheeks and chin were covered in blood. From her throat ran a thin stream of blood. Her eyes were mad with terror. Then she put her poor hands in front of her face, and from behind them came a low cry of despair.

Van Helsing stepped forward and gently pulled the bedcovers over her body. Arthur looked at her face for a moment and then ran out of the room. Van Helsing whispered to me, "Jonathan is in a stupor such as we know the vampire can produce. We can do nothing with poor Madam Mina for a few moments till she recovers herself. I must wake him!"

He dipped the end of a towel in cold water and began to flick him with it on the face. Mina was crying in a way that was heartbreaking to hear. I raised the blind and looked out of the window. There was much moonshine, and as I looked, I could see Quincey Morris run across the lawn and hide himself in the shadow of a great yew tree. It puzzled me to think why he was doing this.

Then Jonathan woke up. He looked around in confusion. Then he jumped up. "In God's name, what does this mean?" he cried. "Dr Seward, Dr Van Helsing, what is it? What has happened? Mina, dear, what is it? What does that blood mean? My God, my God! Has it come to this!"

He was going to run out to find the Count, but Mina held him back. "No, no! Jonathan, you must not leave me. I have suffered enough tonight. You must stay with me. Stay with these friends who will watch over you!"

She pulled him down to sit on the bed beside her. Van Helsing and I tried to calm them both. Then Mina looked at Jonathan, and saw that his white nightshirt was stained with her blood. She drew back from him with a cry.

"Unclean, unclean!" she cried. "I must touch him or kiss him no more. Oh, that it should be that it is I who am now his worst enemy, and whom he may have most cause to fear."

Jonathan took her in his arms. "Nonsense, Mina," he said. "I will not hear you say that. May God judge me if anything comes between us!"

Then Arthur and Quincey came back. Arthur told us that the Count had not been seen anywhere in the house, but he had been in my study. He had burned all our papers — the typed copies of the diaries, and the phonograph cylinders. Luckily, there was another copy of everything in my safe. And Renfield was dead.

Quincey said, "I saw a bat rise from Renfield's window and fly off to the west. I expected to see it go towards Carfax, but it went somewhere else."

Then Van Helsing turned to Mina, very gently. "And now, Madam Mina — poor dear, dear Madam Mina — tell us exactly what happened. God knows that I do not want you to suffer more, but we must know everything. The end is near, if we work quickly."

Mina shook, but she spoke bravely. "I took the sleeping drug that you gave me," she said to me, "but it did not work for a long time. I could not sleep. Then I thought I saw the mist again, coming in under the door, just as before. Jonathan was asleep beside me. I tried to wake him, but I could not. I was terribly afraid. Then the mist became a tall, thin man, all in black. I knew him at once from Jonathan's description. I saw his red lips, his sharp white teeth and his red eyes. I wanted to scream, but I could not. He pointed at Jonathan and whispered, 'Silence! If you make a sound, I shall take him and dash his brains out before your eyes.'

"I could not move. He laughed and put his hand on my shoulder. He pulled my nightdress away from my throat and said, 'First, a little drink.' Oh my God, he put his horrible mouth on my throat! I don't know how long it lasted. I felt my strength going. When he took his mouth away, I saw that it was dripping with fresh blood.

"Then he spoke to me. 'So you, like the others, want to fight me with your clever brain. You want to help these men hunt me. They should have looked after you better! While they were making plans against me, I was making plans against them. And now you, the one they love most, are part of me — my flesh and my blood. You will be my helper. When my mind calls you, you will cross land and sea to obey me.'

"Then he opened his shirt and cut a vein in his chest with his long sharp nail. When the blood came out, he held my hands with one hand, and with the other he pushed my mouth against the wound. I had to swallow, or I could not breathe. Oh my God! My God! What have I done to deserve this?"

She began to rub her lips as if to clean them.

As she finished her story, the eastern sky began to grow red. Dawn was coming. Jonathan sat still beside her, his face grey and terrible. In one night his hair had turned white.

## Chapter 22 — The Burning Wafer

**Jonathan Harker's Journal**

*3 October.* I must write, or I will go mad. I must do something, and writing is all I can do now.

We have decided that Mina will not be kept in the dark any more. We will tell her everything. She was right: hiding things from her did not protect her. It only left her alone. Now she will be part of our work again. She is the bravest of us all.

At breakfast we made our plans. First, we must go to Carfax and destroy the earth in the twenty-nine boxes there. Then to Piccadilly, where the nine boxes are. Arthur and Quincey will find the boxes in Mile End and Bermondsey and destroy them too. We must do it all in daylight, while the Count is weakest.

Van Helsing explained: "In the daytime, he cannot change his shape. He is like a man then. If we find him in one of his houses, he cannot escape as mist or as a bat. And if we destroy all his boxes, he will have nowhere to rest."

Before we left, Van Helsing wanted to protect Mina. "I will put garlic and a crucifix in your room," he said, "and I will touch your forehead with this piece of the Holy Wafer, in the name of the Father, the Son and —"

There was a terrible scream. As the Wafer touched Mina's forehead, it burned her skin, as if it had been a piece of white-hot metal. My poor darling understood immediately what it meant. She fell to her knees on the floor, covered her face with her beautiful hair and cried out:

"Unclean! Unclean!" she cried. "Even God turns away from my body! I must wear this mark of shame on my forehead until the Day of Judgement."

We all stood in silence. There was a red scar on her forehead, where the Wafer had touched it. I knelt beside her and held her in my arms. The others turned away, with tears in their eyes.

Then Van Helsing spoke very seriously. "Perhaps you must wear that mark until the Day of Judgement, when God will make right all the wrongs of the world. But oh, Madam Mina, my dear — I hope we who love you will be there to see that red scar disappear, and your forehead become as pure as your heart. As sure as we live, that scar will go, when God decides to take this burden from us."

We all knelt and prayed together, and we each made a promise to free Mina from this curse.

Then we left for Carfax. The house was as we had left it. We went into the chapel, where the boxes were. Van Helsing opened each of the twenty-nine boxes. In each one he placed a piece of the Holy Wafer and then closed it again. Now the earth was sacred, and the Count could never rest in it again.

As we left, Van Helsing said, "So much is done already. If we are as successful with the other boxes, perhaps tonight's sunset will shine on Madam Mina's forehead and find it white as ivory, with no mark!"

As we walked across the lawn to the train station, we could see the hospital. I looked up at the window of my room and saw Mina. I waved to her and she waved back. Then we went to Piccadilly.

**Dr Seward's Diary**

*3 October.* Before we left, I went to see the body of poor Renfield. I felt terrible. He had tried to warn us, and I had not listened. He had begged me to let him go, and I had refused. If I had believed him, perhaps none of this would have happened. In the end, he died fighting the Count to protect Mina. He was a madman, but at the end he was a brave one.

## Chapter 23 — The Count Escapes

**Jonathan Harker's Journal**

*3 October, afternoon.* We did not want people in Piccadilly to see us breaking into a house. So Arthur, who is now a lord, called a locksmith and said that he had lost his keys. The locksmith opened the door without any questions. He thought that Arthur was the owner.

Inside, the house smelled like Carfax. In the dining room we found eight boxes of earth. One was missing! We opened them and put the Holy Wafer in each one, as we had done at Carfax.

On the table we found many interesting things: papers about the houses in Mile End and Bermondsey, a set of keys, and some of the Count's clothes. Arthur and Quincey took the keys and went at once to Mile End and Bermondsey, to destroy the boxes there. Van Helsing, Dr Seward and I stayed in the Piccadilly house to wait for the Count.

At about three o'clock, Arthur and Quincey sent us a telegram: they had found and destroyed all twelve boxes in Mile End and Bermondsey. So there was only one box left — the one missing from this house.

Then came a telegram from Mina:

> *Take care. He has just left Carfax hurriedly and gone south. He seems to be going round, and may want to see you.*

We waited. It was terrible, waiting there in that silent house. Arthur and Quincey came back at about half past four.

Suddenly we heard a key in the front door. We all got ready, standing on both sides of the room. Then the door of the room opened, and the Count jumped in, like a panther. He was so quick that none of us could stop him. He saw that we were waiting for him, and an evil smile came onto his face. His eyes went from one of us to the other. Then, suddenly, his face changed to a look of hate, and he tried to rush past us.

I had a big **kukri** knife — a curved knife from India — in my hand. I struck at him with all my strength. He jumped back just in time, but the knife cut through his coat and made a wide hole. A lot of banknotes and gold coins fell out onto the floor.

The Count's face was terrible. It was green-yellow, and his eyes burned red, and the red scar on his forehead — the one I had made with the shovel in the castle — stood out on his white skin. The next moment he bent down, grabbed some of the money from the floor, and rushed across the room. He threw himself at the window. The glass broke with a crash, and he jumped down into the stone yard below.

We ran to the window. He was getting up from the ground, unhurt. He turned and looked up at us.

"You think you can stop me," he shouted. "You, with your pale faces, all in a row, like sheep in a butcher's shop! You shall be sorry yet, each one of you! You think you have left me without a place to rest, but I have more. My revenge has only just begun! I spread it over centuries, and time is on my side. Your girls that you all love are mine already. And through them, you and others shall be mine too — my creatures, to do what I command, and to be my jackals when I want to feed. Bah!"

He laughed scornfully and ran out through the yard door. We heard the rusty bolt creak as he closed it behind him.

"We have learned a lot!" said Van Helsing. "He speaks bravely, but he is afraid of us. He is afraid of time, and afraid of being poor. Why else would he take that money? He has only one box of earth left in London, and he knows it. I think he will go back to his own country."

The sun was setting, so we could do nothing more tonight. We went back to the hospital. When Mina saw our faces, she knew that the Count had escaped. But she was very brave.

"We must not give up," she said. "And we must not hate him too much. Think how unhappy he must be. He is lost. When we destroy him, his soul will be free, just as poor Lucy's was. I pray that he may find peace too."

I felt angry when she said this. I wanted to kill him with my own hands. But she was right. She is always right.

**Dr Seward's Diary**

*3 October.* Late at night, Mina came to me with an idea. "I want Dr Van Helsing to **hypnotise** me," she said, "before the dawn. I think I can speak then, and that I can speak freely. I am connected to the Count now. Perhaps I can tell you where he is."

Van Helsing thought it was a very good idea. "Yes," he said. "At sunrise and sunset he is not free. He is between day and night. Perhaps then Madam Mina can see what he sees."

## Chapter 24 — The Chase Begins

**Dr Seward's Phonograph Diary, spoken by Van Helsing**

*4 October.* This is for Jonathan Harker, who must stay with his dear Madam Mina today.

Just before dawn, Madam Mina called me. I hypnotised her. She went into a trance quickly, and I asked her, "Where are you?"

She answered slowly, as if she were very far away: "I do not know. It is all strange to me."

"What do you see?"

"I can see nothing. It is all dark."

"What do you hear?"

"The lapping of water. It is gurgling by, and little waves leap. I can hear them on the outside."

"Then you are on a ship?"

"Oh yes! I hear men running about on the deck above me. I hear a chain creaking, and the loud metal sound of the **capstan** turning."

Then the sun rose, and she woke up. She did not remember anything.

So he is on a ship! He is leaving England with his last box of earth. He is going home. Friend Arthur went at once to Lloyd's, the shipping office, and asked about all the ships that left London last night. There was only one going to the Black Sea: the *Czarina Catherine*, sailing from Doolittle's Wharf to Varna.

Arthur and Quincey went to Doolittle's Wharf. The men there remembered a tall, thin, pale man with very red lips and sharp teeth. He had come to the ship in the afternoon with a big box on a cart. He lifted the box by himself onto the ship — something which usually takes several men. He paid the captain well. Then a thick fog came up suddenly, though the day had been clear. In the morning the ship had gone, at the turn of the tide.

So now we know. The Count has left England. He has escaped to his own land, and he will be there long before us, because the ship will take three weeks to reach Varna. But we can travel by train, and get to Varna in three days. We will be there before him.

**Mina Harker's Journal**

*5 October.* Today I asked the Professor why we must follow the Count. He has gone. Why not let him go?

Van Helsing looked at me very seriously. Then he looked at the red scar on my forehead. "Because," he said gently, "he has poisoned you with his blood. He can live for centuries, and you are only mortal. When you die, you will become like him — unless we destroy him first. And now that he has your blood, and you have his, he can call you to him at any time."

I understood. We must follow him, for my sake.

Then the Professor explained something else. "The Count is clever, but he has a child-brain in some ways. He has not yet learned everything about the modern world. He makes the same mistakes again and again. And there is something more. Madam Mina, you have the power to see what he sees when you are hypnotised. But perhaps he can see what you see too — and hear what you hear. So we must be careful. We must not tell you our plans, in case he learns them through you."

This frightened me. I said at once, "Then you must not tell me anything. I agree. I do not want to help him, even without knowing it."

**Jonathan Harker's Journal**

*5 October, evening.* We had a long meeting. Mina was not there, as she had asked. We decided to go to Varna by the Orient Express. Arthur, Quincey, Seward, Van Helsing and I will all go. And Mina will come with us, because she must be hypnotised every morning and evening to tell us where the Count is.

We will take weapons: rifles, revolvers and knives. Van Helsing will bring crucifixes, garlic and the Holy Wafer. Quincey insisted on bringing his big Winchester rifles. "There may be wolves in those mountains," he said, "and I don't like the idea of meeting them without a good gun."

I am happy that we are doing something at last. But I am very worried about Mina. She has a strange look in her eyes sometimes, especially at sunrise and sunset. And her teeth seem a little sharper. I hope it is only my imagination.

## Chapter 25 — Mina's Request

**Dr Seward's Diary**

*11 October, evening.* Jonathan Harker has asked me to write this, because he says he is not strong enough to do it himself.

This evening, at sunset, Mina asked us all to meet her. She looked very serious. "I want you to promise me something," she said. "All of you. It is very important."

We looked at each other. "What is it?" asked Quincey.

"When I die — and I may die before this is over — you must not let me become like Lucy. If you see that I am changing, if you see that I am becoming one of the Un-Dead, you must kill me. Drive a stake through my heart, as you did for her. Promise me!"

Nobody spoke for a long moment. Then Quincey stood up, knelt in front of her and took her hand. "I promise," he said. "I'm only a rough fellow, and I'm not sure I deserve such an honour. But I swear to you, by everything that is sacred to me, that if the time comes, I will do my duty."

"My true friend!" said Mina, and she cried as she bent and kissed his hand.

"I swear the same, my dear Madam Mina," said Van Helsing.

"And I," said Arthur. "And I," I said.

Then Jonathan turned to her. His face was grey, and his white hair looked whiter than ever. "And must I, too, make such a promise, my wife?"

"You too, my dearest," she said, with deep love and pity in her eyes. "You must not be afraid. You are everything in the world to me. Our souls are joined for ever. In the past, brave men sometimes killed the women they loved, to save them from their enemies. If it must happen, let it be the hand of the man who loves me most. Oh, Jonathan, if I must die, let it be your hand."

Jonathan could not speak. He only nodded and held her in his arms.

Then Mina said one more thing. "I want you to read the Burial Service now," she said, "over me. In case I die before I can hear it at my own funeral."

Jonathan read it in a shaking voice. It was the strangest and saddest thing I have ever seen. I could not stop my tears.

**Jonathan Harker's Journal**

*15 October, Varna.* We left London on the 12th and travelled by train through Paris and on the Orient Express. We arrived in Varna this afternoon. The *Czarina Catherine* has not arrived yet. We are in time!

Every morning and every evening, Van Helsing hypnotises Mina. Each time she says the same thing: "Water lapping. Darkness. Creaking wood." The Count is still at sea.

Arthur has spoken to the shipping agent here. When the *Czarina Catherine* arrives, we will be told at once. We have arranged with the authorities that we may go on board and open the Count's box. If he is in his box during the day, we can destroy him there.

*17 October.* Everything is ready. Van Helsing has brought a large knife and a wild-rose branch. If we put the rose on the box, the Count cannot get out.

*24 October.* A whole week of waiting. Every day we hear the same news from Lloyd's: the ship has not arrived. And every day Mina says the same thing: still at sea, still in darkness.

But her trances are changing. Each time, it is harder for Van Helsing to hypnotise her, and she says less. I am afraid. Is the Count blocking her from us?

*28 October.* Arthur received a telegram from Lloyd's in London:

> *Czarina Catherine reported entering Galatz at one o'clock today.*

Galatz! Not Varna! The ship has gone to another port, up the Danube river. The Count has tricked us! He must have heard our plans through Mina's mind, and told the captain to change his course. He has made the winds and the fog to help him.

We were all shocked. Van Helsing was the first to speak. "Then we go to Galatz," he said calmly. "The next train leaves tomorrow morning. We will be there in one day."

Mina said nothing. She sat with her hands in her lap. Then she looked up at us with her clear eyes. "I am sorry," she whispered. "I think it is my fault. He knows what I know."

"No, Madam Mina," said Van Helsing gently. "It is not your fault. You have been the bravest of us all. And you have given us a weapon too. If he can read your mind, you can read his. We will win this fight."

## Chapter 26 — Galatz

**Dr Seward's Diary**

*29 October.* We arrived at Galatz too late. The *Czarina Catherine* was already in the harbour. We went to see the captain, Captain Donelson. He was a Scot, and he told us a strange story.

"It was the strangest voyage of my life," he said. "The wind was always behind us. We went faster than ever before. And there was fog all the time, whenever we were near land. When we passed the Dardanelles, the fog was so thick that we could not see anything. The crew were afraid. They said there was a devil on board, and they wanted to throw a big box into the sea — the one belonging to a strange old man in London. But I would not let them. Then we arrived here in Galatz, and in the morning a man came with papers for the box. His name was Immanuel Hildesheim. He took it away."

We found Hildesheim. He was a merchant. He told us that he had received a letter from London with money, asking him to collect the box and give it to a man named Petrof Skinsky. Skinsky was a trader who worked with the Slovaks who carry goods along the rivers.

We went to find Skinsky, but we were too late. He had been found dead that morning in a churchyard, with his throat torn open as if by a wild animal.

The Count had killed him, so that he could tell us nothing!

**Mina Harker's Journal**

*30 October.* The men were tired and upset, so I asked them to let me think. I took all the information we have and studied it carefully, like a puzzle. Then I wrote a memorandum.

*Memorandum by Mina Harker:*

*The question is: how will the Count travel to his castle?*

*(a) By road? There are too many difficulties. People would see him. There are customs officers. And he is afraid of being discovered.*

*(b) By railway? He would have to stay in a train with no one to look after him. It is too dangerous.*

*(c) By water? This is the safest way for him. But it is also dangerous, because he cannot cross running water by himself. In his box, however, he can be carried on water by other people.*

*He wanted to be taken to Galatz, near the Danube. From there, the Slovaks could carry him by river. Which river? I have looked at the maps. The River Pruth and the River Sereth both flow into the Danube near Galatz. The Sereth is joined by the River Bistritza, which goes up into the mountains near the Borgo Pass. Castle Dracula is very near there.*

*So I think the Count's box is on a boat on the River Sereth, going towards the Bistritza, and then towards the castle.*

When I showed this to the men, they were very excited. Van Helsing kissed my hand. "Our dear Madam Mina is once more our teacher," he said. "Her eyes have seen where ours were blinded."

Then we made our plans. We decided to split up into three groups:

- Arthur and Jonathan will take a fast steam boat — a **steam launch** — up the river, to try to catch the Count's boat.
- Dr Seward and Quincey will ride on horses along the river bank, in case the Count leaves the river.
- Van Helsing and I will go by train to Veresti, and then by carriage to the Borgo Pass and the castle. If the others do not catch the Count, we will be waiting for him at his home.

Jonathan did not want me to go to the castle. He was afraid. But Van Helsing said, "Madam Mina must come. Her connection to the Count will help us find him. And I will protect her. I promise."

At last Jonathan agreed. He took my hand. "Be careful, my darling," he said. "Come back to me."

**Jonathan Harker's Journal**

*31 October.* We are on the steam launch, moving up the river at full speed. Arthur and I take turns watching the river and sleeping. We have our guns ready.

*1 November.* We have seen many boats, and we stopped some of them, but none had the box. At one village, people told us that a big boat with Slovak men had passed, going quickly up the river.

*2 November, morning.* The river is getting narrower. Our launch has had some problems with its engine, and we have lost some time. But we are not far behind them now. I think of Mina all the time. God protect her!

**Dr Seward's Diary**

*2 November.* Quincey and I have been riding for three days. We have seen nothing yet. The country is wild and beautiful, but cold. Snow is beginning to fall. We must hurry.

## Chapter 27 — The End of the Count

**Mina Harker's Journal**

*1 November.* The Professor and I travelled all day by carriage. The country is beautiful, but the people seem afraid. When they see the red scar on my forehead, they make the sign of the cross and point two fingers at me, to protect themselves against the evil eye.

I feel strange. In the daytime I am tired and want to sleep all the time. But at night I am wide awake. Each morning and evening, the Professor tries to hypnotise me, but it is harder and harder. The last time, I only said, "Darkness. Water. Wood creaking."

*3 November.* Today we reached the Borgo Pass. Jonathan described this place in his journal, but now I seem to know it myself. I knew which road to take without looking at the map. The Professor watched me carefully, but said nothing.

We stopped for the night in the snow, near the castle. It was very cold. The Professor built a fire. Then he did a strange thing. He drew a circle in the snow all around me and the fire, and he put pieces of the Holy Wafer around it. He also put garlic on the horses.

"Will you not come closer to the fire, Madam Mina?" he asked. I tried to stand up and move, but I could not. My feet would not move across the circle. He looked very happy and said quietly, "Good. Now I know you are safe."

**Memorandum by Abraham Van Helsing**

*4 November.* This is for my old and true friend John Seward, in case I do not see him again.

Last night, Madam Mina could not cross the circle I made with the Holy Wafer. That gives me hope — and fear. Hope, because I know that inside the circle she is safe. Fear, because it shows that the Count's poison is working in her.

In the middle of the night, the snow began to fall more thickly, and a mist rose. Then I saw shapes in the snow. They were three women — the same ones that Jonathan saw in the castle. They were beautiful and terrible, with red lips and sharp white teeth. They came close to the circle and smiled at Madam Mina.

"Come, sister," they said, in their sweet voices. "Come to us. Come! Come!"

I was afraid that she would go to them. But when I looked at her, I saw only horror in her eyes. Thank God! She is not yet one of them. The women came closer, but they could not cross the circle of the Holy Wafer. They moved round and round until the morning came. Then they disappeared. The horses were all dead.

In the morning, Madam Mina was asleep, and I could not wake her. I left her inside the circle, where she was safe, and went up to the castle. I took my tools with me.

The castle was just as Jonathan described it: dark and ruined. I found the old chapel. There, in three tombs, lay the three women, asleep in their Un-Death. They were so beautiful that I nearly stopped my work. I felt that same strange power that Jonathan described. But then I remembered Madam Mina, and I did my terrible duty.

One by one, I drove the stake through their hearts and cut off their heads. As I did it, they screamed and twisted. But afterwards, their faces changed, and they looked peaceful. In a few moments, their bodies turned to dust. They had been dead for centuries.

Then I found a great tomb, larger and grander than the others. On it was written one word: DRACULA. It was empty. This was the home of the King-Vampire. I put the Holy Wafer inside it, so that he could never rest there again. Then I put pieces of the Wafer in all the doors of the castle, so that he could never enter it.

When I went back to Madam Mina, she was awake. She ran to me and cried, "Come! Come away from this terrible place! Let us go to meet my husband. I know he is coming towards us."

**Mina Harker's Journal**

*6 November.* It was late in the afternoon. We had been walking for hours in the snow, and we were very tired. We had come to a high rock, from where we could see far across the valley. The Professor used his telescope.

Suddenly he cried, "Look! Look, Madam Mina!" Far below, on the road, there was a group of men on horses, with a cart. On the cart was a big wooden box. The men were the Szgany — the gypsies who work for the Count. They were riding fast towards the castle.

The sun was going down. If they reached the castle before sunset, the Count would be free. He would get out of his box, and we could not stop him.

Then I saw two other men on horses, riding fast from the south. And from the north came two more. It was Jonathan and Arthur on one side, and Dr Seward and Quincey on the other! They were trying to stop the gypsies before the sun set.

The gypsies saw them too. They stopped and pulled out their knives and guns. The two groups of our friends rode up and surrounded them.

"Halt!" shouted Jonathan and Quincey. Arthur and Dr Seward pointed their rifles at the gypsies.

The leader of the gypsies shouted something to his men, and they put themselves around the cart. But Jonathan and Quincey did not stop. They pushed their way through the circle of gypsies. Jonathan was very fierce. I saw the gypsies move back from him. He jumped onto the cart and, with a strength that seemed incredible, lifted the great box and threw it to the ground.

At the same moment, I saw Quincey fight his way to the cart from the other side. The gypsies were attacking him with their knives. He pushed through them, but I saw that he was holding his hand to his side, and blood was coming through his fingers.

Jonathan used his big kukri knife to break open the lid of the box. Quincey used his bowie knife on the other side. The lid came off.

The Count was lying in the box, on the earth. His face was deadly pale, like a wax figure, and his red eyes looked at the setting sun with a terrible look of hate. And then the look changed to one of triumph, because the sun was nearly down.

But at that moment Jonathan struck. His great knife cut through the Count's throat. At the same moment, Quincey's bowie knife went deep into his heart.

It was like a miracle. In less than a breath, the whole body fell into dust and disappeared. And I will be glad for the rest of my life that, in that last moment, I saw a look of peace on his face — something I never thought I could see there.

The castle stood against the red sky, and every stone of its broken walls was clear in the last light of the setting sun.

The gypsies were terrified. They turned and rode away without a word.

Quincey had fallen to the ground. He was holding his side, and blood was pouring through his fingers. I ran to him, and so did the others. Arthur held him in his arms. With a great effort, he took my hand.

"I am only too happy to have been of any service," he said. Then he pointed at me suddenly. "Oh God!" he cried. "It was worth for this to die! Look! Look!"

The sun was just setting, and its red light fell on my face. All the men fell on their knees and said "Amen." Quincey spoke again, weakly: "Thank God it was not all for nothing! Look! Her forehead is as white as the snow! The curse has gone!"

The red scar on my forehead had disappeared.

And then, to our great sadness, he smiled and died in silence — a brave and noble gentleman.

**Note**

*Seven years later.*

Seven years have passed since those terrible days. Some of us have found great happiness since then, and we think it was worth all the pain. Mina and I have a son. He was born on the same day that Quincey Morris died. Mina believes that some of our brave friend's spirit lives in him. He has the names of all of our group of friends, but we call him Quincey.

This summer we travelled to Transylvania and visited the places that are so full of terrible memories for us. It was almost impossible to believe that everything we saw and heard there had really happened. Every sign of it had gone. Only the castle still stood high above the empty land.

When we came home, we talked about the old days. We took all the papers out of the safe, where they have been since we returned. We noticed that almost none of them are original documents. Most are only typed copies, apart from a few notebooks and Van Helsing's memorandum. Nobody would accept these as proof of such a wild story.

Van Helsing summed it all up as he said, with our boy on his knee:

"We do not need proof. We do not ask anyone to believe us! One day this boy will know what a brave woman his mother is. He already knows how sweet and loving she is. Later he will understand that some men loved her so much that they risked everything for her."

JONATHAN HARKER

*THE END*
