## Part 19 — The Trial

The day of the trial arrived. The whole region was talking about it. A young, handsome man, a former seminary student, had tried to kill the wife of the mayor of Verrières, in a church, during mass! It was like a novel. People came from all over to see the trial. The courtroom was full. Many fine ladies were there, dressed in their best clothes, and many of them cried when they saw the young prisoner.

Julien was brought in by two policemen. He was pale but calm, and very elegant in his black clothes. When the ladies saw how young and handsome he was, they whispered with pity.

Mathilde had done everything possible to save him. She had bribed many people. Father de Frilair had promised her that the jury would be kind to Julien. But there was one problem: the foreman of the jury — the man who would lead the jury and announce the verdict — was Monsieur Valenod. He had become very important and rich. He was now the new mayor of Verrières. And he hated Julien.

The lawyer for the prosecution spoke for a long time. He said that Julien was a dangerous young man, ambitious and immoral, who had seduced his employer's wife and then tried to kill her. The lawyer for the defence spoke too. He said that Julien had acted in a moment of madness, and that the jury should have mercy on him.

**Julien's Speech**

Then the judge asked Julien if he wished to say anything. Julien stood up. The courtroom was silent.

"Gentlemen of the jury," he said, in a clear, calm voice. "I do not ask for your mercy. I am not deceiving myself. I deserve death. I tried to kill the most worthy woman in the world, a woman who had been like a mother to me. My crime was horrible, and it was planned. I deserve to die.

"But even if I were less guilty, I see men here who would want to punish me anyway. They do not care about my crime. They care about who I am. I see in front of me men who want to punish, in me, a class of young men — young men born poor, in a humble class, who have had the good luck to get an education, and who have dared to mix with what the rich call 'good society'.

"That is my real crime, gentlemen. And it will be punished more severely because I am not being judged by my equals. I do not see any rich peasants on this jury. I see only rich men who are angry that a carpenter's son has dared to rise above his class."

The courtroom was in an uproar. The ladies cried. Some men were angry. Monsieur Valenod went red.

Julien sat down. He had spoken for twenty minutes. He knew that his words had destroyed any hope of mercy. But he had said the truth, and he was proud of it.

**The Verdict**

The jury left the room to decide. It was late in the evening. The people waited, whispering. Hours passed. At last, at about one o'clock in the morning, the jury came back.

Monsieur Valenod stood up, very seriously, and read the verdict. Julien was guilty of attempted murder, with planning. The judge then announced the sentence: death.

Julien looked at Valenod and smiled. "Well," he thought, "Valenod has his revenge. Now he will marry Madame de Rênal when her husband dies. He has wanted her for years. How he must be enjoying this!"

In the courtroom, many women were crying. Mathilde, hidden among the crowd, fainted.

Madame de Rênal was not there. She had been too ill to come. When she heard the verdict, she fell to the floor, and for a long time she could not speak.
