## Part 3 — The Tutor Arrives

Madame de Rênal was coming out of the drawing room into the garden, with the lightness and grace that were natural to her when nobody was watching. Near the front door, she saw a young peasant. He was very young, and very pale, and he had been crying. He was wearing a clean white shirt and carried a jacket of purple cloth under his arm.

The boy was so pale and gentle that Madame de Rênal, who was a little romantic, thought at first that it was a young girl in disguise, who had come to ask the mayor for some favour. She felt sorry for the poor creature, who seemed afraid to ring the bell. She went towards him.

Julien, who was looking at the door, did not see her coming. He jumped when a soft voice said, near his ear: "What do you want here, my child?"

He turned round quickly, and he was so struck by her kind, gentle look that he forgot his shyness. He forgot everything, even why he had come. He saw only her beauty. She repeated her question.

"I have come to be the tutor, madame," he said at last, ashamed of his tears, which he tried to wipe away.

Madame de Rênal could not speak. They were very close to each other, looking at each other. Julien had never seen a person so well dressed, especially a woman with such a fine, white complexion, speaking to him so gently. Madame de Rênal looked at the big tears on the young man's cheeks, which had been so pale and were now so pink. Then she began to laugh, with the wild happiness of a young girl. She laughed at herself, and she could not believe her happiness. So this was the tutor she had imagined as a dirty, rough priest who would shout at her children!

"What, sir?" she said at last. "You know Latin?"

The word "sir" surprised Julien so much that he thought for a moment. "Yes, madame," he said shyly.

Madame de Rênal was so happy that she said: "You will not be too hard on my poor boys?"

"I? Hard on them? Why?"

"You will be kind to them, won't you, sir?" she said, more gently. "You promise me?"

To hear himself called "sir" again, seriously, by such a well-dressed lady, was more than Julien had ever hoped. In his dreams of glory, he had always told himself that no fine lady would speak to him until he wore a splendid uniform. Madame de Rênal, for her part, was completely charmed by his beautiful complexion, his big black eyes and his pretty hair, which was curling more than usual because he had put his head in the cold water of the fountain to cool it.

"What is your name, sir?" she asked, in a sweet voice.

"Julien Sorel, madame. I am trembling as I enter a strange house for the first time in my life. I need your protection, and you must forgive me for many things in the first days. I have never been to school; I was too poor. I have only spoken to my cousin, the army surgeon, and to Father Chélan. My brothers always beat me. Don't believe them if they speak badly of me. Forgive my faults, madame. I will never have bad intentions."

Then she led him into the house.

**The Latin Test**

Monsieur de Rênal came and looked at Julien with a critical eye. He took him to his room and gave him a new black suit. "You must never see your family again," he said. "Their manners are not suitable for my children."

When Julien came back wearing his new black clothes, he looked like a different person — serious and dignified, like a young priest. Monsieur de Rênal brought in the three little boys and introduced them.

"Gentlemen," Julien said to the children, with great seriousness, "I am here to teach you Latin. You know what it is to recite a lesson. Here is the Holy Bible. Open it anywhere, and read me the first three words of a paragraph. I will recite the whole page by heart, until you tell me to stop."

The eldest boy, Adolphe, opened the book and read three words. Julien recited the whole page in Latin, as easily as if he were speaking French. Monsieur de Rênal looked at his wife proudly, as if to say: "You see what a tutor I have found!" The children were amazed. The servants came to the door to listen. Then some friends of the family arrived, and Julien did it again for them. Soon the whole town was talking about the mayor's wonderful tutor.

**Life in the House**

In less than a month, Julien had won the respect of everyone in the house. The children loved him. He did not love them, but he was patient, fair and serious with them, and he never beat them. He was cold and polite to Monsieur de Rênal, whom he secretly despised. He hated the rich, and he felt that everyone in this house looked down on him because he was poor. When the family laughed at something, Julien thought they were laughing at him.

Madame de Rênal, on the other hand, was very kind to him. She noticed that he had very few clothes. One day, she gave him some money — a few gold coins — and said, "Buy yourself some new shirts. But please don't tell my husband."

Julien's pride was terribly hurt. He went red and stood up very straight. "Madame, I am poor, but I am not a servant," he said coldly. "I cannot accept money from you secretly. It would be lacking in respect towards your husband."

Madame de Rênal was surprised and hurt. She had only wanted to be kind. But she admired him even more for his pride. Instead, she bought the shirts for him with her own money, and paid for books for the children, which Julien could use.

**Elisa**

Madame de Rênal's maid, Elisa, was a young woman who had just received a small inheritance. She fell in love with Julien and told Father Chélan that she wanted to marry him. Father Chélan called Julien and told him about it. It was a good chance for a poor young man: Elisa had money.

But Julien refused. He did not want to marry a servant. He wanted much more from life. "I want to become a priest," he told Father Chélan.

The old priest looked at him with doubt. "My son," he said, "I am afraid you do not have a true calling to the Church. I see in you a dark fire that does not come from God. Think carefully before you choose."

When Madame de Rênal heard that Julien had refused to marry Elisa, she felt a strange joy. She did not understand why. She was so innocent that she did not yet know she was in love.
