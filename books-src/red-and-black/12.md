## Part 12 — Mathilde

Mathilde de La Mole was the most admired young woman in Paris. She was beautiful, rich and of the highest nobility. Many young noblemen wanted to marry her, and one of them, the Marquis de Croisenois, was almost certainly going to be her husband. He was rich, handsome, polite and very boring.

Mathilde was bored too. She was bored by everything: the balls, the parties, the compliments, the young men who were all exactly the same. She was very intelligent, and she read many books — including books that a young lady was not supposed to read. She despised the people around her. She felt that she had been born in the wrong century.

**The Thirtieth of April**

One day, Julien noticed that Mathilde was dressed completely in black. He asked a friend why.

"It's the thirtieth of April," said the friend. "Every year on this day, she wears black. It is a family story. In 1574, one of her ancestors, Boniface de La Mole, was the lover of Queen Marguerite of Navarre. He was involved in a plot against the King, and he was arrested and beheaded in Paris, on the thirtieth of April. That night, Queen Marguerite went secretly to the place of execution. She asked for his head, and she carried it away in her carriage, and buried it herself. Mademoiselle de La Mole admires that story. She thinks that it was the time of great passions, when people really lived. She wears black for him every year."

Julien was interested. "So she is not like the others," he thought.

**The Ball**

Some weeks later, there was a great ball at the house of the Duke de Retz. Julien went with the family. The rooms were full of light, flowers and music. Everybody was looking at Mathilde, who was the most beautiful woman at the ball.

But Mathilde was bored. She refused to dance. She watched the people around her with a cold, mocking smile. Then she noticed Julien. He was standing in a corner, talking to a foreign gentleman — Count Altamira, an Italian. Count Altamira had led a revolution in his country. It had failed, and he had been condemned to death. He had escaped to Paris, where he lived in exile.

Mathilde went closer to listen.

"Yes," Count Altamira was saying, "a death sentence. It is the only thing that cannot be bought. Everything else in our time — titles, honours, positions — everything can be bought with money. But nobody can buy a death sentence. It is the only distinction that is still real."

Julien listened with passion. Mathilde watched Julien's face, and she saw something in his eyes that she had never seen in the eyes of the young men around her: fire, energy, courage. "He is not like the others," she thought. "He is not afraid. He could be a Danton, a leader of a revolution. He could be condemned to death one day."

From that moment, she looked at Julien differently.

**In the Library**

In the following days, Mathilde often came to the library, where Julien was working. She asked him questions about books and history. She wanted to know what he thought. Julien answered coldly and politely. He did not like her. He thought she was proud and wanted to play with him, like a cat with a mouse.

But she continued to come. She talked to him about the past, about the great days when men fought and died for their ideas. She told him about Boniface de La Mole. Julien found that she was very intelligent, and he began to enjoy their conversations.

He also noticed that she treated him in a strange way. Sometimes she was friendly, almost warm. Sometimes she was cold and proud, as if she wanted to remind him that he was only a servant. He did not understand her.

**The Young Noblemen**

The young noblemen who came to the house — the Marquis de Croisenois, Count de Caylus, Count de Luz — did not like Julien. They thought he was strange, too serious and too proud. They called him "the little priest". They were polite to him, because he was the Marquis's secretary, but they did not treat him as an equal.

Mathilde compared them with Julien. "They are all so polite and so empty," she thought. "They would never do anything great or dangerous. They are afraid of everything. They only want to be admired. But Julien — he despises them, I can see it in his eyes. He is poor, but he is proud. He has a great soul. He is the only man here who is really alive."

And one day, alone in her room, Mathilde said to herself, with a kind of joy: "I love him. Yes, I love him! I, Mathilde de La Mole, am in love with my father's secretary — a carpenter's son! What a great, dangerous, extraordinary love! It is like a novel. It is worthy of Queen Marguerite!"

She did not love him the way Madame de Rênal had loved him — simply and completely, with her whole heart. She loved him with her head. She loved the idea of loving him. She loved the danger and the drama of it.
