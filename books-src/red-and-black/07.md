## Part 7 — The Anonymous Letter

Elisa had seen many things. She had noticed that Julien and Madame de Rênal often walked alone together. She had noticed the looks between them. And she was jealous and angry, because Julien had refused to marry her. So she went to Monsieur Valenod and told him everything she suspected.

Valenod was delighted. He hated the mayor. He also had another reason to hate Madame de Rênal: years before, he had tried to court her, and she had refused him. Now he had a chance for revenge. He wrote a letter without signing it — an **anonymous letter** — and sent it to Monsieur de Rênal. The letter said that his wife was in love with the young tutor.

When Monsieur de Rênal read the letter, he was very upset. He walked up and down in his room all night. He did not know what to do. Was it true? He did not really believe it. He had never imagined that his wife could love anyone, and especially not a poor peasant. But he could not forget the letter.

He was not jealous like a man in love. He was jealous like a man who is afraid of being laughed at. "If this is true," he thought, "the whole town will laugh at me. I will be ridiculous." Then he thought of other things. If he sent his wife away, he would lose her money. She was going to inherit a large fortune from an old aunt. If he fought Julien in a duel, everyone would know. Either way, he would lose.

**A Plan**

Madame de Rênal saw that her husband was troubled. She soon found out about the letter. She was frightened, but she was also brave. She loved Julien, and she would do anything to protect him. With a cleverness that surprised Julien, she made a plan.

"We will make a second anonymous letter," she said. "A letter to me this time, that looks like it comes from the same person. Cut out words from a book and stick them on a piece of paper. I will show it to my husband, and I will be very angry. I will say that someone is trying to destroy our family, and I will ask him to send you away. Then he will think it is all a plot by his enemies, and that I am innocent."

Julien made the letter. He cut out words from an old book and stuck them on a page. Madame de Rênal showed it to her husband with tears of anger.

"Look," she said. "Someone has sent this horrible letter to me. It is an insult! I am sure it is Monsieur Valenod. He has always hated you, and he wants to make you look ridiculous. Send the young man away, if you like. But don't let these people think that they can frighten the mayor of Verrières!"

Monsieur de Rênal was confused. His wife was so angry, and she spoke so honestly, that he began to believe her. He decided that the letters were only a trick by his enemies. He did not send Julien away. But he was still uneasy, and the gossip in the town did not stop.

**Dinner at Valenod's**

During this time, Julien was invited to dinner at Monsieur Valenod's house. Valenod wanted to take him away from the mayor and have him as tutor for his own children. Julien went, because he wanted to see what kind of man Valenod was.

The house was large and full of expensive furniture, all new and in bad taste. The dinner was rich, with fine wines. Valenod was loud and vulgar. He boasted about his money, his horses and his friends in high places. The guests were all rich men who laughed at his jokes.

The poor-house, which Valenod managed, was next door. During the dinner, Julien heard the poor people singing in the next building. Valenod became angry and sent a servant to stop them. "Those beggars are making too much noise," he said.

Julien felt sick. These poor people had nothing, and Valenod was growing rich with the money meant for them. Here he was, eating and drinking in luxury, while they were hungry next door. Julien almost wept. But he hid his feelings and was very polite. He was learning to be a hypocrite.

**Father Chélan's Decision**

But the rumours about Julien and Madame de Rênal continued to grow. Everyone in Verrières was talking about them. At last, Father Chélan called Julien.

"My son," he said seriously, "I do not want to know what is true and what is not. But for your own good, and for the good of others, you must leave Verrières. You must go to the seminary at Besançon to study for the priesthood. If you do not want to do that, then go to your friend Fouqué and become a merchant. But you cannot stay here."

Julien knew that the old priest was right. He agreed to go to the seminary.

When Madame de Rênal heard the news, she was in despair. But she understood that it was the only way. Monsieur de Rênal, too, was secretly relieved.

On the last night, Julien went to her room. They did not speak much. She was pale and cold, and she held him in silence. She cried, but she did not complain. She seemed to have already said goodbye to life. Julien was surprised and hurt by her coldness. He did not understand that she was trying not to break down completely.

The next morning, he left Verrières. As the carriage went down the road, he looked back at the house. He thought of her, and he felt a great pain in his heart.
