## Part 9 — The Seminary

Life in the seminary was hard. Every day was the same. The students got up at five o'clock in the morning, prayed, went to classes, prayed again, ate in silence, and went to bed at nine. They wore long black robes, and they walked with their eyes on the ground. There were about three hundred of them.

Julien had expected to meet clever, serious young men, who loved learning and God. He was disappointed. Most of the students were sons of poor peasants, like himself. But they did not want to become priests because they loved God. They wanted to become priests because a priest always has enough to eat. For them, the Church was a way to escape from hard work in the fields. They were ignorant and lazy, and they were very good at one thing: pretending to be holy.

Julien soon found that his intelligence was a danger here. When he answered questions well in class, the other students hated him. When he read books, they said he was proud. They called him "Martin Luther", after the great heretic, because he thought too much. Nobody here wanted a student who thought. The ideal student was one who obeyed, prayed and believed without asking questions.

**Learning to Pretend**

So Julien decided to change. He studied the other students carefully, as an actor studies a role. He noticed how they walked, how they held their heads, how they lowered their eyes. He noticed that the most respected students were those who ate their food with the most humility, and who looked at the crucifix with the most devotion.

He practised walking like them. He practised looking stupid. He even practised the way they ate their eggs at breakfast. But it was very difficult. He could not hide the fire in his eyes. After many months, he was still not good at it.

One day, while he was eating, he looked up at the window and saw a beautiful blue sky. His face showed his joy for a moment. A teacher saw him. "Sorel," he said, "you look at the sky like a pagan. A true Christian thinks of heaven, not of the sky."

Everyone watched him. There were spies everywhere. One of the teachers, Father Castanède, was the chief of the spies. He encouraged the students to report everything they saw. He wanted to destroy Father Pirard, whom he hated, and he knew that Julien was Father Pirard's favourite.

Julien received no letters. Madame de Rênal wrote to him, but her letters were stopped. He did not know if she had forgotten him.

**Father Pirard**

Father Pirard was the only person who understood Julien. He was a strict man, but honest. He belonged to a group of priests called Jansenists, who believed in a pure and strict religion. He hated the hypocrisy and the politics of the Church, and he had many enemies.

He watched Julien carefully. One day, he called him to his room and gave him a job: he would be the tutor for the New and Old Testament for the other students. It was a great honour. The other students hated Julien even more.

Then the examinations came. Julien was the best student in the seminary. But in the oral examination, a priest asked him a trick question about a pagan writer, Horace. Julien, who loved Horace, answered with enthusiasm and quoted many lines. The examiners smiled and wrote down that Julien knew pagan writers better than the Bible. They gave him a low mark — the 198th place out of all the students! It was an injustice, done to hurt Father Pirard.

**Corpus Christi**

On the festival of Corpus Christi, Julien was sent to help decorate the cathedral of Besançon. He worked high up on a ladder, hanging red silk curtains on the great columns. He was brave and skilful, and the old priest in charge of the cathedral was delighted with him.

During the ceremony, Julien was alone near a chapel of the cathedral. He saw two ladies kneeling at a prayer stool. One of them turned her head. It was Madame de Rênal!

When she saw him, she gave a little cry and fainted. The other lady, who was Madame Derville, held her up. "Go away, sir!" she said angrily to Julien. "Don't come near her!"

Julien could not move. He stood there, white and trembling, until the two ladies had gone. All day long, he could think of nothing else. "She is still alive. She is in Besançon. She saw me, and she fainted. She still loves me!" But he did not try to see her again.

**The Offer**

Father Pirard's enemies were growing stronger. In the end, he was forced to resign from the seminary. But he had a powerful friend in Paris: the Marquis de La Mole, one of the richest noblemen in France. Father Pirard had helped him for many years in an important lawsuit against another powerful man. Now the Marquis offered him a position as priest of a parish near Paris.

Before he left, Father Pirard called Julien to his room.

"My son," he said, "I am leaving Besançon. And I am afraid that, without me, my enemies will destroy you. The Marquis de La Mole needs a secretary. I have recommended you. You will live in his house in Paris. He will pay you one hundred louis a year. Do you accept?"

Julien could not believe it. Paris! The great capital, the centre of the world! And a powerful marquis, a man of the highest nobility! It was more than he had ever hoped for.

"Yes, Father," he said. "With all my heart."

Father Pirard looked at him sadly. "Be careful in Paris, my son," he said. "It is a dangerous city for a young man like you. And remember: you have only your talent. The people you will meet will have everything else."
