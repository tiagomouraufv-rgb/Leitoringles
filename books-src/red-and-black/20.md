## Part 20 — The Last Days

After the trial, Julien was put in a cell for prisoners condemned to death. It was small and dark, and it smelled bad. Mathilde came every day. She wanted him to ask for an appeal — to ask a higher court to change the sentence. "There is still hope," she said. "My father has powerful friends. If you appeal, we can save you."

But Julien refused. "I am ready to die," he said. "Why should I live? To be a prisoner for the rest of my life? No. Let me die."

Mathilde cried and begged, but he did not change his mind.

**She Comes**

Then one day, the door of his cell opened, and Madame de Rênal came in.

She had escaped from her husband's house. She had come alone to Besançon. She ran to Julien and threw herself into his arms, crying.

"Julien! Julien!" she said. "Forgive me! Forgive me for that terrible letter! It was not me who wrote it. My confessor told me what to write. He said that it was my duty to God. I wrote it, and I have hated myself every day since then."

Julien held her in his arms. He kissed her hands and her face. He cried too. "It is you who must forgive me," he said. "I tried to kill you. I was mad."

"I forgave you the moment the bullet hit me," she said. "When I knew it was you, I was happy. It meant that you still loved me."

They sat together for a long time, holding each other's hands, like two children. They talked about Vergy, about the lime tree, about the butterflies, about the first evening when he held her hand. They laughed and cried at the same time.

"I never loved anyone but you," said Julien. "In Paris, I was mad with ambition. I wanted to be great. But I only had one real moment of happiness in my life, and that was with you."

She promised to come every day. And she did. She bribed the jailers to let her in. For a few weeks, Julien was happier than he had ever been in his life. He forgot about death. He forgot about Paris and Mathilde and ambition. He only thought about her.

"Before you came," he said to her, "I thought that I was strong and ready to die. Now I am happy, and that makes death harder. But I am not afraid."

He asked her to make him one promise. "Promise me that you will not kill yourself when I die. Promise me that you will live, and look after your children, and also after my child — the child that Mathilde is going to have. They will need you."

She promised.

**Visitors**

Madame de Rênal's husband found out where she was, and he ordered her to come home. She had to obey. But before she left, she held Julien in her arms for a long time.

Julien's father also came to see him. The old man did not come to comfort his son. He came to talk about money. Julien understood, and he gave him all the money he had. The old peasant was satisfied, and he left. "A father who loves money," thought Julien, "and a son who loved glory. We are not so different after all."

Mathilde came too, but Julien was tired of her. She was jealous of Madame de Rênal, and she made scenes. He was polite to her, but his heart was far away.

**The End**

On the morning of the execution, the sun was shining. It was a beautiful day. Julien walked out of the prison calmly, with his head high. He felt brave and strong. He thought of the walks in the woods at Vergy, of the beautiful mountains, of the fresh air. "Everything happened simply," Stendhal writes, "and without any affectation on his part." He did not make speeches. He did not show fear. He walked to the guillotine like a man walking in a garden.

And it was over.

**Afterwards**

Fouqué had bought Julien's body. That night, he sat alone in his room, beside it, crying. Then the door opened, and Mathilde came in. She was dressed in black, and her face was pale and wild.

"I want to see him," she said.

Fouqué could not refuse. She took Julien's head in her hands and kissed his forehead. She was thinking of Queen Marguerite, who had done the same thing for Boniface de La Mole, three hundred years before.

Julien had once said to Fouqué that he wanted to be buried in the little cave in the mountains above Verrières, where he had gone as a boy to be alone and to dream. Fouqué and Mathilde took his body there, at night, in a carriage. Many priests followed, singing. In the light of many candles, Mathilde, alone in her carriage, carried on her knees the head of the man she had loved so much. When they arrived, she buried it with her own hands. Then she had the cave decorated with Italian marble, at great expense.

Madame de Rênal kept her promise. She did not try to kill herself. But three days after Julien's death, she died, holding her children in her arms.
