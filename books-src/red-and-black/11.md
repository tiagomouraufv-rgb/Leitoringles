## Part 11 — Paris

When Julien arrived in Paris, the first thing he did was visit the places connected with Napoleon. He went to Malmaison, the house where the Emperor had lived with Josephine, and he cried there. Then he went to see Father Pirard, who took him to the house of the Marquis de La Mole.

The Hôtel de La Mole was a huge, magnificent building in the rich Faubourg Saint-Germain, where the oldest noble families of France lived. There was a big courtyard, a garden, and many beautiful rooms with gold, mirrors and silk. Julien had never seen anything like it. He felt very small.

"Be careful," whispered Father Pirard as they went up the great staircase. "Here, the slightest mistake will be noticed. They will judge you by your manners, not by your intelligence. Don't show your feelings. And don't be proud."

**The Marquis**

The Marquis de La Mole was a small, thin man with lively eyes and a grey wig. Julien had expected a proud, cold nobleman. Instead, he found a polite, elegant man who spoke to him kindly. The Marquis looked at him carefully and said a few words. Then he said to Father Pirard, in a low voice, "He seems intelligent. Let's see how he writes."

He gave Julien a letter to copy. When Julien finished, the Marquis looked at it and smiled. "You have written *cela* with two *l*'s," he said. "That is a mistake. Be careful with your spelling." Julien went red with shame. He would never forget that small mistake.

The Marquis gave him a room in the house, an office in the library, and a salary. He also gave him money to buy fine clothes, and he sent a tailor, a dancing master and a fencing master to teach him. "You must look like a gentleman," he said. "In Paris, appearance is everything."

Julien worked very hard. He spent all day in the library, writing letters, copying documents and organising the Marquis's papers. He was intelligent and careful, and the Marquis was soon very pleased with him. Within a few months, Julien was managing many of the Marquis's business affairs.

**The Family**

At dinner, Julien met the rest of the family.

The Marquise de La Mole was a tall, cold, proud woman. She only cared about noble birth. For her, a man who was not of noble family did not exist. She hardly looked at Julien.

Their son, Count Norbert, was a handsome, elegant young man, polite and kind but a little empty. He was always busy with his horses and his friends.

And there was their daughter, Mathilde. She was nineteen years old, tall and slim, with beautiful blonde hair and very bright blue eyes. She was very beautiful, but there was something cold and proud in her face. Julien did not like her at first. "She has the eyes of a cat," he thought. "She is too proud. She looks at me as if I were a piece of furniture."

**Mistakes**

In the first weeks, Julien made many mistakes. He did not know how to behave in a rich Paris house. Once, he rode a horse in the street and fell off in front of everybody. Count Norbert laughed, but kindly. "You ride like a priest," he said. Another time, Julien got into an argument with a man in a café, and challenged him to a duel. He fought and was slightly wounded. Later, he found out that the man was only a servant who had stolen his master's name.

Julien was very ashamed of these mistakes. But the Marquis was amused by them. He liked Julien's energy and his originality. "He is different from the young men of our class," he said to Father Pirard. "They are all the same. They have no fire. This young man is like a volcano."

The Marquis became fond of him. When he was ill with gout and had to stay in bed, he asked Julien to sit with him and talk. They talked about everything: history, politics, books, Napoleon. The Marquis was surprised by Julien's intelligence.

One day, the Marquis said: "My dear Sorel, I want to give you a present. Here is a blue coat. When you wear the blue coat, you will be my friend, and we will talk as equals. When you wear your black coat, you will be my secretary, and I will give you orders." Julien was deeply moved by this kindness.

**The Salon**

Every evening, many important people came to the Marquise's drawing room — dukes, generals, ambassadors, bishops and rich bankers. Julien sat in a corner and watched them. He was disappointed. They were rich and elegant, but they were terribly boring. They never talked about anything important. They were afraid of ideas, afraid of anything new, afraid of the future. They only talked about the weather, about horses, about who had married whom. Nobody said anything clever, because it was dangerous to be clever.

"So this is high society," Julien thought. "These are the masters of France. They are not better than the people of Verrières. They are only more polite."
