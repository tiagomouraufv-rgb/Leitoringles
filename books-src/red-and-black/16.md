## Part 16 — The Marquis's Anger

When the Marquis de La Mole read his daughter's letter, he was furious. He called Julien to his room.

"You monster!" he shouted. "You have destroyed my family! My daughter, the daughter of the Marquis de La Mole, with a peasant — a carpenter's son! And I trusted you! I treated you like a son!"

He walked up and down, shouting and swearing. Julien stood still and said nothing. At last he said quietly: "Sir, you are right. I deserve your anger. If you want to kill me, I am ready. I will leave a letter saying that I killed myself."

The Marquis looked at him. He saw that Julien was serious. His anger became less violent, but he was still in despair.

"What can I do?" he cried. "I wanted my daughter to be a duchess! I had chosen the Marquis de Croisenois for her. And now — now everything is lost!"

For many days, the Marquis did not know what to do. He thought of sending Julien to America. He thought of sending Mathilde to a convent. He thought of fighting a duel with Julien. But Mathilde was firm.

"Father," she said, "I love Julien. I will marry him, or I will marry nobody. If you send him away, I will follow him. If you try to stop me, everyone in Paris will know the truth, and our name will be laughed at."

**A New Name**

At last, the Marquis decided to make the best of the situation. If his daughter must marry a nobody, he would make that nobody into a somebody.

He gave Julien some land, and a large income. He arranged for him to receive a noble title and a new name: the Chevalier Julien Sorel de La Vernaye. He even let a rumour be spread that Julien was not really the son of the carpenter, but the secret son of a nobleman. And he made him a lieutenant in a regiment of hussars — the most elegant regiment in the army.

Julien could not believe his luck. He went to Strasbourg to join his regiment. He wore a splendid uniform, red and blue, with gold decorations. He had fine horses and servants. The other officers respected him. He was a soldier at last — a lieutenant at twenty-three, almost like Napoleon's officers!

"My novel is finished," he thought proudly, "and I owe it all to myself. I have made this proud young girl love me. Her father cannot live without her, and she cannot live without me." He began to dream of becoming a colonel, and then a general.

"After all," he said to himself, "am I really the son of that carpenter? Perhaps the rumour is true. Perhaps I am the son of some great nobleman." He became proud and cold with the other officers, like a real aristocrat.

**The Letter from Verrières**

But the Marquis was a careful man. Before he gave his final permission for the marriage, he wanted to know more about Julien's past. He wrote to Madame de Rênal in Verrières, where Julien had been a tutor, and asked her for information about the young man's character.

Some weeks later, her answer arrived. The Marquis read it and turned pale. He showed it to Mathilde.

The letter said:

"Sir, it is my religious duty to tell you the truth, painful as it is. This young man is poor and ambitious. To succeed, he uses hypocrisy and he seduces women. When he lived in my house, he tried to win my confidence and that of my family in order to advance himself. He seduced me. He has no religion and no principles. His only aim is to make himself master of the house and of the fortune of the people he lives with. He leaves misery and regret behind him wherever he goes."

Madame de Rênal had not written this letter freely. Her confessor, a strict and jealous priest, had dictated it to her. He had told her that it was her duty to God to warn the Marquis. She had suffered terribly as she wrote it, and she had cried over every word.

The Marquis wrote immediately to Julien in Strasbourg: "I refuse to allow the marriage. I will give you money, but you must leave France at once, and never see my daughter again."

Mathilde wrote too. She sent Julien a copy of Madame de Rênal's letter. "Everything is lost," she wrote. "Come at once."

When Julien read Madame de Rênal's letter, he went pale. He did not say a word. He left the regiment at once, took a fast carriage and travelled day and night — not to Paris, but to Verrières.
