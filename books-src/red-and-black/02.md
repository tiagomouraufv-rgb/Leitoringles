## Part 2 — Father and Son

Old Sorel was very surprised when the mayor offered to take his son Julien into his house. He could not understand why a rich man would want such a useless boy. But he was a clever peasant, and he did not show his surprise. He only said that he would think about it. As soon as the mayor had gone, he went to the sawmill to find his son.

The sawmill stood beside a small stream that came down from the mountains. The water turned a big wheel, and the wheel moved a great saw up and down, cutting the tree trunks into planks. Julien's two older brothers were there, cutting wood with their axes. They were huge, strong men, like giants.

But Julien was not working. Old Sorel looked everywhere for him. At last he saw him. He was sitting high up on one of the beams of the roof, reading a book, while he should have been watching the saw.

Nothing made old Sorel more angry than this. He could not read himself, and he hated books. He thought they were useless. He jumped up, and with one blow of his hand he knocked the book out of Julien's hands and into the stream. With a second blow on the head, he almost made Julien fall from the beam.

"Lazy dog!" he shouted. "Always reading your cursed books, when you should be watching the saw! Come down, you animal! I want to speak to you."

Julien came down. His eyes were full of tears — not because of the pain, but because he had lost his book. It was his favourite book in the world: the *Memorial of Saint Helena*, the story of Napoleon's last years, told in his own words.

**Julien**

Julien was a small, slim young man of eighteen or nineteen. He looked weak, but he was strong and could bear a lot of pain. His face was pale and delicate, and he had big, black eyes. When he was calm, those eyes were thoughtful and full of fire. When he was angry, they showed a terrible hatred. His hair was dark brown, and his forehead was low.

His family despised him. His father and brothers beat him often, because he was weak and did not like physical work, and because he always had his nose in a book. Everybody in the family thought he was useless. Julien hated them in return.

He had learned a lot from two people. The first was an old army surgeon who had served under Napoleon. The old man had taught him Latin and history, and had told him many stories about Napoleon's battles. When the surgeon died, he left Julien his medal of the Legion of Honour and his books.

The second was Father Chélan, who had taught him theology. Julien had learned the whole New Testament in Latin by heart. He could recite any page from beginning to end.

Julien had one great dream. He wanted to rise in the world, like Napoleon, who had been born poor and had become Emperor of France. In Napoleon's time, a poor, brave young man could become a general at thirty by fighting in the army. But Napoleon had gone. Now the priests were the most powerful men in France. They were the ones who became rich and important. A bishop could earn a hundred thousand francs a year — three times more than Napoleon's famous generals. So Julien had decided to become a priest. He did not really believe in God. He only wanted power and success.

He had shown his religious knowledge once in front of everyone. Then one day, at a dinner, he had praised Napoleon, and Father Chélan had been displeased. After that, Julien learned to hide his true thoughts. He kept his love of Napoleon a secret, and he always carried his arm in a sling for two months, as a punishment to himself for his mistake. He became a hypocrite. That is the only way, he thought, for a poor man to succeed.

**The Offer**

Old Sorel told Julien about the mayor's offer. "Answer me without lying, you book-reader," he said. "How do you know Madame de Rênal? When did you speak to her?"

"I have never spoken to her," said Julien. "I have only seen her in church."

"But you looked at her, you hypocrite!"

"Never! You know that in church I only see God," said Julien, with a holy look.

Old Sorel did not believe him. But he understood that this was a chance to make money. "Go and get ready," he said. "Tomorrow we'll go to see the mayor."

When Julien learned that he would live in the mayor's house, he was not happy at first. He was afraid of one thing: he would have to eat with the servants. "I would rather die," he said to himself. His pride could not bear it.

The next day, old Sorel went to see the mayor alone, and he bargained for a long time. He wanted more money. He wanted to know what his son would eat, and where he would sleep. In the end, the mayor agreed to pay four hundred francs a year, and Julien would eat with the family, not with the servants. He would also be given a new suit of black clothes.

Before he went to the mayor's house, Julien went to the church. He wanted to be alone, to think. The church was dark and empty, and the windows were covered with red curtains for a festival. The red light fell on the floor, and it looked like blood. On a bench, Julien found a piece of newspaper. He read: "Details of the execution and last moments of Louis Jenrel, executed at Besançon on..." The rest was torn. Julien noticed that Jenrel had the same letters as his own name, Sorel. He felt a strange chill. Then he saw the red light on the floor again. "It looks like blood," he thought. Then he laughed at himself, and he left the church.
