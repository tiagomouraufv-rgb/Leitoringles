## Part 1 — The Mayor of Verrières

The little town of Verrières is one of the prettiest in the region of Franche-Comté, in the east of France. Its white houses, with their pointed roofs of red tiles, lie along the side of a hill. Below the town, the river Doubs runs through a valley. Above it rise the high peaks of the Jura mountains, which are covered with snow from October until May.

When a traveller arrives in Verrières, the first thing he hears is a terrible noise. It comes from a big factory in the main street. Inside, enormous hammers rise and fall with a crash that shakes the ground, and each hammer makes thousands of nails every day. Pretty young girls from the town put small pieces of iron under the hammers, and the pieces come out as nails.

If the traveller asks, "Who owns this fine factory, which makes so much noise?", he is told at once, in a slow, proud voice: "Oh, it belongs to the mayor."

And if the traveller stops for a moment in the main street, he will probably see a tall man walking past with an important air. His hair is grey, and he is dressed in grey. He wears several medals on his coat. He has a high forehead and a nose like a hawk. At first sight, he looks like a gentleman, and even a pleasant one. But after a while, the traveller notices something unpleasant in his face: a look of self-satisfaction, of narrow pride, and of a man who only thinks about money. This man is the mayor, Monsieur de Rênal.

Monsieur de Rênal is very rich. His big house, with its gardens and its walls, is the finest in the town. In Verrières, the most important thing for a man's reputation is the size of his walls. A man who builds big stone walls around his property is respected by everyone. Monsieur de Rênal had built many. He had also bought, at a very high price, a piece of land from an old carpenter named Sorel, who owned a sawmill by the river. Old Sorel was a clever peasant, and he made the mayor pay six times more than the land was worth.

In Verrières, everybody thinks about one thing: making money. Every action is judged by one question: "Will it bring in money?" The people there are not bad, but they are jealous and narrow-minded, and they are always watching each other. In a small town like this, nobody can do anything without the whole town knowing about it. And there is nothing worse in Verrières than to be talked about.

**The Public Walk**

Above the river, there is a beautiful public walk, with a long terrace and big old plane trees. It is one of the most beautiful views in France. The mayor had made it larger, and it was called the Cours de la Fidélité — the Walk of Loyalty — to show that the town was loyal to the King. There is a stone on the wall with the mayor's name on it.

One day, the mayor ordered the town gardener to cut the branches of the old trees very short, to make them look neat and round. People who loved the trees were angry. One of them was the old priest of Verrières, Father Chélan.

**Father Chélan**

Father Chélan was eighty years old, but he was still strong and healthy, and he walked straight like a young man. He was honest and kind, and he had a strong sense of justice. He did not care about money or power.

Some time before, a gentleman from Paris named Monsieur Appert had come to Verrières. He was well known for his visits to prisons, hospitals and poor-houses, to see if the poor people inside were treated well. When he arrived, Father Chélan, who knew him, took him to visit the town prison and the poor-house. This made the important people of the town very angry, because they did not want a stranger from Paris to see what was happening there.

The director of the poor-house was Monsieur Valenod. He was a big, loud, red-faced man, with thick black whiskers. He was becoming richer and richer, and everybody whispered that he was stealing money that was meant for the poor. Valenod was furious about the visit, and so was the mayor. They threatened the old priest. "You could lose your position," they told him.

"I am eighty years old," said Father Chélan calmly. "I have lived here for fifty-six years. I baptized almost all of you. If they take my position away, I will live on my small income. But I will not stop doing what I think is right."

**A Tutor for the Children**

One fine autumn day, Monsieur de Rênal was walking on the public walk with his wife. She was a tall, beautiful woman of about thirty, with a simple, natural grace. She was not a woman who wanted to be admired. She knew nothing about the world, and she did not want to know. She was happy to spend her time with her three little boys.

As they walked, the mayor said, in an important voice: "I have decided to take young Sorel, the carpenter's son, into our house. He will be the tutor for our boys. He knows Latin very well. Father Chélan says that he is a very serious young man, and that he will become a priest. I will pay him three hundred francs a year, and food and lodging."

His wife was surprised. "Why?" she asked.

"Because of Valenod," said the mayor. "Valenod has just bought two beautiful horses from Normandy. He is getting too proud. But he has no tutor for his children. If I have a tutor for mine, people will see that I am more important than he is. That will put him in his place." Then he added: "And besides, I am sure Valenod wants the young man for himself. I must take him first."

Madame de Rênal said nothing. She was not happy with the idea. She imagined a dirty, rough young priest, badly dressed, who would shout at her children and perhaps even beat them. She loved her boys very much, and she was afraid for them.

But her husband had decided. He went the same afternoon to the sawmill to speak to old Sorel.
