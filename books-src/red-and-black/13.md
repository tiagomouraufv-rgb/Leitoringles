## Part 13 — A Letter and a Trap?

Julien noticed that Mathilde was changing towards him. She looked at him more often. She came to the library almost every day. She asked his opinion in front of the young noblemen, and she listened to his answers with great attention. The young men were jealous and angry.

At first, Julien did not understand. Then he began to think. "Is it possible that she loves me? No, impossible. She is a great lady, and I am a peasant." But slowly, the idea grew in his mind. And with it grew his pride. "If she loves me," he thought, "what a victory! The proud Mathilde de La Mole, in love with the carpenter's son! What a revenge on all these nobles who look down on me!"

He did not love her. He found her proud and cold. But he wanted to win her, as he had wanted to win Madame de Rênal at Vergy — as a battle, a conquest.

**The Letter**

One day, a servant brought Julien a letter. It was from Mathilde. He opened it with shaking hands. It was a declaration of love.

"I need to speak to you," she wrote. "I can no longer hide what I feel. I love you."

Julien could not believe it. He read the letter again and again. Then he felt a wild joy. "I have won! I, a poor peasant, have won the love of the daughter of a marquis!"

But then a terrible suspicion came into his mind. "What if it is a trap? What if Mathilde and her brother and their friends are playing a joke on me? What if they want me to make a fool of myself, so that they can laugh at me, or even kill me? These nobles are capable of anything."

He was so afraid of a trap that he made a copy of the letter and sent the original to his friend Fouqué, far away in the mountains, to keep it safe. "If anything happens to me," he wrote to Fouqué, "publish this letter."

Then he answered Mathilde, very carefully and coldly. He did not want to show his feelings.

**More Letters**

Mathilde wrote again, and again. Each letter was more passionate than the last. Julien answered each one with the same careful coldness. He was waiting to see what would happen.

Then one evening, Mathilde gave him a letter in the garden. It said:

"I need to talk to you. Tonight, at one o'clock in the morning, come to my room. Take the gardener's ladder and climb up to my window. The moon will not be up."

Julien was sure that it was a trap now. "Climb up to her window at night, with a ladder, in the moonlight — in front of all the servants? If they find me, they will kill me, and say that I was a thief. Or they will laugh at me for the rest of my life."

He thought about it all day. He decided not to go. Then he thought: "If I don't go, she will think I am a coward. And I will despise myself. I must go. If it is a trap, I will die bravely."

He wrote a letter to Fouqué, telling him everything. He loaded his pistols. Then he waited.

**One O'Clock**

At one o'clock, Julien took the long ladder from the garden and carried it to Mathilde's window. His heart was beating fast. He looked around carefully. Nobody was there. He climbed up the ladder, with a pistol in his pocket.

The window opened. Mathilde was there.

"So you have come," she said, in a low, trembling voice. "I was not sure you would come."

She was very pale. She was as frightened as he was. Julien climbed into the room. He looked around for hidden enemies. He looked under the bed and behind the curtains. There was nobody there. It was not a trap. Mathilde really loved him.

They did not know what to say. They were both shy and nervous. Mathilde talked very fast, and very formally. She tried to be cold and proud. She said that she had decided to give herself to him because it was her duty — because he was brave and worthy, and she had promised herself. She spoke as if she were reading from a book.

At last, she gave herself to him. But it was not like Madame de Rênal. There was no tenderness, no simple joy. Both of them were acting a part. Julien was proud of his victory. Mathilde was proud of her courage. But neither of them was truly happy.

Before dawn, Julien climbed down the ladder and returned to his room.
