## Part 5 — Love and Guilt

**Fouqué**

After the evening of the hand, Julien asked for a few days of holiday, and he went to visit his only friend, Fouqué. Fouqué was a timber merchant who lived alone in a small house high in the mountains. He was a simple, honest, cheerful man.

Julien climbed for hours through the forest. On the way, he found a small cave high up in the rocks. He sat there alone, looking down at the whole valley, and he felt completely free. He lit a fire and wrote down his thoughts, and then he burned the paper. Nobody must know his secret dreams.

At Fouqué's house, his friend made him an offer. "Julien," he said, "I have a good business, and I am making money. Come and work with me. You are clever, you can write and count. In a few years, you will earn four thousand francs a year. We will be partners."

It was a good offer, and Julien thought about it seriously. It would mean a quiet, safe life, without lies. But it would also mean giving up his great dreams. To be a timber merchant in the mountains, when he wanted to be Napoleon! He refused. Fouqué was disappointed. "You are a strange fellow," he said.

**Two O'Clock**

When Julien returned to Vergy, Madame de Rênal was so happy to see him that she could not hide it. Julien noticed. He thought: "This woman is in love with me. I must become her lover. It is my duty." Again, it was pride that pushed him, not love. He felt that he had to win her, as Napoleon had to win a battle. And he wanted to take revenge on her husband, and on all the rich people who looked down on him.

One evening, when they were alone, he whispered to her: "Madame, tonight at two o'clock I shall come to your room. I have something to tell you."

She was shocked and angry. "Never!" she said. "How dare you!"

Julien felt humiliated. But he had made a decision, and he would not change it. At two o'clock in the morning, he went along the dark corridor in his bare feet, trembling. His heart was beating so hard that he thought he would faint. He would rather have faced a firing squad. At the door of her room, he stopped. "If I don't go in now, I am a coward," he told himself. He opened the door.

Madame de Rênal jumped out of bed when she saw him. "Go away!" she cried. "You are terrible!"

Julien forgot all his plans, all his careful phrases. He fell on his knees beside her bed, took her hand, and began to cry. He cried like a child. And she, who loved him, could not resist his tears.

A few hours later, when Julien left her room, he was not as happy as he had expected. "Is this all?" he thought. "Is this what it is to be loved?" He was proud of his victory, but he had not felt the great passion he had read about in books. He had been too busy thinking about how to act, like a soldier following his orders.

**True Love**

But in the days that followed, everything changed. Madame de Rênal loved him with a pure, simple, total love. She did not think about her reputation, or about the danger. She only thought about him. When they were together, she was so happy that she seemed younger, almost like a girl.

Little by little, Julien forgot his pride and his calculations. He was young, and she was beautiful and kind, and she loved him. He began to love her too. In her arms, he forgot his hatred of the rich and his dreams of glory. He felt for the first time that someone cared for him, not for his Latin or his cleverness, but for himself.

She also taught him many things he did not know: how to behave in society, how to speak to important people, what to say and what not to say. Julien was a peasant's son, and he knew nothing about these things. She did it gently, without hurting his pride. She was ten years older than he was, but in some things she was like a child, and he was the one who knew more.

They spent the summer in happiness. When Monsieur de Rênal was away, they walked in the garden for hours. When he was there, they hid their feelings, and Julien was as cold and polite as before. Monsieur de Rênal noticed nothing. He only thought about his money and his rivals.

**The Child's Illness**

Then one day, the youngest boy, Stanislas, became very ill with a high fever. For several days, the doctors thought he would die.

Madame de Rênal was in despair. And a terrible idea came into her mind: God was punishing her for her sin. She was an adulteress, and now God was going to take her child from her. She sat by the boy's bed day and night, crying and praying. "Oh, God, take me, not him! Punish me, not my innocent child!"

She wanted to tell her husband everything — to confess her sin, so that God would forgive her and save her son. Julien was terrified. If she confessed, she would be ruined, and he would be ruined too.

"If you tell him," he said to her, "he will throw you out. You will lose your children, your home, your reputation. Everyone in Verrières will talk about you. And that will not save Stanislas."

"I don't care about myself," she said. "I only care about my son. I have sinned, and I must pay for it."

Julien saw how much she suffered. For the first time, he did not think about himself. He saw that her love was real — so real that she was ready to destroy herself for it. He felt deep pity and tenderness for her. "If I could," he thought, "I would die for her."

At last, after many terrible days, Stanislas got better. The fever went down, and he began to eat again. Madame de Rênal thanked God on her knees. She did not confess. But from that time, there was always a little shadow of guilt in her happiness. And Julien loved her more than before.
