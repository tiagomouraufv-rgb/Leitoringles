## Part 4 — A Hand in the Dark

In the spring, the family moved to their country house at Vergy, a village in the mountains, about two hours from Verrières. There was an old castle there, with a big garden and a forest of walnut trees, and a beautiful view over the valley of the Doubs.

Madame de Rênal was very happy at Vergy. She ran in the garden with her children and chased butterflies with them, using big nets of thin cloth. Julien, who was free from the eyes of the town, felt happy too. He went for long walks alone in the mountains, and he read his books about Napoleon. In the evenings, everyone sat in the garden under a big lime tree.

Madame de Rênal's cousin, Madame Derville, came to stay with them. She was a clever, sensible woman. She soon noticed that her cousin was different — happier, more lively and more beautiful than before. And she noticed the young tutor. She began to suspect what Madame de Rênal herself did not understand.

Madame de Rênal began to dress more carefully. She asked for new summer dresses to be made. She did not ask herself why.

**The Decision**

One evening, while they were sitting in the dark garden, Julien's hand touched Madame de Rênal's hand by chance. She quickly pulled her hand away.

Julien felt insulted. "She pulled her hand away from me because I am a poor peasant," he thought. And he decided that the next evening, he would take her hand, and she would leave it in his. It was not love that made him decide this. It was pride. He felt that it was his duty, like a soldier who must win a battle. If he failed, he would despise himself.

All the next day, he thought about it. He was so nervous that he could hardly speak to anyone. He watched the clock. He told himself: "At exactly ten o'clock tonight, I will do what I promised myself, or I will go up to my room and shoot myself."

In the evening, the three of them sat under the lime tree in the dark: Madame de Rênal, Madame Derville and Julien. The night was hot and heavy. A storm was coming. Julien was so nervous that his voice changed, and he could not talk normally. Madame de Rênal noticed and asked him if he was ill.

Every stroke of the church clock sounded in Julien's heart. Nine o'clock. A quarter past nine. Half past nine. A quarter to ten. Julien felt sick with fear. He despised himself for his cowardice.

At last, the clock began to strike ten. With each stroke, Julien felt as if a hammer was hitting his chest. When the last stroke sounded, he put out his hand and took Madame de Rênal's hand.

She tried to pull it away. But he held it firmly. Her hand was cold, and it trembled. Julien pressed it with a strength he did not know he had. She made one more effort to free it, and then she left her hand in his.

Julien's heart was full of joy — not because he loved Madame de Rênal, but because he had won. He had done his duty. He felt like a general after a victory. He began to talk easily and cheerfully, and he talked so well that Madame Derville was amazed.

Madame de Rênal said nothing. She was too moved to speak. She was afraid that if she spoke, Madame Derville would notice something. Her hand stayed in Julien's for the rest of the evening.

When they went to bed, Julien slept like a man after a battle. But Madame de Rênal did not sleep at all. For the first time in her life, she was in love. She was happy, and she was terrified. "Is this what love is?" she thought. "This strange happiness? It is a sin!" She was a good, religious woman. She thought of her husband and her children. She thought she was a terrible woman. And then she thought of Julien's eyes, and she was happy again. She spent the whole night in this storm of feelings.

The next morning, Julien felt only a little proud of his victory. He had almost forgotten it. He went for a walk in the mountains and read his book about Napoleon.

**The Portrait**

A few days later, Monsieur de Rênal decided to change the mattresses in all the beds in the house. He told the servants to bring all the straw mattresses down, and to put new ones in their place.

Julien was suddenly very pale. "Madame," he whispered to Madame de Rênal, "I have a secret. In my mattress, there is a small box. Inside it is a portrait. If your husband finds it, I am lost. Please, please, go to my room and take it out before anyone sees it. And promise me that you won't look at it."

Madame de Rênal ran upstairs and found the box. She was very pale too. "A portrait!" she thought. "It must be the portrait of a woman — a woman he loves!" She felt terrible jealousy. But she did not open the box. She gave it to Julien, who thanked her and ran into the garden. There, he burned the portrait.

It was not the portrait of a woman. It was a picture of Napoleon. If Monsieur de Rênal, who hated Napoleon and loved the King, had found it, he would have sent Julien away at once.

**On the Mountain**

Sometimes Julien climbed alone to the top of a high rock above the valley. From there, he could see for many miles. He watched a big hawk flying in great circles in the sky. It moved quietly and powerfully, without effort. Julien envied it. "That was Napoleon's destiny," he thought. "One day, will it be mine?"
